Chapter 27: The Civil Rights Movement

Section 27.3 only for Unit 7
1. How would you characterize the different forms that the Black Power movement took? How were these forms related to traditions in African American history?

Black Power took three main forms, all of them rejecting the idea that Black Americans had to wait patiently for white society to grant equality. Cultural Black Power celebrated pride and identity — “Black is Beautiful,” natural hair, and Black studies programs. Political Black Power, especially the Black Panther Party (founded 1966), armed itself against police brutality while also running free breakfasts and clinics. Religious and separatist Black Power, like the Nation of Islam and Malcolm X, pushed Black people to build their own institutions instead of assimilating. None of this was new — it connected to older traditions like Marcus Garvey’s 1920s nationalism, Reconstruction-era armed self-defense, and independent Black churches going back before the Civil War. Black Power was the latest round of a long debate: fight to be included in white society, or build a separate Black world.

2. What did the Chicano and American Indian movements have in common with the Black freedom movement?

Both movements copied the Black freedom struggle’s tactics (protests, marches, boycotts) and its core demand: cultural pride and political power. César Chávez and Dolores Huerta’s United Farm Workers (UFW) organized Mexican American farmworkers through strikes and the famous grape boycott, while younger activists formed groups like the Brown Berets to push for bilingual education and Chicano studies. The American Indian Movement (AIM), founded in 1968, occupied Alcatraz Island in 1969 and took over Wounded Knee in 1973 to grab national attention, just like civil rights sit-ins had. All three movements refused to quietly assimilate, demanded self-determination, and used grassroots protest instead of waiting for politicians.

27.3 — Key Terms

American Indian Movement (AIM)
Native American civil rights organization founded in 1968 that used direct action — including the occupation of Alcatraz and the 1973 takeover of Wounded Knee — to demand treaty rights and tribal sovereignty.
Black nationalism
Belief that Black Americans should build independent political, economic, and cultural institutions rather than assimilate into white society; rooted in traditions going back to Marcus Garvey.
Black Panther Party
Founded in Oakland in 1966 by Huey Newton and Bobby Seale; organized armed self-defense against police brutality and ran community programs like free breakfasts and health clinics.
Black Power
Slogan popularized by Stokely Carmichael in 1966; emphasized racial pride, self-determination, economic independence, and a rejection of white moderation and gradualism.
Kerner Commission (1968)
Johnson-appointed commission that investigated the urban riots and concluded that the nation was moving toward two societies, one black and one white, separate and unequal, blaming white racism for inner-city conditions.
Occupation of Alcatraz (1969)
Native American activists calling themselves “Indians of All Tribes” occupied the former federal prison island for 19 months to demand the return of unused federal land and raise awareness of Native rights.
Riots of 1967
“Long hot summer” uprisings in cities like Newark and Detroit, sparked by police incidents but fueled by poverty, unemployment, housing discrimination, and brutality in Black urban neighborhoods.
United Farm Workers (UFW)
Farm labor union led by César Chávez and Dolores Huerta that organized primarily Mexican American and Filipino farmworkers through strikes, marches, and the grape boycott.
Young Lords
Puerto Rican activist group modeled on the Black Panthers that organized in New York and Chicago to demand better housing, health care, and education in Latino neighborhoods.

Chapter 28: Liberal Crisis & Conservative Rebirth

APUSH Era 8 (1945–1980)
Identify the Big IdeaWhat were liberalism’s social and political achievements in the 1960s, and how did debates over liberal values contribute to conflict at home and reflect tension abroad?
1. What new roles did the federal government assume under Great Society initiatives, and how did they extend the New Deal tradition?

Johnson’s Great Society made the federal government bigger and more involved in daily life than ever before. The War on Poverty created programs like Head Start, Job Corps, and VISTA to break the cycle of poverty through education and community action rather than just cash. Medicare gave health insurance to seniors, and Medicaid covered low-income Americans — the first major federal health programs in U.S. history. Other laws funded public schools and college loans, created HUD, and the Immigration Act of 1965 scrapped the old quota system, opening immigration to the world. This all built on the New Deal’s core idea that the government is responsible for ordinary Americans’ welfare. But where the New Deal fixed the economy, the Great Society aimed at social justice, racial equality, and quality of life.

2. What factors accounted for the resurgence of feminism in the 1960s?

The biggest spark was Betty Friedan’s The Feminine Mystique (1963), which named “the problem that has no name” and made millions of housewives realize their frustration was widespread, not personal. The civil rights movement also inspired women activists — and angered them, since many faced sexism from the movement’s male leaders. Bigger shifts mattered too: more women were going to college, marrying later, and joining the workforce, which made unfair pay and hiring laws feel more obvious. The birth control pill (1960) gave women real control over their lives and made career planning possible. Finally, a 1963 federal report from Kennedy’s Commission on the Status of Women documented widespread workplace discrimination, setting up the legal battles of the decade.

3. What legal victories did women achieve in the 1960s?

Women won several major legal wins. The Equal Pay Act of 1963 required equal pay for the same work — the first federal law against wage discrimination based on sex. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 (Title VII) banned job discrimination based on sex, race, religion, and national origin; the word “sex” was actually added by a segregationist trying to sink the bill, but it passed anyway and became a huge tool for feminists. In 1965, Griswold v. Connecticut established a right to privacy in birth control, setting up Roe v. Wade later. Frustrated with weak enforcement, Betty Friedan co-founded the National Organization for Women (NOW) in 1966 to push for real change. By decade’s end, women had a real legal framework to fight discrimination — though full equality was still a long way off.

28.1 — Key Terms

Great Society
LBJ’s sweeping domestic program that expanded the New Deal through the War on Poverty, Medicare, Medicaid, federal aid to education, civil rights enforcement, and immigration reform.
Immigration Act of 1965
Also called the Hart-Celler Act; abolished the national-origins quota system of the 1920s, opening immigration to Asia, Latin America, and Africa and transforming U.S. demographics.
The Feminine Mystique (1963)
Betty Friedan’s bestseller describing the unhappiness of suburban housewives — “the problem that has no name” — that helped launch second-wave feminism.
War on Poverty
LBJ’s set of Great Society anti-poverty programs — including the Office of Economic Opportunity, Head Start, Job Corps, VISTA, and community action — that attacked poverty’s structural causes.
1. How did American military engagement in Vietnam evolve under Johnson?

When Johnson took office in November 1963, there were only about 16,000 U.S. advisors in South Vietnam. Within five years, that number grew to over 500,000 combat troops. The turning point was August 1964, when Johnson claimed (misleadingly) that North Vietnamese ships had attacked U.S. destroyers in the Gulf of Tonkin. Congress responded with the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, which gave Johnson sweeping power to use force without a formal war declaration. In 1965 he launched Operation Rolling Thunder, a long bombing campaign, and sent Marines to Da Nang. By 1968, the U.S. was fighting both a conventional war and a guerrilla war, using weapons like napalm and Agent Orange. The breaking point was the Tet Offensive in January 1968 — a coordinated Viet Cong attack on dozens of cities. Technically a U.S. victory, it was a political disaster, because Americans saw on TV that the enemy could strike anywhere. Johnson’s approval collapsed and he announced in March 1968 that he wouldn’t seek reelection.

2. How did the war in Vietnam affect the United States domestically in the 1960s?

The war reshaped America at home. It drained money Johnson wanted for the Great Society, forcing him to underfund both war and social programs. Inflation rose, and trust in the government collapsed as officials kept lying about how the war was going. This was the first war on TV, and footage of combat and civilian casualties eroded public support. A huge antiwar movement grew on college campuses, where students burned draft cards and held “teach-ins.” The draft itself was unfair — working-class and minority men got drafted while middle-class white college students got deferments — which fueled anger about class inequality. MLK and other civil rights leaders came out against the war, pointing out that America preached freedom abroad while practicing racism at home. By 1968, the combination of war, urban riots, the assassinations of King and RFK, and the violent Democratic Convention in Chicago made the country feel like it was falling apart.

3. What were the factors behind the student protest movements of the 1960s?

Postwar prosperity and the GI Bill expanded higher education, so by the mid-1960s there were more college students than ever, packed onto huge campuses. Many were inspired by the civil rights movement, adopting its nonviolent protest tactics for their own causes. The draft and the Vietnam War made activism personal — any college-age guy could be sent to fight and die. On the left, Students for a Democratic Society (SDS), founded in 1960, called for “participatory democracy” in its 1962 Port Huron Statement and led antiwar and free speech protests. On the right, Young Americans for Freedom (YAF), also founded in 1960, pushed a conservative agenda of free markets, anti-communism, and traditional values — training the generation that would elect Goldwater, Nixon, and Reagan. The student movement wasn’t just a liberal phenomenon; it was a generational mobilization on both sides.

28.2 — Key Terms

Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (1964)
Congressional resolution, passed after reports of attacks on U.S. destroyers off North Vietnam, that gave Johnson broad authority to use military force in Southeast Asia without a declaration of war.
Students for a Democratic Society (SDS)
Leftist student organization founded in 1960; its 1962 Port Huron Statement called for “participatory democracy,” and SDS became central to antiwar and free speech protests.
Young Americans for Freedom (YAF)
Conservative student organization founded in 1960 at William F. Buckley’s estate; articulated a vision of free markets, anti-communism, and traditional values that shaped the modern conservative movement.
1. Why might a Democratic supporter of FDR in the 1940s have decided to vote for Republican Richard Nixon in 1968?

By 1968 the old FDR Democratic coalition (Northern workers, Southern segregationists, urban immigrants, Black Americans) was falling apart. The Civil Rights Act (1964) and Voting Rights Act (1965) pushed away white Southerners who had been Democrats since Reconstruction. Urban riots from 1965–68 scared city and suburban voters, and rising crime made “law and order” a top concern while Democrats seemed soft on it. The Great Society’s costly new programs felt to some white voters like their party had abandoned them to help minorities. The antiwar movement, counterculture, and campus radicals made older voters feel the country was being wrecked. Nixon exploited all of this, promising “law and order,” appealing to the silent majority, and using the Southern Strategy — coded racial messages about states’ rights and busing — to pull white Southerners into the GOP. For many 1940s FDR Democrats, voting Nixon in 1968 felt like voting to restore the country they remembered.

2. In what ways did 1968 represent a turning point in postwar history?

1968 was probably the most turbulent year in postwar American history. It started with the Tet Offensive, which destroyed the credibility of Johnson’s Vietnam policy and led him to drop out of the race on March 31. On April 4, MLK was assassinated, setting off riots in more than 100 cities. On June 5, RFK was assassinated on the night he won California, closing off the last hope of uniting the antiwar left with working-class voters. In August, the Democratic Convention in Chicago dissolved into violent clashes between protesters and police, all on live TV. Nixon won a narrow three-way race in November (with segregationist George Wallace taking millions of votes), while the Soviets invaded Czechoslovakia. Together, 1968 shattered faith that liberalism could solve America’s problems, opened the door to conservatism, and set the political fault lines — race, war, culture, law and order — that would dominate the next several decades.

3. How did women’s liberation after 1968 differ from the women’s movement of the early 1960s?

The early-1960s women’s movement (led by groups like NOW) focused on legal and economic equality — equal pay, equal hiring, and eventually an Equal Rights Amendment. It was run mostly by middle-class professionals and aimed at integrating women into existing institutions on equal terms with men. The women’s liberation movement after 1968 was younger, angrier, and more grassroots. Many of its leaders had come from the civil rights and antiwar movements and were furious about sexism they faced there. Instead of just asking to be let in, they challenged the whole system — marriage, family roles, sexuality, beauty standards, and the media. Consciousness-raising groups in living rooms coined the slogan “the personal is political.” Activists protested the Miss America pageant, opened women’s health clinics, and fought for abortion rights. Where early feminism sought reform, women’s liberation sought to transform the culture itself.

4. What gains did women achieve in the late 1960s/early 1970s?

Women made enormous gains. Title IX (1972) banned sex discrimination at any federally funded school, transforming access to college, professional programs, and especially sports. Roe v. Wade (1973) established a constitutional right to abortion. The Equal Rights Amendment passed Congress in 1972 and was almost ratified, but ended three states short. Other wins: military academies opened to women, the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (1974) gave them equal access to loans, marriage and divorce laws were reformed (including no-fault divorce), and sexual harassment and marital rape were finally recognized as legal issues. Women flooded into higher education and the professions, and it became normal to plan careers and delay marriage. A conservative backlash was already gathering, but this was arguably the biggest leap forward for American women since suffrage.

28.3 — Key Terms

Nixon’s Southern Strategy
Republican electoral strategy of appealing to white Southern voters through coded racial messaging — “law and order,” states’ rights, opposition to busing — to pull the once-solid Democratic South into the GOP coalition.
Stonewall Riots (1969)
Uprising by LGBTQ patrons against a police raid at the Stonewall Inn in Greenwich Village; widely regarded as the birth of the modern gay rights movement.
Title IX (1972)
Federal law banning sex discrimination in any education program receiving federal funds; dramatically expanded women’s access to schools, professional programs, and athletics.
Women’s Liberation Movement
Younger, more radical wing of second-wave feminism that emerged after 1968; challenged patriarchy in family, sexuality, and culture through consciousness-raising and grassroots activism.
1. How did the Vietnam War end? What was its legacy for the United States?

Nixon’s policy, Vietnamization, slowly turned combat over to South Vietnam while keeping U.S. air support. He also secretly bombed Cambodia in 1970 to cut supply lines, which triggered massive protests — at Kent State in May 1970, National Guardsmen killed four student protesters. After years of talks, Kissinger signed the Paris Peace Accords in January 1973, ending U.S. involvement. But fighting between North and South continued, and in April 1975 North Vietnam took Saigon. The last Americans fled by helicopter from the embassy roof — an image of defeat. Over 58,000 Americans and 2–3 million Vietnamese died. The war produced the Vietnam syndrome (a deep reluctance to get involved in foreign wars), the War Powers Act (1973) limiting presidential war powers, and the end of the draft. Trust in government was shattered, the military took decades to recover, and veterans came home to a divided country, often without adequate care for Agent Orange exposure or PTSD.

2. What was the legacy of the Watergate scandal?

Watergate started in June 1972 when five burglars linked to Nixon’s reelection committee were caught breaking into Democratic headquarters. Washington Post reporters Woodward and Bernstein, Senate hearings, and a special prosecutor slowly revealed a much bigger pattern of political sabotage, illegal surveillance, and cover-up. When a secret Oval Office taping system was exposed, the Supreme Court (U.S. v. Nixon, 1974) ordered the tapes released, and they proved Nixon orchestrated the cover-up. Facing impeachment, Nixon resigned on August 9, 1974 — the only president ever to do so. Ford’s controversial pardon quickly followed. Watergate deepened Americans’ distrust of government (already shaken by Vietnam), led to reforms like campaign finance laws and the Independent Counsel, and established that no president is above the law. Long-term, it cemented the cynical style of American politics we still live with today.

28.4 — Key Terms

Silent Majority
Phrase Nixon used to describe ordinary, patriotic, middle-class Americans who he claimed supported the Vietnam War and rejected the counterculture and antiwar protests; central to his 1968 and 1972 coalitions.
Watergate Scandal (1972–73)
Political scandal that began with a break-in at the Democratic National Committee headquarters; widening revelations of abuses of power and a cover-up forced Nixon to resign in August 1974.

Chapter 29: The Search for Order in an Era of Limits

APUSH Era 8/9 (1970s)
Identify the Big IdeaHow did the legacy of social changes — such as shifting gender roles, civil rights, and challenges to the family — in the 1960s continue to reverberate in the 1970s, leading to both new opportunities and political disagreement?
1. What major factors led to the birth of the environmental movement in the 1970s? What was the movement able to achieve?

The biggest spark was Rachel Carson’s Silent Spring (1962), which exposed how pesticides like DDT were poisoning ecosystems and threatening human health. A string of visible disasters deepened the alarm: the 1969 Santa Barbara oil spill, the Cuyahoga River catching fire, and thick smog over major cities. The first Earth Day in April 1970 drew an estimated 20 million people. Mostly under Nixon, Congress passed a huge wave of environmental laws: the Clean Air Act (1970), the creation of the EPA, the Clean Water Act (1972), the Endangered Species Act (1973), and a ban on DDT in 1972. After the Three Mile Island nuclear accident in 1979, nuclear power basically stopped growing in the U.S. for decades. The movement didn’t fix everything, but it permanently embedded environmental protection in federal law.

2. Why did the US enter an energy crisis in the 1970s?

By the 1970s, the U.S. was importing lots of its oil from the Middle East, making it vulnerable to unstable producers. In October 1973, during the Yom Kippur War, the Arab-led OPEC imposed an oil embargo on the U.S. Oil prices quadrupled, gas stations ran dry, long lines formed, and odd-even rationing was imposed. A second shock hit in 1979 after the Iranian Revolution cut off Iran’s exports. At home, U.S. oil production had peaked in 1970, so the country could no longer supply itself. American life was built around cheap gas and big cars, and when prices exploded the economy broke — high inflation plus slow growth, a strange mix called stagflation that defied normal economic tools. Nixon, Ford, and Carter all tried price controls, conservation, and a new Department of Energy, but the crisis shattered the postwar sense of unlimited American abundance.

3. What major developments shaped the American economy in the 1970s and contributed to its transformation?

Several big changes reshaped the economy. First, deindustrialization: factories in the Northeast and Midwest (steel, autos, textiles) collapsed under foreign competition from Germany and Japan, creating the Rust Belt in cities like Detroit, Pittsburgh, and Cleveland. Meanwhile, the Sun Belt grew with new industries like defense, oil, tourism, and (eventually) computing. Second, stagflation broke the government’s old economic tools and shook faith in the New Deal’s Keynesian approach. Third, a tax revolt took off, capped by California’s Proposition 13 (1978), which showed Americans had turned against big government. Fourth, more women joined the workforce, reshaping family life. Fifth, globalization accelerated, putting American workers under intense international competition. Together these changes eroded faith in liberal economics and set up Reagan’s conservative revolution.

29.1 — Key Terms

Deindustrialization
Decline of manufacturing in the 1970s as factories closed in the Northeast and Midwest due to foreign competition, aging plants, rising costs, and corporate disinvestment.
Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)
Federal agency created by Nixon in 1970 to enforce new environmental laws like the Clean Air and Clean Water Acts and regulate pollution.
OPEC
Organization of Petroleum Exporting Countries; dominated by Arab states, its 1973 oil embargo caused a fourfold jump in oil prices and triggered the U.S. energy crisis.
California’s Proposition 13 (1978)
Ballot initiative that capped California property taxes; launched the nationwide tax revolt and helped build the political base for the conservative Reagan revolution.
Rachel Carson’s Silent Spring (1962)
Bestselling book that exposed the ecological damage caused by pesticides like DDT; widely credited with launching the modern environmental movement.
Rust Belt
Region of the Northeast and Midwest hit hardest by deindustrialization; cities like Detroit, Pittsburgh, and Cleveland lost huge shares of their manufacturing jobs and population.
Three Mile Island
1979 partial nuclear meltdown at a Pennsylvania power plant; sharply turned public opinion against nuclear power and stalled construction of new plants for decades.
1. How did affirmative action attempt to address the legacy of discrimination, and why did it become so controversial?

Affirmative action was a set of policies meant to open up opportunities for groups — especially Black Americans and women — shut out of schools, jobs, and government contracts. It started with a 1961 Kennedy executive order and expanded under LBJ in 1965. Universities, companies, and agencies began considering race and gender in hiring and admissions, sometimes with quotas. The logic: after centuries of slavery and discrimination, just saying “everyone’s equal now” wasn’t enough. Critics called it “reverse discrimination” — unfair to white men who had no personal role in historical racism. Allan Bakke, a white applicant rejected twice by UC Davis Medical School (which reserved 16 of 100 spots for minorities), sued. In Bakke v. University of California (1978), the Supreme Court split the difference: racial quotas were unconstitutional, but race could still be considered as one factor. The ruling kept affirmative action legal but made it a permanent political battleground.

2. Why did the Equal Rights Amendment initially gain momentum, and why did it ultimately fail?

The Equal Rights Amendment (ERA), first proposed in 1923, was a constitutional amendment banning sex discrimination. Congress finally passed it in 1972, and it looked like a lock — within a year, 30 of the 38 states needed had ratified it, and polls showed most Americans in favor. Then came a backlash led by Phyllis Schlafly, whose “STOP ERA” campaign organized traditionalist women around the claim that the ERA would end laws protecting women, subject them to the draft, legalize unisex bathrooms, and destroy the family. She tied the ERA to every cultural fear of the 1970s — feminism, abortion, gay rights. By 1977 ratification stalled three states short, and even after Congress extended the deadline to 1982, no more states ratified (five actually rescinded). The ERA’s defeat was the first major win for the Religious Right and proved that grassroots conservatism was a serious political force.

29.2 — Key Terms

Affirmative Action
Policies that considered race and gender in hiring, admissions, and contracting to remedy historical discrimination; began with executive orders under Kennedy and Johnson.
Bakke v. University of California (1978)
Supreme Court ruling that struck down rigid racial quotas in university admissions as unconstitutional but allowed race to be considered as one factor among many.
Equal Rights Amendment
Proposed constitutional amendment banning sex-based discrimination; passed Congress in 1972 but fell three states short of ratification after a backlash led by Phyllis Schlafly.
Roe v. Wade (1973)
Supreme Court decision establishing a constitutional right to abortion based on the right to privacy; immediately became one of the most divisive rulings in U.S. history.
1. How did deindustrialization reshape work, income, and daily life for American families after 1970?

Deindustrialization ended the postwar era of rising wages and secure factory jobs that let a single male breadwinner support a middle-class family. As Rust Belt steel mills, auto plants, and textile factories closed, millions of well-paid union jobs disappeared, replaced by lower-paid service jobs with no benefits or security. Wages for men stagnated in the mid-1970s and stayed flat for decades. Families adapted in two ways: married women entered the workforce in record numbers (most married moms were working outside the home by the late 1970s), and Americans took on far more consumer debt, including credit cards. Life felt more unstable — divorce rates rose, young people delayed marriage, and many working-class communities, especially white ones, felt like they were falling behind. This combination of economic anxiety and cultural strain set up the political turn to conservatism.

Chapter 30: Conservative America Ascendant (1973–1991)

APUSH Era 9 (1980–present)
Identify the Big IdeaWhat factors made the rise of the New Right possible, and what ideas about freedom and citizenship did conservatives articulate in the 1980s?
1. Which were the key groups of the new Republican coalition? Were their goals complementary or contradictory?

Reagan’s 1980 coalition brought together several groups. Economic conservatives and libertarians wanted lower taxes, deregulation, and free markets. Cold War hawks wanted a huge military buildup to confront the Soviets. Social conservatives — especially the Religious Right led by Jerry Falwell’s Moral Majority (1979) — opposed abortion, the ERA, and gay rights. Neoconservatives, former liberals disillusioned with the Great Society and antiwar movement, brought intellectual firepower to anti-communism. Finally, white Southerners and working-class “Reagan Democrats” crossed over, alienated from their own party. The groups often contradicted each other — libertarians wanted government out of both the marketplace and the bedroom, but religious conservatives wanted it to enforce morality; economic conservatives wanted to cut spending, while defense hawks wanted to massively increase it. What held them together was shared opposition to the liberal establishment, the Soviet Union, and 1960s-70s chaos. Reagan papered over the contradictions with a unifying vision of American renewal.

2. In what ways was the New Right “reactive,” responding to liberalism, and in what ways was it “proactive,” asserting its own agenda?

The New Right was reactive in that much of its energy came from opposing liberal policies: Great Society welfare, high taxes, affirmative action, busing, the counterculture, the antiwar movement, changing gender roles, Supreme Court rulings on school prayer and abortion, and détente with the Soviets. Groups like STOP ERA and antiabortion activists were mainly about saying no. But it was also proactive, with its own positive vision. Think tanks like the Heritage Foundation (1973) built a conservative intellectual base. Activists pioneered new political tools — direct mail fundraising (Richard Viguerie), talk radio, Christian TV, PACs, and evangelical get-out-the-vote drives. Conservatives pushed a positive agenda: supply-side economics, personal responsibility, traditional family values, a strong military, and freedom from big government — ideas Barry Goldwater had laid out in The Conscience of a Conservative (1960). By the late 1970s, conservatism wasn’t just complaining about liberalism — it was a full alternative worldview.

30.1 — Key Terms

Iran Hostage Crisis (1979–1980)
Iranian revolutionaries seized the U.S. embassy in Tehran and held 52 American hostages for 444 days; the crisis crippled Carter’s presidency and helped elect Reagan.
Religious Right
Politically mobilized evangelical Protestants and conservative Catholics, led by organizations like Jerry Falwell’s Moral Majority, who opposed abortion, the ERA, and gay rights and became a pillar of the Reagan coalition.
The Conscience of a Conservative (1960)
Barry Goldwater’s book laying out a conservative vision of limited government, free markets, anti-communism, and traditional values; a foundational text for the modern conservative movement.
1. Why are the 1980s seen as the beginning of a conservative age?

Reagan won 44 states in 1980 and 49 in 1984 — one of the biggest landslides in U.S. history — and his VP George H. W. Bush won a third Republican term in 1988. Reagan’s core ideas (lower taxes, deregulation, free markets, distrust of big government) became the mainstream for decades, even among Democrats; Bill Clinton later said “the era of big government is over.” Beyond elections, Reagan reshaped the judiciary with Supreme Court picks like Sandra Day O’Connor, Antonin Scalia, and Anthony Kennedy plus more than 350 lower federal judges. He weakened labor unions by firing striking air traffic controllers in 1981, slashed top tax rates, and deregulated businesses. Culturally, patriotism and traditional values surged. Combined with the Soviet Union’s collapse by 1991, the decade felt like a vindication of conservative ideas.

2. What were the key elements of Reagan’s domestic policy? How did that policy reflect conservative ideology?

Reagan’s domestic policy had four pillars: tax cuts, deregulation, cuts to domestic spending, and more military spending. The Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 cut income tax rates by 25% across the board and dropped the top rate from 70% to 50% (later 28% in 1986). Federal regulations on banking, airlines, trucking, and the environment were rolled back. Reagan cut spending on food stamps, welfare, Medicaid, and college aid, though he left Social Security and Medicare alone (too popular). Defense spending exploded to around $300 billion a year. These policies matched core conservative beliefs — Reagan’s famous line, “government is not the solution; government IS the problem.” Conservatives believed lower taxes and fewer regulations would unleash growth, that welfare created dependency, and that the free market was smarter than bureaucrats. These policies were the biggest break from New Deal/Great Society liberalism since FDR.

3. To what extent did Reaganomics achieve its goals?

Reaganomics had mixed results. Some wins: inflation dropped sharply from double digits (mostly thanks to Fed chair Paul Volcker); after a 1981–82 recession, the economy grew steadily through 1990 and created millions of jobs; tax rates fell sharply; and the stock market soared. But it also failed at big things. Tax cuts were supposed to pay for themselves through growth — they didn’t. The national debt nearly tripled from $900 billion in 1980 to $2.7 trillion in 1989, and the U.S. went from the world’s biggest lender to its biggest debtor. Income inequality widened sharply: the rich got much richer while working-class wages stagnated. Poverty rose during the recession and never fully recovered. Deregulation caused the savings-and-loan crisis, costing taxpayers hundreds of billions. Supply-side economics became a Republican article of faith, but the evidence for its core claim was always thin.

30.2 — Key Terms

AIDS Crisis (1980s)
Emergence of the HIV/AIDS epidemic, which disproportionately killed gay men, hemophiliacs, and drug users; the Reagan administration was criticized for its slow response, and activist groups like ACT UP demanded research and treatment.
“Reagan Democrats”
White, often working-class, traditionally Democratic voters (many from the Rust Belt and South) who crossed over to vote for Reagan in 1980 and 1984 because of concerns about taxes, culture, and national strength.
Supply-Side Economics (“Reaganomics”)
Economic theory that deep cuts in income and business taxes would stimulate investment, growth, and tax revenue; became the framework of Reagan’s domestic economic policy.
1. How did Reagan’s approach to the Soviet Union change between 1981 and 1989?

Reagan’s Soviet policy changed dramatically between his two terms. In his first term he was aggressively confrontational — calling the USSR the “evil empire” in 1983, launching a huge military buildup, and proposing the SDI (“Star Wars”) missile defense system. He also backed anti-communist rebels worldwide under the Reagan Doctrine, pushing tensions to Cuban Missile Crisis levels. In his second term he flipped toward diplomacy, driven by the rise of Mikhail Gorbachev as Soviet leader in 1985. Gorbachev’s reforms — perestroika (economic restructuring) and glasnost (openness) — opened real negotiation. The two leaders met at four summits and in 1987 signed the INF Treaty, eliminating an entire class of nuclear missiles in Europe. That same year, Reagan famously said, “Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall!” By the time Reagan left office in 1989, U.S.-Soviet relations had gone from near-war to real partnership, and the Berlin Wall fell later that year.

2. What factors led to the end of the Cold War?

The biggest cause was the internal collapse of the Soviet system. The Soviet economy had basically stopped growing by the 1970s and fell way behind the West. A costly war in Afghanistan (1979–89) drained Soviet resources, and corruption and cynicism sapped the regime’s legitimacy. Gorbachev’s reforms, meant to save communism, actually loosened the government’s grip and unleashed forces that toppled it. American policy helped: Reagan’s military buildup and SDI put unbearable pressure on an already stretched Soviet economy, and his second-term partnership with Gorbachev helped Gorbachev take bold risks. In Eastern Europe, people in Poland (Solidarity), Hungary, Czechoslovakia, and East Germany pushed back once they realized the Soviets wouldn’t send tanks this time. In 1989 the Berlin Wall fell; in 1990 Germany reunified; in December 1991 the Soviet Union itself dissolved — a combination of Soviet decay, American pressure, and people power.

3. What was Reagan’s legacy?

Reagan’s legacy is huge and still argued about. Admirers credit him with restoring American confidence after the 1970s, winning the Cold War without a shot, rebuilding the military, sparking a long economic boom, lowering top tax rates, and turning conservatism into the dominant political force for decades. He reshaped the Republican Party, stacked the federal courts, and set the terms of political debate for a generation. Critics point to big failures: the national debt tripled, the U.S. went from the world’s biggest lender to its biggest borrower, and income inequality widened sharply. Poverty and homelessness grew. The administration barely responded to the AIDS crisis, costing thousands of lives. Deregulation caused the savings-and-loan collapse. The Iran-Contra affair — secretly selling weapons to Iran and funneling profits to Nicaraguan rebels — nearly got Reagan impeached. Critics also argue his “welfare queens” and “states’ rights” rhetoric had racial dog whistles. His legacy is both a conservative revolution and a set of economic and social fault lines the country is still dealing with.

4. Why did the US intervene in conflicts between Iraq and Iran, and between Iraq and Kuwait? What were the American goals in each case?

Both interventions were about protecting U.S. interests in the Persian Gulf — especially oil and regional stability. In the Iran-Iraq War (1980–88), the U.S. sided mostly with Saddam Hussein’s Iraq against revolutionary Iran (which had taken 52 American hostages in 1979). The U.S. gave Iraq intelligence, loans, and technology, and escorted Kuwaiti oil tankers against Iranian attacks. The goal wasn’t for Iraq to win but to stop Iran from dominating the region. At the same time, the secret Iran-Contra affair showed the administration was also selling weapons to Iran — a contradictory policy. When Saddam invaded Kuwait in August 1990, Bush assembled a huge UN coalition. The Persian Gulf War began in 1991 with a massive air campaign and a 100-hour ground war (Operation Desert Storm) that drove Iraqi forces out. U.S. goals: restore Kuwait, deter future aggression, protect oil, and enforce international rules against invasion. Bush deliberately didn’t overthrow Saddam, fearing instability. The war briefly ended the “Vietnam syndrome” and announced America as the world’s lone superpower.

Class Presentation Topics (1980–2020)

Short background on every event in the 8 presentation groups — the 1980s through 2020.

Group 1 — 1980–1984

The Election of 1980
Ronald Reagan defeated incumbent Jimmy Carter in a landslide, winning 44 states. Voters blamed Carter for inflation, gas shortages, and the Iran hostage crisis. The election marked the start of the modern conservative era.
Reaganomics
Reagan’s supply-side economic policy: big tax cuts, deregulation, reduced domestic spending, and increased military spending. The idea was that tax cuts would “trickle down,” though deficits exploded instead.
The PATCO Strike (1981)
11,000 air traffic controllers went on strike. Reagan fired all of them and banned them from federal service for life. It broke the strike and signaled the beginning of decades of weakened labor union power.
The “Evil Empire” Speech
In 1983, Reagan publicly called the Soviet Union an “evil empire,” a sharp departure from Nixon-era détente. The speech captured his early confrontational Cold War stance.
Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI / “Star Wars”)
Reagan’s proposed space-based missile defense system announced in 1983. Critics called it unrealistic, but it pressured the USSR into arms negotiations they couldn’t afford to match.
Appointment of Sandra Day O’Connor
In 1981, Reagan appointed O’Connor, the first woman ever on the Supreme Court. She became an influential swing vote for 25 years.

Group 2 — 1985–1989

Iran-Contra Affair
Reagan administration officials secretly sold weapons to Iran (which was under embargo) and used the profits to fund Contra rebels in Nicaragua, violating a congressional ban. The scandal nearly led to impeachment.
Immigration Reform and Control Act (1986)
Gave legal status to about 3 million undocumented immigrants already in the U.S., while also trying to punish employers who hired unauthorized workers. A rare bipartisan immigration law.
The “Tear Down This Wall” Speech (1987)
Reagan stood at the Brandenburg Gate in West Berlin and challenged Soviet leader Gorbachev: “Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall!” The Berlin Wall fell two years later.
INF Treaty (1987)
Reagan and Gorbachev agreed to eliminate all intermediate-range nuclear missiles from Europe — the first real nuclear disarmament treaty of the Cold War.
Exxon Valdez Oil Spill (1989)
An Exxon oil tanker ran aground in Alaska’s Prince William Sound, spilling 11 million gallons of crude oil. It became one of the worst environmental disasters in U.S. history and pushed new oil safety laws.
The Fall of the Berlin Wall (1989)
On November 9, 1989, East Germans tore down the wall that had divided Berlin since 1961. It marked the effective end of the Cold War and the collapse of Soviet control over Eastern Europe.

Group 3 — 1990–1994

The Persian Gulf War (1991)
After Iraq invaded Kuwait in 1990, George H. W. Bush led a UN coalition that expelled Iraqi forces in a 100-hour ground war (Operation Desert Storm). It was a major U.S. military victory and briefly ended the “Vietnam syndrome.”
Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA)
Signed in 1990, the ADA banned discrimination against people with disabilities in jobs, schools, transportation, and public places — requiring wheelchair ramps, accessible bathrooms, and more.
The 1992 Election
Bill Clinton defeated George H. W. Bush and third-party candidate Ross Perot, ending 12 years of Republican control of the White House. Clinton ran as a centrist “New Democrat.”
The LA Riots (1992)
Sparked when four LAPD officers were acquitted despite video showing them beating Rodney King. Six days of riots in Los Angeles killed over 60 people and caused $1 billion in damage, highlighting racial tensions in urban America.
NAFTA (1994)
The North American Free Trade Agreement created a free-trade zone between the U.S., Canada, and Mexico. Supporters said it boosted trade; critics said it sent American manufacturing jobs to Mexico.
Contract with America (1994)
Led by Newt Gingrich, Republicans campaigned on a 10-point conservative agenda (balanced budget, welfare reform, tax cuts) and won control of both houses of Congress for the first time in 40 years.

Group 4 — 1995–1999

The Oklahoma City Bombing (1995)
Anti-government extremist Timothy McVeigh bombed a federal building in Oklahoma City, killing 168 people — the deadliest domestic terror attack in U.S. history before 9/11.
The Welfare Reform Act (1996)
Clinton signed a Republican-led law replacing AFDC with TANF. It added work requirements and time limits for welfare, fulfilling Clinton’s pledge to “end welfare as we know it.”
The Dot-Com Boom
In the late 1990s, internet startups flooded the stock market. Huge growth, huge hype. The bubble burst in 2000–2001 and wiped out trillions in stock value.
The Clinton Impeachment (1998)
Clinton was impeached by the House for lying under oath about his affair with intern Monica Lewinsky. The Senate acquitted him, and he served out his term.
The Repeal of Glass-Steagall (1999)
Congress repealed Depression-era law that separated commercial and investment banking. Critics later blamed the repeal for helping cause the 2008 financial crisis.
The Defense of Marriage Act (DOMA, 1996)
Clinton-signed law defining marriage as between a man and a woman for federal purposes. The Supreme Court struck down its key section in 2013 and the rest in 2015.

Group 5 — 2000–2004

The 2000 Election
Bush vs. Gore came down to Florida, where the vote was so close it took 36 days and a Supreme Court ruling (Bush v. Gore) to decide. Bush won despite losing the popular vote.
September 11th Attacks
Al-Qaeda hijackers crashed planes into the World Trade Center, the Pentagon, and a field in Pennsylvania, killing nearly 3,000 people. It transformed American foreign policy and led to the War on Terror.
The USA PATRIOT Act
Passed weeks after 9/11, the law vastly expanded government surveillance powers — wiretapping, data collection, financial tracking — in the name of counter-terrorism. Civil liberties groups strongly opposed it.
The Invasion of Iraq (2003)
Bush invaded Iraq, claiming Saddam Hussein had “weapons of mass destruction” (he didn’t). The invasion quickly toppled Saddam but led to years of insurgency and instability.
Department of Homeland Security (DHS)
Created in 2002 by merging 22 existing federal agencies. DHS coordinates immigration, border security, emergency response, and counter-terrorism.
The Bush Doctrine
The Bush administration’s post-9/11 foreign policy: the U.S. claimed the right to preemptive war against potential threats and to spread democracy in the Middle East by force.

Group 6 — 2005–2009

Hurricane Katrina (2005)
A Category 5 hurricane devastated New Orleans, killing over 1,800. FEMA’s slow response and images of Black residents stranded for days exposed deep problems with federal disaster response and racial inequality.
The iPhone Launch (2007)
Apple released the first iPhone, combining a phone, internet browser, and music player. It kicked off the smartphone era and fundamentally changed how Americans live and communicate.
District of Columbia v. Heller (2008)
Supreme Court ruled 5–4 that the Second Amendment protects an individual right to own firearms, independent of service in a militia — a major expansion of gun rights.
The 2008 Financial Crisis
The housing bubble burst, major banks collapsed (Lehman Brothers), and the stock market crashed. The federal government bailed out banks and the auto industry. It triggered the Great Recession.
The Election 2008
Barack Obama defeated John McCain to become the first Black U.S. president. His campaign used grassroots organizing and digital fundraising at unprecedented scale.
The Lilly Ledbetter Fair Pay Act (2009)
The first law Obama signed. It made it easier for women to sue employers over pay discrimination by resetting the statute of limitations with each unfair paycheck.

Group 7 — 2010–2014

The Affordable Care Act (2010)
Also known as Obamacare. It expanded health insurance to about 20 million Americans, banned denial for pre-existing conditions, and required most people to have insurance. Polarizing, but it survived multiple legal and political challenges.
Citizens United v. FEC (2010)
Supreme Court ruled that limits on corporate and union spending on elections violate free speech, opening the door to unlimited outside political spending through “Super PACs.”
The Tea Party Movement
A grassroots conservative movement that rose after Obama’s election, opposing higher taxes, the ACA, and the 2009 stimulus. It helped Republicans win back the House in 2010 and pushed the GOP further right.
The End of “Don’t Ask, Don’t Tell” (2010)
Obama signed legislation ending the 1993 policy that had forced gay service members to hide their sexuality. For the first time, LGBTQ Americans could serve openly in the military.
“Arab Spring” & U.S. Involvement (2011)
Pro-democracy uprisings across the Middle East toppled dictators in Tunisia, Egypt, and Libya. The U.S. supported NATO action in Libya but the region broadly destabilized in the aftermath.
The Rise of the Gig Economy
Companies like Uber (2009), Airbnb (2008), and TaskRabbit made it possible for Americans to work short, flexible contracts through apps. The gig economy brought flexibility but also weaker labor protections and unstable income.

Group 8 — 2015–2020

The Paris Climate Accord (2015)
A global agreement in which 196 countries pledged to cut greenhouse gas emissions to limit climate change. Obama joined; Trump withdrew in 2017; Biden rejoined in 2021.
The 2016 Election
Donald Trump defeated Hillary Clinton, winning the Electoral College despite losing the popular vote by about 3 million. His populist, nationalist campaign reshaped the Republican Party.
The US-China Trade War
Trump imposed tariffs on hundreds of billions of dollars of Chinese goods starting in 2018. China retaliated. The trade war affected American farmers, factories, and consumers and marked a turn against globalization.
The #MeToo Movement (2017)
After allegations against Hollywood producer Harvey Weinstein, millions of women used #MeToo to share stories of sexual harassment and assault. It led to firings, resignations, and a national reckoning with workplace misconduct.
The First Impeachment of Donald Trump (2019)
Trump was impeached by the House for abuse of power and obstruction of Congress, after pressuring Ukraine to investigate Joe Biden. The Senate acquitted him.
Death of George Floyd (2020)
Minneapolis police officer Derek Chauvin killed George Floyd by kneeling on his neck for over 9 minutes. Video of the killing sparked the largest protest movement in U.S. history, in cities across the world.

The Big Story: America from the 1960s to the End of the Cold War

An essay-length narrative tying together everything in Unit 7.

The period covered by Unit 7 — roughly the mid-1960s through the end of the Cold War in 1991 — is one of the most dramatic stretches in American history. In less than three decades, the country swung from the high-water mark of postwar liberalism to the full conservative revolution of the Reagan era, lost a war, saw its president resign in disgrace, lived through an economic crisis that shook its faith in its own future, and then watched the Soviet Union collapse and a new American-dominated world order take shape. Underneath all of it was a running argument about what America was supposed to be, who it was supposed to include, and what the government was supposed to do.

The story really starts with Lyndon Johnson and the Great Society. Johnson believed the federal government could solve poverty, fix schools, guarantee health care, and finish the civil rights movement’s work by law. For a moment, it looked like he was right. Medicare, Medicaid, the Voting Rights Act, Head Start, and the Immigration Act of 1965 all came in a burst of legislation unlike anything since the New Deal. But the ambition of the Great Society ran headlong into two walls. The first was Vietnam. As Johnson escalated the war from 16,000 advisors to more than 500,000 troops, the money and the moral authority that the Great Society needed were drained away. The second wall was something harder to name — the growing feeling, on both the right and the left, that big federal government wasn’t actually working. On the right, conservatives attacked the welfare state as wasteful and paternalistic. On the left, Black Power, women’s liberation, the Chicano movement, the American Indian Movement, and the New Left all insisted that American liberalism wasn’t radical enough to fix racism, patriarchy, or poverty.

1968 cracked the country open. The Tet Offensive destroyed Johnson’s Vietnam policy. Johnson stepped aside. Martin Luther King Jr. was assassinated in April, and over 100 cities burned. Robert F. Kennedy was assassinated two months later. The Democratic Convention in Chicago exploded into televised violence between antiwar protesters and police. Richard Nixon won the presidency that fall by promising “law and order” to what he called the “silent majority,” and by pulling white Southerners into the Republican Party with the Southern Strategy — a coded racial appeal about states’ rights, busing, and crime. The FDR Democratic coalition that had dominated American politics since 1932 was breaking apart, and the building blocks of the modern Republican Party were being put in place.

The 1970s extended that crisis. Nixon eventually ended the Vietnam War through the Paris Peace Accords in 1973, but the war’s effects lingered for decades: a shattered military, a broken draft, the War Powers Act, and the “Vietnam syndrome” that made Americans deeply suspicious of foreign interventions. And Nixon himself became a symbol of a much bigger problem. The Watergate scandal revealed not just a botched burglary, but a pattern of political sabotage, illegal surveillance, and obstruction of justice organized from inside the White House. Nixon resigned in August 1974, the only U.S. president ever to do so. The message from Vietnam and Watergate together was unmistakable: the government had lied, and people were no longer sure they could trust it.

At the same time, the American economy that had made the country’s postwar self-image possible — rising wages, secure factory jobs, one-income families — was falling apart. The 1973 OPEC oil embargo quadrupled gas prices, triggered long lines at the pump, and exposed the fragility of an economy built on cheap oil. Stagflation — high inflation combined with slow growth — broke the tools the federal government had used since the New Deal. Deindustrialization gutted the Rust Belt; Pittsburgh, Cleveland, Detroit, Buffalo, and Gary lost huge shares of their factories, jobs, and populations. Married women entered the workforce in record numbers to keep family incomes afloat. Americans took on more consumer debt. And the idea that the government was supposed to solve economic problems through programs and regulation started to feel, to many voters, like part of the problem.

All of this set the stage for a conservative comeback. Phyllis Schlafly’s STOP ERA campaign defeated the Equal Rights Amendment in the 1970s and proved that grassroots conservative women were a real political force. Jerry Falwell’s Moral Majority mobilized evangelicals around abortion, school prayer, and family values. California’s Proposition 13 (1978) showed that Americans were fed up with taxes. The Iran hostage crisis, a stuck economy, and Jimmy Carter’s “malaise” speech all combined to finish off the liberal consensus. In 1980, Ronald Reagan swept into office promising to cut taxes, shrink government, rebuild the military, and restore American greatness. He won 44 states.

Reagan’s domestic policy — tax cuts, deregulation, reduced social spending, and a huge military buildup — put the New Right’s ideology into action. His 1981 tax act slashed income tax rates; the 1986 tax reform dropped the top marginal rate to 28%. He fired striking air traffic controllers in 1981 and permanently weakened American labor unions. He appointed conservative judges who would shape American law for a generation, including Sandra Day O’Connor (the first woman on the Supreme Court). The economy boomed from 1983 to 1990 after a sharp recession, inflation dropped, and the stock market soared. But the bill came due in other ways. The national debt tripled. Income inequality widened dramatically. The AIDS crisis spread while the administration looked the other way, costing thousands of lives. Savings-and-loan banks collapsed after being deregulated. The Iran-Contra affair — an illegal scheme to sell weapons to Iran and fund Nicaraguan rebels with the proceeds — almost ended Reagan’s presidency.

And yet, abroad, Reagan’s Cold War approach worked in ways few people expected. His first-term military buildup and “evil empire” rhetoric scared the Soviets badly. But the real change came in his second term, after Mikhail Gorbachev became Soviet leader in 1985. Gorbachev’s reforms — perestroika and glasnost — were meant to save communism, but they ended up accelerating its collapse. Reagan and Gorbachev met four times, signed the INF Treaty in 1987 eliminating a whole class of nuclear weapons, and together transformed U.S.-Soviet relations from near-war to real partnership. In 1989 the Berlin Wall fell. In 1990 Germany reunified. In December 1991 the Soviet Union itself dissolved. The Cold War, which had shaped American foreign policy since the late 1940s, was over.

The 1991 Persian Gulf War bookended the period. Iraq’s invasion of Kuwait in August 1990 prompted George H. W. Bush to assemble a massive international coalition under UN authority. Operation Desert Storm drove Iraqi forces out of Kuwait in 100 hours of ground combat. Bush deliberately stopped short of overthrowing Saddam Hussein, but the war briefly ended the Vietnam syndrome and announced that America was now the world’s only superpower.

Taken together, Unit 7 is the story of how the liberal consensus of the 1960s broke apart, how the conservative revolution of the 1980s took its place, and how America came out of that long, turbulent period as the dominant nation of a reshaped world. The fault lines set in those decades — race, gender, culture, religion, globalization, the role of government, the meaning of patriotism — are the same ones American politics is still fighting over today. Understanding this period isn’t just about memorizing dates; it’s about understanding why America looks the way it does now.
