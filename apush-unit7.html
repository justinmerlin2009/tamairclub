Chapter 27: The Civil Rights Movement

Section 27.3 only for Unit 7
1. How would you characterize the different forms that the Black Power movement took? How were these forms related to traditions in African American history?

The Black Power movement took many forms, but it was united by a rejection of the idea that Black Americans had to patiently earn equality through a white-dominated system. It grew out of frustration with the slow pace of civil rights reform, the persistence of poverty and police violence in northern cities, and the refusal of white moderates to push for economic justice. One strand was cultural nationalism — the celebration of African heritage, “Black is Beautiful,” natural hair, African dress, and the development of Black studies programs at universities. Another strand was political radicalism, represented most famously by the Black Panther Party, founded in Oakland in 1966 by Huey Newton and Bobby Seale. The Panthers organized armed community self-defense against police brutality but also ran free breakfast programs, clinics, and schools in Black neighborhoods. A third strand was religious and separatist, most visible in the Nation of Islam and the teachings of Malcolm X, who argued that Black Americans should build independent institutions rather than integrate into white society.

These forms connected to deep traditions in African American history. Black nationalism and calls for self-determination echoed Marcus Garvey’s UNIA in the 1920s and even earlier pan-African thinkers. The idea of armed self-defense went back to Black communities resisting Reconstruction-era violence. The tradition of building independent Black institutions — churches, schools, businesses — had roots stretching back to the antebellum free Black community. Black Power was not a break from earlier Black struggle; it was a continuation of a long-standing debate over whether integration or self-determination was the better path to freedom.

2. What did the Chicano and American Indian movements have in common with the Black freedom movement?

The Chicano and American Indian movements borrowed directly from the tactics, rhetoric, and pride of the Black freedom struggle. Each movement combined direct action with a demand for cultural pride and political power. César Chávez and Dolores Huerta’s United Farm Workers (UFW) organized Mexican American farmworkers through strikes, boycotts (most famously the grape boycott), and nonviolent marches modeled on the civil rights movement. Younger Chicano activists formed groups like the Brown Berets and pushed for bilingual education, Chicano studies, and an end to police abuse in the Southwest.

The American Indian Movement (AIM), founded in 1968, used direct action to demand treaty rights, tribal sovereignty, and better conditions on reservations. The occupation of Alcatraz (1969) and the 1973 takeover of Wounded Knee were dramatic protests designed, like the civil rights sit-ins, to force the nation to pay attention. The Young Lords, a Puerto Rican group modeled on the Black Panthers, brought similar activism to urban neighborhoods in New York and Chicago. What all these movements had in common with Black Power was a rejection of assimilation, a demand for ethnic pride and self-determination, an embrace of grassroots protest, and a willingness to challenge both liberal gradualism and conservative backlash.

Chapter 28: Liberal Crisis & Conservative Rebirth

APUSH Era 8 (1945–1980)
Identify the Big IdeaWhat were liberalism’s social and political achievements in the 1960s, and how did debates over liberal values contribute to conflict at home and reflect tension abroad?
1. What new roles did the federal government assume under Great Society initiatives, and how did they extend the New Deal tradition?

Lyndon Johnson’s Great Society massively expanded the federal government’s role in American life, taking New Deal liberalism into areas FDR had never reached. The federal government now took direct responsibility for fighting poverty, guaranteeing health care for the elderly and poor, protecting civil rights, funding education, and shaping immigration. The War on Poverty created the Office of Economic Opportunity, Job Corps, VISTA, and Head Start, which tried to attack the structural causes of poverty through education, job training, and community action rather than simple cash relief. Medicare provided health insurance for Americans over 65, and Medicaid covered low-income Americans — the first major federal health care programs in U.S. history.

Other Great Society programs included the Elementary and Secondary Education Act (federal aid to public schools), the Higher Education Act (federal loans and scholarships), the Department of Housing and Urban Development, and the National Endowment for the Arts and Humanities. The Immigration Act of 1965 abolished the national-origins quota system that had been in place since the 1920s and opened U.S. immigration to the rest of the world. These programs extended the New Deal tradition by keeping its core idea — that the federal government is responsible for the welfare of ordinary Americans — but went much further. Where the New Deal had focused on economic recovery, the Great Society focused on social justice, racial equality, and quality of life.

2. What factors accounted for the resurgence of feminism in the 1960s?

Several overlapping factors revived feminism in the 1960s. Betty Friedan’s The Feminine Mystique (1963) gave voice to what she called “the problem that has no name” — the frustration and boredom of educated, middle-class white women confined to suburban housewife roles. The book made millions of women realize their discontent was widespread and structural, not personal. At the same time, the civil rights movement gave women activists both inspiration (the language of equality, nonviolent tactics, grassroots organizing) and personal motivation, since many female activists in SNCC and SDS had experienced sexism inside the movements themselves.

Demographic and economic changes mattered too. More women were going to college, delaying marriage, and entering the paid workforce, which made legal restrictions on their jobs and pay more visible and more resented. The development of the birth control pill in 1960 gave women more control over reproduction and allowed them to plan careers. Finally, Kennedy’s Presidential Commission on the Status of Women (chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt) produced a 1963 report documenting pervasive workplace discrimination, setting the stage for the legal and political battles of the decade.

3. What legal victories did women achieve in the 1960s?

Women won several major legal victories during the 1960s. The Equal Pay Act of 1963 required that men and women doing the same work be paid equally — the first federal law against sex-based wage discrimination. Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 banned employment discrimination on the basis of sex as well as race, religion, and national origin. (Ironically, “sex” was added by a segregationist congressman who hoped including it would sink the bill; instead, it became one of the most important tools feminists used for decades.) In 1965, the Supreme Court ruled in Griswold v. Connecticut that married couples had a constitutional right to privacy in their use of contraception — a precedent that laid the groundwork for Roe v. Wade in 1973.

In 1966, frustrated by weak federal enforcement of Title VII, Betty Friedan and others founded the National Organization for Women (NOW), which pushed for enforcement of existing laws and new legislation, including an Equal Rights Amendment. By the end of the decade, women had a real legal framework to challenge discrimination — even if enforcement was inconsistent and the battle for full equality would continue for decades.

1. How did American military engagement in Vietnam evolve under Johnson?

When Johnson took office after Kennedy’s assassination in November 1963, there were about 16,000 U.S. military advisors in South Vietnam. Within five years, that number had grown to over 500,000 combat troops, and American involvement had transformed from a limited advisory role into a full-scale ground war. The turning point came in August 1964, when Johnson claimed (falsely or at least misleadingly) that North Vietnamese ships had attacked American destroyers in the Gulf of Tonkin. Congress responded by passing the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, which gave Johnson broad authority to use military force in Southeast Asia without a formal declaration of war. It became the legal basis for the entire escalation that followed.

In 1965, Johnson launched Operation Rolling Thunder, a sustained bombing campaign against North Vietnam, and sent the first large deployment of Marines to Da Nang. By 1968, the U.S. was fighting a conventional war against the North Vietnamese Army and a guerrilla war against the Viet Cong in the South, using search-and-destroy missions, napalm, defoliants like Agent Orange, and strategic hamlet programs. The Tet Offensive in January 1968 — a coordinated surprise attack by the Viet Cong on dozens of South Vietnamese cities — was technically a U.S. military victory but a political catastrophe, because it showed that after three years of escalation, the enemy was still capable of striking everywhere at once. Johnson’s approval collapsed, and in March 1968 he announced he would not run for reelection.

2. How did the war in Vietnam affect the United States domestically in the 1960s?

The war reshaped American society. Domestically, it drained resources Johnson had hoped to pour into the Great Society, forcing him to choose between “guns and butter” — and ultimately underfunding both. It drove massive inflation, unbalanced the federal budget, and damaged Americans’ trust in the government as officials repeatedly lied or minimized what was happening. Television brought the war into living rooms, and graphic footage of combat, civilian casualties, and events like the Tet Offensive eroded public support.

The war also split the country. A large antiwar movement emerged, especially on college campuses, where students protested the draft, burned draft cards, and organized “teach-ins.” The draft disproportionately fell on working-class and minority men, while middle-class college students could often get deferments, which fueled anger about class inequality. Civil rights leaders including Martin Luther King Jr. came out against the war, linking militarism abroad to poverty and racism at home. By 1968, the combination of war, urban riots, assassinations (King in April, Robert Kennedy in June), and protests at the Democratic National Convention in Chicago produced a sense of national crisis unlike anything since the Civil War.

3. What were the factors behind the student protest movements of the 1960s?

Several factors produced the student movements of the 1960s. Postwar prosperity and the GI Bill had dramatically expanded higher education; by the mid-1960s, there were more college students than ever before, concentrated on large campuses. Many students were inspired by the civil rights movement, especially the sit-ins and Freedom Rides, and applied the same tactics — nonviolent direct action, grassroots organizing, moral confrontation — to their own concerns. The draft and the Vietnam War gave their activism immediate personal stakes.

On the left, the most important group was Students for a Democratic Society (SDS), founded in 1960 and best known for its 1962 Port Huron Statement, which called for “participatory democracy” and rejected Cold War liberalism. SDS was central to organizing antiwar protests and the free speech movement at Berkeley in 1964. On the right, Young Americans for Freedom (YAF), founded in 1960 at William F. Buckley’s estate, articulated a conservative vision of individual freedom, free markets, anti-communism, and traditional values. YAF became a training ground for the conservative movement that would eventually carry Barry Goldwater, Richard Nixon, and Ronald Reagan to national prominence. The 1960s student movement was not just a leftist phenomenon — it was a generational mobilization on both sides of the political spectrum.

1. Why might a Democratic supporter of FDR in the 1940s have decided to vote for Republican Richard Nixon in 1968?

A white working-class or middle-class voter who had been loyal to FDR’s New Deal coalition in the 1940s could have many reasons to vote Republican by 1968. The Democratic Party of FDR’s era was built on a fragile alliance of Northern industrial workers, Southern white segregationists, urban ethnic groups, and Black Americans. By the late 1960s, that coalition was breaking apart. The Civil Rights Act (1964) and Voting Rights Act (1965) alienated white Southerners who had been Democrats since Reconstruction. The urban riots of 1965–68 scared many white city and suburban voters. “Law and order” became a dominant concern as crime rates rose, and Democrats seemed to many voters to be soft on disorder.

At the same time, the Great Society’s expansion of government spending on programs seen as benefiting minorities led some white voters to feel the Democratic Party had abandoned them. The antiwar movement, counterculture, and campus radicalism made many older voters feel their country was being torn apart by people who had none of the patriotism or discipline they valued. Nixon’s campaign brilliantly exploited all of this, promising “law and order,” appealing to what he called the silent majority, and using the Southern Strategy to win over white Southerners with coded racial messaging about states’ rights and “forced busing.” For a 1940s FDR Democrat, voting for Nixon in 1968 could feel like voting to restore order, stability, and the country they remembered.

2. In what ways did 1968 represent a turning point in postwar history?

1968 was probably the most turbulent year in postwar American history, and it marked the end of the optimistic liberal consensus of the early 1960s. The Tet Offensive in January destroyed the credibility of Johnson’s Vietnam policy. Johnson shocked the country by withdrawing from the presidential race on March 31. On April 4, Martin Luther King Jr. was assassinated in Memphis, setting off riots in more than 100 cities. On June 5, Robert F. Kennedy was assassinated in Los Angeles on the night he won the California Democratic primary, closing off what many saw as the last hope of uniting the antiwar left with working-class white and minority voters.

In August, the Democratic National Convention in Chicago devolved into violent clashes between antiwar protesters and Mayor Daley’s police, broadcast live on television. Nixon won a narrow three-way race in November (with segregationist George Wallace pulling millions of votes). Internationally, the Soviets invaded Czechoslovakia to crush the Prague Spring. Taken together, 1968 shattered confidence that liberalism could solve America’s problems, opened the door to the conservative ascendancy, and set the political fault lines — race, war, culture, “law and order” — that would dominate the next several decades.

3. How did women’s liberation after 1968 differ from the women’s movement of the early 1960s?

The early-1960s women’s movement, led by groups like NOW, focused on legal and economic equality — equal pay, equal employment, enforcement of civil rights protections, and eventually the Equal Rights Amendment. It was largely led by middle-class professional women, worked through legislation and lawsuits, and aimed at integrating women into existing institutions on equal terms with men.

The women’s liberation movement that emerged after 1968 was younger, more radical, and more grassroots. Many of its leaders had come out of the civil rights and antiwar movements and were furious about the sexism they had experienced there. Rather than just asking to be included, they challenged the basic structures of patriarchy — marriage, the family, sexuality, beauty standards, and male-dominated media. Consciousness-raising groups, in which women gathered in living rooms to share personal experiences, coined the slogan “the personal is political.” Activists protested the Miss America pageant (1968), published books like Kate Millett’s Sexual Politics, opened women’s health clinics, and fought for abortion rights. Women’s liberation was broader, angrier, and more culturally radical than the earlier reform-oriented feminism.

4. What gains did women achieve in the late 1960s/early 1970s?

The late 1960s and early 1970s brought huge gains. Title IX (1972) banned sex discrimination in any education program receiving federal funds — this transformed women’s access to higher education, professional schools, and especially athletics. Roe v. Wade (1973) established a constitutional right to abortion, rooted in the right to privacy developed in Griswold. The Equal Rights Amendment passed Congress in 1972 and was initially ratified by most states, though it ultimately fell short by three states and was never added to the Constitution.

Other gains included the opening of military academies to women, expanded access to credit through the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (1974), stronger enforcement of Title VII, state reforms of marriage and divorce law (including no-fault divorce), the recognition of sexual harassment and marital rape as legal issues, and an explosion of women in higher education and the professions. Culturally, it became normal for women to plan careers, delay marriage, and live independently. Not all battles were won — and a powerful backlash was gathering — but this was arguably the most transformative period for American women since women’s suffrage.

1. How did the Vietnam War end? What was its legacy for the United States?

Nixon pursued a policy he called Vietnamization — gradually turning combat operations over to the South Vietnamese while continuing U.S. air support. At the same time, he expanded the war into Cambodia and Laos in 1970, secretly bombing Cambodia to disrupt North Vietnamese supply lines. The invasion of Cambodia triggered massive protests, including at Kent State, where National Guardsmen killed four students in May 1970. After years of negotiations, Nixon’s national security advisor Henry Kissinger signed the Paris Peace Accords in January 1973, which ended direct U.S. involvement and returned American POWs. The U.S. withdrew its last combat troops, but the war between North and South Vietnam continued. In April 1975, North Vietnamese forces took Saigon, ending the war with a communist victory and images of Americans evacuating the embassy by helicopter.

The legacy of the war was profound. More than 58,000 Americans and an estimated 2–3 million Vietnamese were killed. The war produced the so-called “Vietnam syndrome” — a deep reluctance of both policymakers and the public to get involved in foreign wars. It led to the War Powers Act (1973), which limited the president’s ability to send troops into combat without congressional approval. It shattered Americans’ trust in their government, contributed to the end of the draft in 1973, and damaged the military’s reputation for a generation. It also left lasting wounds for veterans, who returned to a divided country and often received inadequate care for physical and psychological injuries, including exposure to Agent Orange and PTSD.

2. What was the legacy of the Watergate scandal?

The Watergate scandal began in June 1972 when five burglars linked to Nixon’s reelection committee were caught breaking into Democratic National Committee headquarters at the Watergate complex in Washington, D.C. Investigative reporting by the Washington Post (Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein), Senate hearings, and a special prosecutor gradually revealed that the break-in was part of a much broader pattern of political sabotage, illegal surveillance, and abuses of power by Nixon and his White House. When a secret taping system inside the Oval Office was exposed, the Supreme Court unanimously ruled in United States v. Nixon (1974) that he had to turn over the tapes. The tapes proved Nixon had personally orchestrated the cover-up. Facing certain impeachment, Nixon resigned on August 9, 1974 — the only U.S. president ever to do so. His successor, Gerald Ford, soon pardoned him, a deeply controversial decision.

Watergate’s legacy was lasting. It deepened Americans’ distrust of government and politicians, which had already been shaken by Vietnam. It led to a wave of reforms: campaign finance laws, strengthening of the Freedom of Information Act, new ethics rules, the Independent Counsel law, and broader congressional reassertion of oversight. It also set a precedent that no president is above the law — the tapes case established that executive privilege is limited. Politically, Watergate hurt the Republican Party in the short term, helping Democrats win big in 1974 and 1976, but in the longer term it contributed to a cynical, suspicious style of American politics that has never really gone away.

28.4 — Key Terms

Silent Majority
Phrase Nixon used to describe ordinary, patriotic, middle-class Americans who he claimed supported the Vietnam War and rejected the counterculture and antiwar protests; central to his 1968 and 1972 coalitions.
Watergate Scandal (1972–73)
Political scandal that began with a break-in at the Democratic National Committee headquarters; widening revelations of abuses of power and a cover-up forced Nixon to resign in August 1974.

Chapter 29: The Search for Order in an Era of Limits

APUSH Era 8/9 (1970s)
Identify the Big IdeaHow did the legacy of social changes — such as shifting gender roles, civil rights, and challenges to the family — in the 1960s continue to reverberate in the 1970s, leading to both new opportunities and political disagreement?
1. What major factors led to the birth of the environmental movement in the 1970s? What was the movement able to achieve?

The modern environmental movement grew out of a combination of scientific warnings, visible disasters, and broader activist energy from the 1960s. The most important single spark was Rachel Carson’s Silent Spring (1962), which exposed how pesticides like DDT were poisoning ecosystems, killing birds, and threatening human health. Carson’s book shifted how Americans thought about industrial chemistry, corporate responsibility, and the fragility of the natural world. A series of visible environmental disasters deepened public concern — the 1969 Santa Barbara oil spill, the Cuyahoga River in Cleveland catching fire that same year, smog covering major cities, and fears about nuclear radiation. Activists connected these problems to the consumer culture, industrial growth, and military-industrial complex that environmentalism critiqued.

The movement produced an extraordinary legislative record, mostly during Nixon’s presidency. The first Earth Day was held in April 1970, drawing an estimated 20 million participants. That same year, Nixon signed the Clean Air Act and created the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). Congress followed with the Clean Water Act (1972), Endangered Species Act (1973), and laws regulating pesticides, toxic waste, and drinking water. DDT was banned in 1972. The Three Mile Island nuclear accident in 1979 derailed the expansion of nuclear power in the U.S. The movement didn’t solve every problem, but it permanently embedded environmental protection in federal law and created a durable public constituency for clean air, clean water, and wildlife protection.

2. Why did the US enter an energy crisis in the 1970s?

The energy crisis had both global and domestic causes. By the 1970s, the U.S. was importing a growing share of its oil from the Middle East, making it dependent on politically unstable producers. The Organization of Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC), dominated by Arab states, realized it had enormous leverage. In October 1973, during the Yom Kippur War, OPEC’s Arab members imposed an oil embargo on the United States and other countries that supported Israel. Oil prices quadrupled, gas stations ran out of fuel, long lines formed at pumps, and odd-even rationing was introduced. A second oil shock followed the 1979 Iranian Revolution, when Iran’s oil exports collapsed and prices spiked again.

Domestically, U.S. oil production had actually peaked in 1970 and was declining, so the country could no longer meet its own demand. The economy had been built around cheap gasoline, big cars, and sprawling suburbs. When oil prices exploded, the whole economic model shook — high inflation combined with stagnant growth, a condition economists called stagflation, which defied standard Keynesian remedies. Presidents Nixon, Ford, and Carter all tried to respond with price controls, conservation appeals, the creation of the Department of Energy, and research into alternative energy. Carter even called it the “moral equivalent of war.” But the crisis shattered the postwar sense of unlimited American abundance.

3. What major developments shaped the American economy in the 1970s and contributed to its transformation?

Several overlapping developments transformed the American economy in the 1970s. The first was deindustrialization: the manufacturing base of the Northeast and Midwest — steel, auto, textiles, rubber — collapsed in the face of foreign competition (especially from West Germany and Japan), aging factories, rising labor and energy costs, and corporate disinvestment. Whole cities in what came to be called the Rust Belt — Pittsburgh, Cleveland, Detroit, Buffalo, Gary — lost large shares of their industrial jobs and population. Meanwhile, a new economy grew in the Sun Belt, based on defense contracting, oil and gas, tourism, agribusiness, and — by the late 1970s — the beginnings of computing and finance.

Second, stagflation defied government policy tools and damaged confidence in the Keynesian economic consensus that had dominated since the New Deal. Third, the tax revolt took off, epitomized by California’s Proposition 13 (1978), which capped property taxes and signaled a broader rejection of the idea that growing government was the answer to every problem. Fourth, women continued to enter the workforce in large numbers, reshaping family life and consumer markets. And fifth, globalization began to accelerate, exposing American workers and firms to intense international competition. All of these changes reduced faith in the liberal economic order of the postwar decades and created the political conditions for Reagan’s conservative turn.

1. How did affirmative action attempt to address the legacy of discrimination, and why did it become so controversial?

Affirmative action was a set of policies designed to expand opportunities for groups — especially African Americans and women — that had historically been excluded from education, employment, and government contracts. Its origins go back to a 1961 executive order by Kennedy requiring federal contractors to “take affirmative action” to ensure equal employment, and to Lyndon Johnson’s 1965 Executive Order 11246, which expanded the concept. In the late 1960s and 1970s, universities, corporations, and government agencies adopted policies that considered race and gender as factors in hiring and admissions, set hiring goals and timetables, and sometimes used quotas. The underlying reasoning was that after centuries of slavery, Jim Crow, and sex discrimination, “color-blind” equal opportunity alone would not level the playing field — active steps were needed to break entrenched patterns of exclusion.

Affirmative action became deeply controversial because critics argued it amounted to “reverse discrimination,” treated individuals as members of groups rather than on their own merits, and unfairly penalized white men (especially working-class ones) who had no personal role in historical racism. Allan Bakke, a white applicant rejected twice by UC Davis Medical School, sued when he learned the school set aside 16 of 100 seats for minority applicants. In Bakke v. University of California (1978), the Supreme Court ruled in a split decision that rigid racial quotas were unconstitutional but that race could be considered as one factor among many in admissions. The ruling left the legal status of affirmative action uncertain and made it a permanent battleground in American politics — with new challenges continuing in every decade since.

2. Why did the Equal Rights Amendment initially gain momentum, and why did it ultimately fail?

The Equal Rights Amendment (ERA) — a constitutional amendment stating that “Equality of rights under the law shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex” — had first been proposed by Alice Paul in 1923. In 1972, riding the energy of second-wave feminism, Congress finally passed the amendment by overwhelming bipartisan majorities and sent it to the states. It initially seemed to be sailing to ratification: within a year, 30 of the required 38 states had approved it. Opinion polls showed clear majorities of Americans, including conservative women, in favor.

Momentum stalled because of a powerful grassroots backlash led by Phyllis Schlafly, a conservative Catholic activist from Illinois. Through her “STOP ERA” campaign (the acronym stood for “Stop Taking Our Privileges”), Schlafly organized traditionalist women to argue that the ERA would end laws protecting women, subject them to the draft, legalize unisex bathrooms, strip mothers of alimony and custody rights, and ultimately threaten the family. She effectively tied the ERA to the cultural anxieties of the 1970s — feminism, abortion, gay rights, changing family structures. By 1977 ratification stalled three states short of the 38 needed, and even after Congress extended the deadline to 1982, no more states ratified. Five states actually rescinded their approval. The ERA’s defeat was the signature victory of the emerging Religious Right and showed that grassroots conservatism was a political force to be reckoned with.

1. How did deindustrialization reshape work, income, and daily life for American families after 1970?

Deindustrialization ended the postwar era of rising wages and secure factory jobs that had allowed a single (usually male) breadwinner to support a middle-class family. As steel mills, auto plants, and textile factories closed across the Rust Belt, millions of well-paid unionized jobs disappeared. Many displaced workers ended up in lower-paid service jobs — retail, food service, hospitality — without the benefits, job security, or union protections they had had before. Real wages for men stagnated beginning in the mid-1970s and would basically not grow for decades.

To maintain their living standards, families adapted in two major ways. First, married women entered the paid workforce in record numbers — by the end of the 1970s, a majority of married women with children were working outside the home. Two-income families became the norm rather than the exception, which reshaped household life, child care arrangements, and gender expectations. Second, Americans took on much more consumer debt, including credit cards, which spread widely in the 1970s and 1980s. Daily life felt more precarious, divorce rates rose, young people delayed marriage and childbearing, and many communities — especially white working-class ones — experienced declining economic mobility and deepening social strain. This combination of economic anxiety and cultural change formed the backdrop for the political turn to conservatism.

Chapter 30: Conservative America Ascendant (1973–1991)

APUSH Era 9 (1980–present)
Identify the Big IdeaWhat factors made the rise of the New Right possible, and what ideas about freedom and citizenship did conservatives articulate in the 1980s?
1. Which were the key groups of the new Republican coalition? Were their goals complementary or contradictory?

The New Right that built Ronald Reagan’s 1980 coalition brought together several distinct groups. First were economic conservatives and libertarians, who wanted lower taxes, deregulation, smaller government, and free-market policies. They drew on thinkers like Milton Friedman and Friedrich Hayek, as well as the tax revolt movement led by Proposition 13. Second were Cold War anti-communists and defense hawks, who believed the U.S. had gone soft in the 1970s under détente and wanted a massive military buildup to confront the Soviet Union. Third were traditionalist social conservatives — especially the Religious Right, led by Jerry Falwell’s Moral Majority (founded 1979), which opposed abortion, the ERA, gay rights, the sexual revolution, secular public schools, and changes in gender roles. Fourth were neoconservatives, former liberals disillusioned with the Great Society and the antiwar movement, who brought intellectual heft to anti-communism and cultural traditionalism. Finally, there were white Southern voters and working-class “Reagan Democrats” alienated from the post-1968 Democratic Party.

These goals were often complementary in practice but contradictory in principle. Libertarians wanted government out of both the marketplace and the bedroom, but religious conservatives wanted government to enforce traditional morality. Economic conservatives wanted to cut spending, but defense hawks wanted to massively increase military spending. Neoconservatives wanted an activist foreign policy while paleoconservatives wanted isolation. What held the coalition together was shared opposition to the liberal establishment, to the Soviet Union, and to what they saw as the chaos of the 1960s and 1970s. Reagan was skilled enough to paper over the contradictions with a unifying vision of American renewal, though the tensions would eventually resurface within the GOP.

2. In what ways was the New Right “reactive,” responding to liberalism, and in what ways was it “proactive,” asserting its own agenda?

The New Right was reactive in that much of its energy came from opposition to specific liberal policies and cultural changes. Conservatives reacted against the Great Society’s welfare programs, high taxes, affirmative action, forced busing, and federal regulation. They reacted against the counterculture, the antiwar movement, changing gender roles, and the Supreme Court’s decisions on school prayer, abortion, and criminal rights. They reacted against détente, which they saw as appeasement of the Soviets. Grassroots organizations like STOP ERA, antibusing groups, and antiabortion groups were largely oppositional in their framing.

But the New Right was also proactive, with its own distinct vision. Think tanks like the Heritage Foundation (founded 1973) and the American Enterprise Institute built an intellectual infrastructure for conservatism. Activists developed innovative political tools — direct mail fundraising (pioneered by Richard Viguerie), new talk radio and Christian broadcasting networks, grassroots political action committees (PACs), and get-out-the-vote operations in evangelical churches. Intellectually, conservatives articulated a positive vision built around supply-side economics, individual responsibility, traditional family values, strong national defense, and the claim that American greatness depended on freeing citizens from government interference. Barry Goldwater’s The Conscience of a Conservative (1960) had laid out much of this vision years earlier. By the late 1970s, conservatism was no longer just an objection to liberalism; it was a full alternative worldview.

1. Why are the 1980s seen as the beginning of a conservative age?

The 1980s are seen as the beginning of a conservative age because Ronald Reagan’s election in 1980 and reelection in 1984 marked a decisive shift in American political, economic, and cultural life. Reagan won in 1980 by defeating incumbent Jimmy Carter in a landslide, carrying 44 states, and in 1984 he won 49 states against Walter Mondale — one of the largest electoral victories in U.S. history. Even after Reagan, his vice president George H. W. Bush won a third consecutive Republican presidential term in 1988, and many of Reagan’s core ideas — lower taxes, deregulation, free markets, skepticism of big government — became the new common sense of American politics for decades, even among Democrats (Bill Clinton in the 1990s would declare that “the era of big government is over”).

The conservative shift was not just about elections. Reagan reshaped the federal judiciary, appointing Sandra Day O’Connor, Antonin Scalia, and Anthony Kennedy to the Supreme Court and more than 350 lower federal judges. Labor unions were weakened after Reagan fired striking air traffic controllers in 1981. Tax rates on the highest incomes were slashed. Business deregulation accelerated. Culturally, patriotism and traditional values surged in popular media, and Americans expressed renewed optimism about the country and capitalism. Combined with the collapse of the Soviet Union by 1991, the 1980s felt like a vindication of conservative ideas.

2. What were the key elements of Reagan’s domestic policy? How did that policy reflect conservative ideology?

Reagan’s domestic policy rested on four main pillars: tax cuts, deregulation, cuts to domestic spending, and increased military spending. The Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 slashed individual income tax rates by 25% across the board, lowered the top marginal rate from 70% to 50% (later to 28% in the 1986 Tax Reform Act), and cut taxes on capital gains and businesses. Federal regulations on banking, airlines, trucking, telecommunications, and the environment were rolled back. Reagan cut or capped spending on food stamps, welfare, public housing, job training, Medicaid, and federal student aid, though he largely protected Social Security and Medicare, which were too politically popular to touch. Meanwhile, defense spending soared, reaching about $300 billion a year by the mid-1980s.

These policies reflected conservative ideology in several ways. They rested on the belief that the government is not the solution; the government is the problem (Reagan’s famous line). They assumed that economic freedom — lower taxes, fewer regulations — would unleash entrepreneurs, investment, and growth. They treated welfare programs as creating dependency rather than solving poverty. They trusted the market over federal bureaucrats to allocate resources. And they redefined patriotism around military strength and a confident anti-communist foreign policy. Altogether, they marked the clearest break from the New Deal/Great Society liberal consensus since the 1930s.

3. To what extent did Reaganomics achieve its goals?

Reaganomics had mixed and contested results. By its own measures, it could claim some important successes. Inflation, which had reached double digits under Carter, dropped sharply — largely because of tight monetary policy by the Federal Reserve under Paul Volcker, but Reagan’s policies supported the effort. After a sharp recession in 1981–82, the economy entered a long expansion from late 1982 through 1990, with strong GDP growth and millions of new jobs. Income tax rates were dramatically lowered. Americans’ confidence in the economy and the country rebounded. Stock markets soared.

But Reaganomics also fell well short of its goals in important ways. Despite promises that tax cuts would pay for themselves through growth, they did not — federal deficits exploded, and the national debt nearly tripled from about $900 billion in 1980 to $2.7 trillion in 1989. The U.S. went from the world’s largest creditor nation to its largest debtor. Income inequality widened substantially; the rich got much richer while working-class and poor Americans saw stagnant or declining real wages. Poverty rates rose during the recession and never fully recovered. Deregulation contributed to the savings-and-loan crisis, which cost taxpayers hundreds of billions of dollars in the late 1980s and early 1990s. Supply-side economics became a lasting article of faith for the Republican Party, but the evidence for its central claim — that tax cuts generate enough growth to pay for themselves — remained thin.

1. How did Reagan’s approach to the Soviet Union change between 1981 and 1989?

Reagan’s Soviet policy changed dramatically over his two terms. In his first term (1981–1985), Reagan pursued an aggressively confrontational approach. He called the Soviet Union the “evil empire” in 1983, launched a massive military buildup, and proposed the Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI), nicknamed “Star Wars,” to build a space-based missile defense system. He supported anti-communist rebels worldwide — including the Contras in Nicaragua, the mujahideen in Afghanistan, and UNITA in Angola — under what was called the Reagan Doctrine. Tensions reached their highest levels since the Cuban Missile Crisis, and some Soviet officials genuinely feared a U.S. first strike.

In his second term (1985–1989), Reagan dramatically shifted toward diplomacy and negotiation. The key catalyst was the rise of Mikhail Gorbachev to Soviet leadership in 1985. Gorbachev launched reforms — perestroika (economic restructuring) and glasnost (political openness) — and was willing to engage Reagan seriously. The two leaders met at four summits — Geneva (1985), Reykjavík (1986), Washington (1987), and Moscow (1988). In 1987, they signed the INF Treaty, eliminating an entire class of intermediate-range nuclear missiles from Europe — the first real disarmament treaty of the Cold War. Reagan famously stood at the Brandenburg Gate in 1987 and challenged, “Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall!” By the time Reagan left office in January 1989, U.S.-Soviet relations had transformed from near-war to genuine partnership, and the Berlin Wall would fall later that year.

2. What factors led to the end of the Cold War?

The end of the Cold War had multiple overlapping causes. The most important was the internal weakness of the Soviet system. The centrally planned Soviet economy had essentially stopped growing by the 1970s and fell further behind the West in technology, consumer goods, and living standards. A costly war in Afghanistan (1979–89) drained Soviet resources and morale. Corruption, cynicism, and ethnic tensions undermined the legitimacy of communist rule. Gorbachev’s reforms — which were meant to save communism — instead loosened the regime’s grip and unleashed forces that toppled it.

American policy also mattered. Reagan’s military buildup and SDI put enormous pressure on a Soviet economy already stretched to the limit; Soviet leaders concluded they could not keep up. The Reagan Doctrine raised the costs of Soviet interventions abroad. Reagan’s willingness, in his second term, to meet Gorbachev as a genuine partner helped Gorbachev make bold moves. In Eastern Europe, the peoples of Poland (with the Solidarity movement), Hungary, Czechoslovakia, and East Germany pushed back against communist rule once it became clear the Soviets would not intervene to prop up local regimes — the crucial turning point being Gorbachev’s renunciation of the Brezhnev Doctrine. In 1989, the Berlin Wall fell; in 1990, Germany reunified; in December 1991, the Soviet Union itself dissolved. It was not any one cause but a combination of Soviet decay, American pressure, and popular uprising.

3. What was Reagan’s legacy?

Reagan’s legacy is large and contested. On the positive side, admirers credit him with restoring American national confidence after the malaise of the 1970s, winning the Cold War without firing a shot, rebuilding the military, sparking a long economic expansion, permanently lowering top tax rates, and transforming political conservatism from a minority movement into a dominant force. He reshaped the Republican Party, the federal judiciary, and the terms of debate in American politics for a generation. His communication skills and unshakable optimism made him one of the most personally popular presidents of the 20th century.

Critics point to serious failures and costs. The national debt tripled under Reagan, and the country became a debtor nation. Income inequality widened dramatically. Poverty and homelessness grew. The administration was slow and dismissive in responding to the AIDS crisis, costing thousands of lives. Deregulation contributed to the savings-and-loan collapse. The Iran-Contra affair — in which administration officials secretly sold arms to Iran and funneled the profits to the Nicaraguan Contras, in violation of U.S. law and congressional prohibitions — damaged Reagan’s credibility and nearly led to impeachment. Critics also argue his rhetoric about “welfare queens” and “states’ rights” carried racial undertones that polarized American politics. Reagan’s legacy is thus both a conservative realignment of American politics and a set of economic, social, and political fault lines that the country is still navigating today.

4. Why did the US intervene in conflicts between Iraq and Iran, and between Iraq and Kuwait? What were the American goals in each case?

The U.S. intervened in both conflicts to protect what it saw as vital strategic interests in the Persian Gulf — especially access to oil and regional stability. In the Iran-Iraq War (1980–88), the U.S. sided mostly with Saddam Hussein’s Iraq against the revolutionary Islamic Republic of Iran, which had seized American hostages in 1979 and denounced the U.S. as the “Great Satan.” The U.S. provided Iraq with intelligence, loans, and dual-use technology, and by the end of the war was escorting Kuwaiti oil tankers through the Gulf after Iranian attacks. The goal was not to see Iraq “win” but to prevent Iran’s revolutionary regime from dominating the region and threatening oil supplies. At the same time, the secret Iran-Contra affair revealed that the administration had also been selling arms to Iran — a deeply contradictory policy.

In August 1990, Saddam Hussein invaded Kuwait, a small, oil-rich U.S. ally. President George H. W. Bush responded by assembling a broad international coalition under U.N. authority. The Persian Gulf War in early 1991 began with a massive air campaign, followed by a 100-hour ground war (Operation Desert Storm) that drove Iraqi forces out of Kuwait. The American goals were to restore Kuwaiti sovereignty, deter aggression against other Gulf states (especially Saudi Arabia), protect the flow of oil, and demonstrate that in a post-Cold War world, international norms against territorial conquest would be enforced. Bush deliberately stopped short of overthrowing Saddam, fearing Iraq’s collapse would destabilize the region. The war was a dramatic U.S. military success, briefly ended the “Vietnam syndrome,” and signaled the emergence of America as the world’s lone superpower.
