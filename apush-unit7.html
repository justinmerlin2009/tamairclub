Chapter 27: The Civil Rights Movement

Section 27.3 only for Unit 7
1. How would you characterize the different forms that the Black Power movement took? How were these forms related to traditions in African American history?

The Black Power movement had different forms, but they all agreed on one thing: Black Americans shouldn’t have to wait patiently for a white-run system to give them equality. The movement came out of frustration with how slow civil rights progress was, with poverty and police violence in northern cities, and with white moderates who wouldn’t push for real economic change. There were basically three main types. Cultural Black Power focused on pride and identity — “Black is Beautiful,” natural hair, African dress, and Black studies programs on college campuses. Political Black Power was more radical, like the Black Panther Party (founded in Oakland in 1966 by Huey Newton and Bobby Seale). The Panthers armed themselves to defend their neighborhoods from police, but they also ran free breakfasts, clinics, and schools for Black kids. Religious/separatist Black Power, like the Nation of Islam and Malcolm X, said Black people should build their own separate institutions instead of trying to fit into white society.

These ideas weren’t brand new. Black nationalism went back to Marcus Garvey in the 1920s. The idea of armed self-defense went back even further, to Black people defending themselves during Reconstruction. Building your own churches, schools, and businesses goes all the way back to free Black communities before the Civil War. So Black Power was really a continuation of an old debate in Black history: should we fight to be included in white society, or should we build our own separate world?

2. What did the Chicano and American Indian movements have in common with the Black freedom movement?

The Chicano and Native American movements copied a lot from Black activists. They used the same kinds of tactics — protests, marches, boycotts — and had the same attitude: be proud of who you are and demand real political power. César Chávez and Dolores Huerta led the United Farm Workers (UFW), which organized Mexican American farmworkers through strikes and boycotts (the grape boycott is the most famous one). Younger Chicano activists started groups like the Brown Berets and pushed for things like bilingual education, Chicano studies classes, and an end to police abuse in the Southwest.

The American Indian Movement (AIM), started in 1968, used the same kind of bold protests. Native activists occupied Alcatraz Island for 19 months starting in 1969, and in 1973 they took over Wounded Knee. These protests were meant to grab national attention, just like the civil rights sit-ins had. The Young Lords, a Puerto Rican group modeled on the Black Panthers, did the same kind of work in New York and Chicago neighborhoods. What all these groups had in common with Black Power: they refused to just blend in quietly, they demanded pride in their culture, they used protest instead of waiting for politicians, and they pushed back against both slow-moving liberals and angry conservatives.

27.3 — Key Terms

American Indian Movement (AIM)
Native American civil rights organization founded in 1968 that used direct action — including the occupation of Alcatraz and the 1973 takeover of Wounded Knee — to demand treaty rights and tribal sovereignty.
Black nationalism
Belief that Black Americans should build independent political, economic, and cultural institutions rather than assimilate into white society; rooted in traditions going back to Marcus Garvey.
Black Panther Party
Founded in Oakland in 1966 by Huey Newton and Bobby Seale; organized armed self-defense against police brutality and ran community programs like free breakfasts and health clinics.
Black Power
Slogan popularized by Stokely Carmichael in 1966; emphasized racial pride, self-determination, economic independence, and a rejection of white moderation and gradualism.
Kerner Commission (1968)
Johnson-appointed commission that investigated the urban riots and concluded that the nation was moving toward two societies, one black and one white, separate and unequal, blaming white racism for inner-city conditions.
Occupation of Alcatraz (1969)
Native American activists calling themselves “Indians of All Tribes” occupied the former federal prison island for 19 months to demand the return of unused federal land and raise awareness of Native rights.
Riots of 1967
“Long hot summer” uprisings in cities like Newark and Detroit, sparked by police incidents but fueled by poverty, unemployment, housing discrimination, and brutality in Black urban neighborhoods.
United Farm Workers (UFW)
Farm labor union led by César Chávez and Dolores Huerta that organized primarily Mexican American and Filipino farmworkers through strikes, marches, and the grape boycott.
Young Lords
Puerto Rican activist group modeled on the Black Panthers that organized in New York and Chicago to demand better housing, health care, and education in Latino neighborhoods.

Chapter 28: Liberal Crisis & Conservative Rebirth

APUSH Era 8 (1945–1980)
Identify the Big IdeaWhat were liberalism’s social and political achievements in the 1960s, and how did debates over liberal values contribute to conflict at home and reflect tension abroad?
1. What new roles did the federal government assume under Great Society initiatives, and how did they extend the New Deal tradition?

Lyndon Johnson’s Great Society made the federal government much bigger and more involved in people’s lives than it had ever been before. For the first time, the government took direct responsibility for fighting poverty, giving health care to the elderly and poor, protecting civil rights, funding schools, and reforming immigration. Johnson’s War on Poverty created programs like Head Start (early education for low-income kids), Job Corps (job training), and VISTA (a domestic version of the Peace Corps). The goal wasn’t just to hand out money — it was to break the cycle of poverty through education and community programs. Medicare gave health insurance to everyone over 65. Medicaid covered low-income Americans. These were the first major federal health care programs in U.S. history.

Other Great Society laws included federal money for public schools and college loans, a new Department of Housing and Urban Development, and the National Endowment for the Arts. The Immigration Act of 1965 got rid of the old 1920s quota system that favored Europeans, and opened immigration up to the rest of the world. All of this built on the New Deal’s basic idea — the government should take care of ordinary Americans — but went way further. The New Deal was mostly about fixing the economy. The Great Society was about fairness, equality, and quality of life.

2. What factors accounted for the resurgence of feminism in the 1960s?

A bunch of different things came together to bring feminism back in the 1960s. The biggest spark was Betty Friedan’s book The Feminine Mystique (1963), which talked about “the problem that has no name” — the idea that smart, educated women were miserable being stuck at home as housewives in the suburbs. The book made millions of women realize they weren’t alone and that the problem was bigger than just them. The civil rights movement also played a big role. It gave women a model for activism (protests, equality talk, grassroots organizing), and many women inside the civil rights movement were being treated as second-class by the male leaders, which made them angry enough to start their own movement.

Bigger trends also mattered. More women were going to college, getting married later, and working outside the home. That made unfair laws about pay and jobs more obvious and more frustrating. The birth control pill, which came out in 1960, gave women way more control over their lives — they could plan careers without worrying about unplanned pregnancies. Finally, Kennedy set up a Commission on the Status of Women (led by Eleanor Roosevelt) that put out a big report in 1963 showing how much workplace discrimination there really was. That report helped kick off the legal battles of the decade.

3. What legal victories did women achieve in the 1960s?

Women won some big legal wins in the 1960s. The Equal Pay Act of 1963 said men and women doing the same job had to be paid the same — this was the first federal law ever banning pay discrimination based on sex. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 (Title VII) banned job discrimination based on sex, race, religion, and national origin. Funny story: a segregationist congressman added “sex” to the bill hoping it would be so ridiculous that the whole bill would fail. Instead, it passed, and that one word became one of the most powerful tools feminists had for decades. In 1965, the Supreme Court case Griswold v. Connecticut said married couples had a right to use birth control privately. That decision later set the stage for Roe v. Wade (1973).

In 1966, Betty Friedan and others got fed up with how weakly the government was enforcing the new laws, so they started the National Organization for Women (NOW). NOW pushed the government to actually enforce the rules and fought for new laws like the Equal Rights Amendment. By the end of the 1960s, women had real legal tools to fight discrimination — though full equality was still a long way off.

28.1 — Key Terms

Great Society
LBJ’s sweeping domestic program that expanded the New Deal through the War on Poverty, Medicare, Medicaid, federal aid to education, civil rights enforcement, and immigration reform.
Immigration Act of 1965
Also called the Hart-Celler Act; abolished the national-origins quota system of the 1920s, opening immigration to Asia, Latin America, and Africa and transforming U.S. demographics.
The Feminine Mystique (1963)
Betty Friedan’s bestseller describing the unhappiness of suburban housewives — “the problem that has no name” — that helped launch second-wave feminism.
War on Poverty
LBJ’s set of Great Society anti-poverty programs — including the Office of Economic Opportunity, Head Start, Job Corps, VISTA, and community action — that attacked poverty’s structural causes.
1. How did American military engagement in Vietnam evolve under Johnson?

When Johnson became president after JFK was assassinated in November 1963, there were only about 16,000 American military advisors in South Vietnam. Within five years, that number went up to over 500,000 combat troops — what started as an advisory role turned into a full-on war. The turning point was August 1964, when Johnson said North Vietnamese ships had attacked U.S. Navy destroyers in the Gulf of Tonkin. (The truth was more complicated and Johnson may have lied about what really happened.) Congress responded by passing the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, which gave Johnson the power to use the military in Vietnam without Congress ever actually declaring war. That resolution became the legal excuse for everything that came after.

In 1965, Johnson started Operation Rolling Thunder, a long bombing campaign against North Vietnam, and sent the first big wave of Marines to Da Nang. By 1968, the U.S. was fighting both a regular war against the North Vietnamese Army and a guerrilla war against the Viet Cong, using weapons like napalm and Agent Orange (a chemical that destroyed forests and caused long-term health problems). The big shock was the Tet Offensive in January 1968. The Viet Cong launched surprise attacks on dozens of South Vietnamese cities at once. Technically, the U.S. won militarily, but it was a political disaster — after three years of being told America was winning, people saw on TV that the enemy could attack everywhere. Johnson’s approval tanked, and in March 1968 he announced he wouldn’t run for reelection.

2. How did the war in Vietnam affect the United States domestically in the 1960s?

The war changed everything back home. It ate up money that Johnson wanted to spend on the Great Society, so he had to pick between “guns and butter” (war vs. social programs) — and ended up underfunding both. Inflation went up, the federal budget was a mess, and Americans lost trust in their government because officials kept lying about how the war was going. This was the first war where TV brought combat footage into people’s living rooms, and images of burned villages and dead soldiers turned a lot of people against the war.

The war split the country in half. A huge antiwar movement grew, especially on college campuses. Students protested the draft, burned their draft cards, and held “teach-ins” to discuss the war. The draft was also really unfair — working-class and Black/Latino men got drafted while middle-class white college students could get out of it with deferments. This made people angry about class inequality. Civil rights leaders like Martin Luther King Jr. spoke out against the war, pointing out that America was fighting for “freedom” in Vietnam while still being racist at home. By 1968, the combination of war, urban riots, the assassinations of MLK (April) and Robert Kennedy (June), and violent protests at the Democratic Convention in Chicago made the country feel like it was falling apart.

3. What were the factors behind the student protest movements of the 1960s?

A few things came together to create the student movements of the 1960s. After WWII, the economy boomed and the GI Bill helped way more Americans go to college than ever before. By the mid-1960s, college campuses were packed with students. Many of them were inspired by the civil rights movement — they saw how sit-ins and Freedom Rides worked, and they wanted to use those same tactics for their own causes. The draft and the Vietnam War also made the stakes super personal: if you were a college-age guy, you or your friends could be sent off to fight and die.

On the left, the biggest group was Students for a Democratic Society (SDS), founded in 1960. Their 1962 Port Huron Statement called for “participatory democracy” — basically, ordinary people should have a real voice in government. SDS led a lot of the antiwar protests and the famous Berkeley free speech movement in 1964. On the right, Young Americans for Freedom (YAF), started in 1960 at William F. Buckley’s estate, pushed a conservative vision: free markets, anti-communism, individual freedom, and traditional values. YAF trained a whole generation of conservative leaders — its alumni later helped elect Barry Goldwater, Nixon, and Reagan. So the 1960s student movement wasn’t just a liberal thing; it was young people getting politically active on both sides.

28.2 — Key Terms

Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (1964)
Congressional resolution, passed after reports of attacks on U.S. destroyers off North Vietnam, that gave Johnson broad authority to use military force in Southeast Asia without a declaration of war.
Students for a Democratic Society (SDS)
Leftist student organization founded in 1960; its 1962 Port Huron Statement called for “participatory democracy,” and SDS became central to antiwar and free speech protests.
Young Americans for Freedom (YAF)
Conservative student organization founded in 1960 at William F. Buckley’s estate; articulated a vision of free markets, anti-communism, and traditional values that shaped the modern conservative movement.
1. Why might a Democratic supporter of FDR in the 1940s have decided to vote for Republican Richard Nixon in 1968?

A lot of voters who had loved FDR in the 1940s felt like their party had changed by 1968. FDR’s Democratic coalition was an unlikely mix: Northern factory workers, Southern white segregationists, urban immigrants, and Black Americans. That mix started falling apart in the 1960s. The Civil Rights Act (1964) and Voting Rights Act (1965) pushed away white Southerners, who had been voting Democrat since Reconstruction. Urban riots between 1965 and 1968 scared a lot of white people in the cities and suburbs. Crime was going up, and many voters thought Democrats were being too soft on disorder and lawlessness.

On top of that, the Great Society’s expensive new programs felt to some white voters like the party was focused on helping minorities instead of them. The antiwar protests, hippies, and campus radicals made older voters feel like their country was being wrecked by people who didn’t respect it. Nixon was really smart about playing to all of this. He promised “law and order,” said he spoke for the silent majority (ordinary patriotic Americans who weren’t protesting), and used the Southern Strategy — coded racial talk about “states’ rights” and opposition to school busing — to pull white Southerners into the Republican Party. For a 1940s FDR Democrat, voting Nixon in 1968 could feel like voting to get the country back to normal.

2. In what ways did 1968 represent a turning point in postwar history?

1968 was probably the craziest year in American history since WWII. It ended the optimism that had defined the early 1960s. It started with the Tet Offensive in January, which destroyed whatever was left of Americans’ trust in Johnson’s Vietnam policy. On March 31, Johnson shocked the country by announcing he wouldn’t run for reelection. On April 4, Martin Luther King Jr. was assassinated in Memphis, and riots broke out in more than 100 cities. On June 5, Robert F. Kennedy was assassinated in LA the night he won the California primary. For a lot of people, RFK had been the last hope for uniting antiwar protesters with working-class and minority voters — his death closed that door.

In August, the Democratic National Convention in Chicago turned into a riot. Antiwar protesters clashed with Mayor Daley’s police on live TV. In November, Nixon won a close three-way race (segregationist George Wallace grabbed millions of votes). Even internationally, things were falling apart — the Soviets invaded Czechoslovakia to crush a reform movement. Put it all together, and 1968 broke Americans’ faith that liberalism could fix the country’s problems. It opened the door for conservatives to take over politics, and it set up the political fights — race, war, culture, “law and order” — that still shape America today.

3. How did women’s liberation after 1968 differ from the women’s movement of the early 1960s?

The early-1960s women’s movement (groups like NOW) was mostly focused on legal and economic equality — equal pay, equal hiring, fair laws, and eventually an Equal Rights Amendment. It was mostly run by middle-class professional women, and it worked through things like lawsuits and new laws. The goal was to get women into existing institutions on the same terms as men.

The women’s liberation movement after 1968 was way younger, angrier, and more grassroots. A lot of its leaders had been in the civil rights and antiwar movements, and they were pissed off about the sexism they faced there. Instead of just asking to be let in, they challenged the whole system — marriage, family roles, sex, beauty standards, and how women were shown in the media. They started “consciousness-raising” groups where women met in living rooms to talk about their personal experiences and realized their problems were political, not just personal. Activists protested the Miss America pageant in 1968, opened women’s health clinics, and fought for abortion rights. Where early feminism wanted reform, women’s liberation wanted to change the whole culture.

4. What gains did women achieve in the late 1960s/early 1970s?

Women made huge progress in the late 1960s and early 1970s. Title IX (1972) banned sex discrimination at any school that got federal funding — this opened up college, grad school, and especially sports to women. Roe v. Wade (1973) made abortion legal nationwide, building on the privacy right from Griswold. The Equal Rights Amendment passed Congress in 1972 and was almost added to the Constitution — most states ratified it, but it ended up three states short and failed.

Other big wins: military academies opened to women, women got equal access to credit cards and loans (1974 Equal Credit Opportunity Act), laws about marriage and divorce were updated (including no-fault divorce), and sexual harassment and marital rape were finally recognized as real legal issues. Tons more women started going to college and entering professional careers. Culturally, it became normal for women to plan careers, put off marriage, and live on their own. Not everything worked out — a conservative backlash was building — but this was probably the biggest leap forward for women since they got the right to vote.

28.3 — Key Terms

Nixon’s Southern Strategy
Republican electoral strategy of appealing to white Southern voters through coded racial messaging — “law and order,” states’ rights, opposition to busing — to pull the once-solid Democratic South into the GOP coalition.
Stonewall Riots (1969)
Uprising by LGBTQ patrons against a police raid at the Stonewall Inn in Greenwich Village; widely regarded as the birth of the modern gay rights movement.
Title IX (1972)
Federal law banning sex discrimination in any education program receiving federal funds; dramatically expanded women’s access to schools, professional programs, and athletics.
Women’s Liberation Movement
Younger, more radical wing of second-wave feminism that emerged after 1968; challenged patriarchy in family, sexuality, and culture through consciousness-raising and grassroots activism.
1. How did the Vietnam War end? What was its legacy for the United States?

Nixon’s plan was called Vietnamization — slowly turning combat over to the South Vietnamese while still giving them U.S. air support. But he also expanded the war, secretly bombing Cambodia in 1970 to cut off North Vietnamese supply lines. When news of the Cambodia bombing got out, it caused huge protests. At Kent State in May 1970, National Guardsmen shot and killed four student protesters. After years of talks, Nixon’s advisor Henry Kissinger signed the Paris Peace Accords in January 1973, officially ending U.S. involvement and getting American POWs back. But the war between North and South Vietnam kept going. In April 1975, North Vietnam took Saigon and won the war. The last Americans left by helicopter from the embassy roof — an image of American defeat.

The war left deep scars. Over 58,000 Americans and an estimated 2–3 million Vietnamese died. The war created the “Vietnam syndrome” — Americans and politicians became super reluctant to get involved in foreign wars. Congress passed the War Powers Act (1973) to limit the president’s ability to send troops without congressional approval. The draft ended in 1973. Americans stopped trusting their government, and the military’s reputation took decades to recover. Veterans came home to a divided country, often didn’t get proper care for injuries like Agent Orange exposure or PTSD, and many felt ignored or blamed for a war they hadn’t started.

2. What was the legacy of the Watergate scandal?

The Watergate scandal started in June 1972 when five burglars connected to Nixon’s reelection campaign got caught breaking into Democratic Party headquarters at the Watergate complex in Washington, D.C. Reporters at the Washington Post (Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein), Senate hearings, and a special prosecutor slowly uncovered that the break-in was part of a much bigger pattern of illegal stuff the Nixon White House was doing — spying on political enemies, dirty tricks, and covering it up. Then it came out that Nixon had secretly recorded everything in the Oval Office. The Supreme Court ruled in United States v. Nixon (1974) that he had to hand over the tapes. The tapes showed Nixon himself had organized the cover-up. Facing impeachment, Nixon resigned on August 9, 1974 — the only president ever to resign. His VP Gerald Ford took over and then pardoned Nixon, which a lot of people were furious about.

Watergate had huge long-term effects. It made Americans deeply distrust their government — a distrust that had already started with Vietnam. Congress passed reforms: new campaign finance laws, stronger ethics rules, an Independent Counsel law, and more congressional oversight of the president. It also established a big principle: no president is above the law. The tapes case showed that a president can’t hide behind “executive privilege” to break the rules. Politically, Watergate hurt Republicans in the short term (Democrats won big in 1974 and 1976), but long-term, it added to the cynical, suspicious political culture we still live with today.

28.4 — Key Terms

Silent Majority
Phrase Nixon used to describe ordinary, patriotic, middle-class Americans who he claimed supported the Vietnam War and rejected the counterculture and antiwar protests; central to his 1968 and 1972 coalitions.
Watergate Scandal (1972–73)
Political scandal that began with a break-in at the Democratic National Committee headquarters; widening revelations of abuses of power and a cover-up forced Nixon to resign in August 1974.

Chapter 29: The Search for Order in an Era of Limits

APUSH Era 8/9 (1970s)
Identify the Big IdeaHow did the legacy of social changes — such as shifting gender roles, civil rights, and challenges to the family — in the 1960s continue to reverberate in the 1970s, leading to both new opportunities and political disagreement?
1. What major factors led to the birth of the environmental movement in the 1970s? What was the movement able to achieve?

The environmental movement came from a mix of scientific warnings, visible disasters, and leftover activist energy from the 1960s. The biggest single trigger was Rachel Carson’s book Silent Spring (1962). It showed how pesticides like DDT were poisoning wildlife, killing birds, and making people sick. After the book, Americans started thinking differently about chemicals, companies, and nature. A bunch of visible disasters also shook people up: in 1969 there was a massive oil spill off Santa Barbara, and the Cuyahoga River in Cleveland was so polluted it caught on fire. Smog was blocking out the sun in big cities. Activists connected all of this to modern consumer culture and to the industries driving it.

The movement got a ton done, mostly under Nixon. The first Earth Day happened in April 1970 and drew about 20 million people. That same year, Nixon signed the Clean Air Act and created the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). Then came the Clean Water Act (1972), Endangered Species Act (1973), and laws protecting drinking water and regulating toxic waste. DDT was banned in 1972. After the Three Mile Island nuclear accident in 1979, nuclear power basically stopped growing in the U.S. for decades. The movement didn’t fix everything, but it made environmental protection a permanent part of federal law.

2. Why did the US enter an energy crisis in the 1970s?

The energy crisis was both a world problem and an American one. By the 1970s, the U.S. was buying a lot of its oil from the Middle East, which made us depend on countries that weren’t always friendly. The Arab-led group OPEC (Organization of Petroleum Exporting Countries) realized they could use oil as a weapon. In October 1973, during the Yom Kippur War between Israel and its neighbors, OPEC put an oil embargo on the U.S. and other countries supporting Israel. Oil prices shot up 4x. Gas stations ran out of fuel, there were huge lines at the pump, and people could only buy gas on odd or even days based on their license plate. A second oil shock hit in 1979 after the Iranian Revolution cut off Iran’s oil exports.

At home, U.S. oil production had actually peaked in 1970 and was dropping. The country couldn’t keep up with its own demand anymore. Everything about American life — big cars, cheap gas, suburbs spread out far from work — assumed cheap oil. When prices exploded, the economy broke. You had high inflation AND slow growth at the same time, which economists called stagflation. This was really weird, because usually those two things don’t happen together, so normal economic tools didn’t work. Presidents Nixon, Ford, and Carter all tried stuff: price controls, asking people to conserve, making the Department of Energy, researching renewable energy. Carter called it “the moral equivalent of war.” But the crisis shattered the postwar feeling that America could have whatever it wanted.

3. What major developments shaped the American economy in the 1970s and contributed to its transformation?

A few major things changed the American economy in the 1970s. First was deindustrialization: factories in the Northeast and Midwest (steel, autos, textiles) closed down. Foreign competition from Germany and Japan was beating American companies, factories were old, labor and energy costs were up, and corporations weren’t reinvesting. Cities like Pittsburgh, Cleveland, Detroit, and Buffalo — now called the Rust Belt — lost huge numbers of jobs and people. Meanwhile, the Sun Belt (the South and West) grew with new industries: defense contracting, oil and gas, tourism, big agriculture, and by the late 1970s, computers and finance.

Second, stagflation broke the government’s economic tools and shook people’s faith in the way the economy had been run since the New Deal. Third, people started revolting against taxes — California’s Proposition 13 (1978) capped property taxes and showed a bigger shift: Americans were fed up with big government. Fourth, tons more women entered the workforce, changing families and the economy. Fifth, globalization took off, putting American workers in tougher competition with workers around the world. All of these changes chipped away at people’s trust in liberal economic policies and set the stage for Reagan’s conservative revolution.

29.1 — Key Terms

Deindustrialization
Decline of manufacturing in the 1970s as factories closed in the Northeast and Midwest due to foreign competition, aging plants, rising costs, and corporate disinvestment.
Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)
Federal agency created by Nixon in 1970 to enforce new environmental laws like the Clean Air and Clean Water Acts and regulate pollution.
OPEC
Organization of Petroleum Exporting Countries; dominated by Arab states, its 1973 oil embargo caused a fourfold jump in oil prices and triggered the U.S. energy crisis.
California’s Proposition 13 (1978)
Ballot initiative that capped California property taxes; launched the nationwide tax revolt and helped build the political base for the conservative Reagan revolution.
Rachel Carson’s Silent Spring (1962)
Bestselling book that exposed the ecological damage caused by pesticides like DDT; widely credited with launching the modern environmental movement.
Rust Belt
Region of the Northeast and Midwest hit hardest by deindustrialization; cities like Detroit, Pittsburgh, and Cleveland lost huge shares of their manufacturing jobs and population.
Three Mile Island
1979 partial nuclear meltdown at a Pennsylvania power plant; sharply turned public opinion against nuclear power and stalled construction of new plants for decades.
1. How did affirmative action attempt to address the legacy of discrimination, and why did it become so controversial?

Affirmative action was a set of policies meant to open up opportunities for groups — especially Black Americans and women — who had been shut out of schools, jobs, and government contracts for most of American history. It started with a 1961 Kennedy executive order and expanded under LBJ in 1965. In the late 1960s and 1970s, colleges, companies, and government agencies started considering race and gender when hiring or admitting people, setting goals for how many minorities to hire, and sometimes using quotas. The idea was simple: after centuries of slavery, Jim Crow, and discrimination against women, just saying “everyone’s equal now” wasn’t enough. You had to actively work to undo generations of unfairness.

But affirmative action got controversial fast. Critics called it “reverse discrimination” — they said it treated people as members of groups instead of individuals, and that it unfairly punished white men who hadn’t done anything wrong personally. Allan Bakke was a white guy who got rejected twice from UC Davis Medical School. When he found out the school set aside 16 spots out of 100 for minority applicants, he sued. In Bakke v. University of California (1978), the Supreme Court split the difference: racial quotas were unconstitutional, but schools could still consider race as one factor among many. The ruling didn’t settle the issue — it made affirmative action a political fight that’s still going on today.

2. Why did the Equal Rights Amendment initially gain momentum, and why did it ultimately fail?

The Equal Rights Amendment (ERA) was a proposed constitutional amendment that said the government couldn’t deny equal rights based on sex. It had first been proposed by Alice Paul way back in 1923. Finally, in 1972, Congress passed it by huge margins from both parties and sent it to the states for ratification. It looked like it was cruising to success: within a year, 30 of the 38 states needed had approved it. Polls showed most Americans, including conservative women, were in favor.

Then came the backlash, led by a conservative Catholic woman named Phyllis Schlafly. Her “STOP ERA” campaign (Stop Taking Our Privileges) organized traditionalist women who argued the ERA would: end laws protecting women, force women to be drafted into the military, legalize unisex bathrooms, take away alimony and custody rights from mothers, and destroy the family. Schlafly basically connected the ERA to every 1970s cultural fear — feminism, abortion, gay rights, changing families. By 1977, momentum stalled three states short of the 38 needed. Congress extended the deadline to 1982, but no more states ratified, and five actually took back their approval. The ERA’s defeat was the first big win for the rising Religious Right and showed that grassroots conservatism was a real political force.

29.2 — Key Terms

Affirmative Action
Policies that considered race and gender in hiring, admissions, and contracting to remedy historical discrimination; began with executive orders under Kennedy and Johnson.
Bakke v. University of California (1978)
Supreme Court ruling that struck down rigid racial quotas in university admissions as unconstitutional but allowed race to be considered as one factor among many.
Equal Rights Amendment
Proposed constitutional amendment banning sex-based discrimination; passed Congress in 1972 but fell three states short of ratification after a backlash led by Phyllis Schlafly.
Roe v. Wade (1973)
Supreme Court decision establishing a constitutional right to abortion based on the right to privacy; immediately became one of the most divisive rulings in U.S. history.
1. How did deindustrialization reshape work, income, and daily life for American families after 1970?

Deindustrialization ended the post-WWII era of rising wages and stable factory jobs that had let one man’s income support a whole middle-class family. When steel mills, auto plants, and textile factories closed across the Rust Belt, millions of well-paying union jobs disappeared. A lot of those workers ended up in lower-paying service jobs like retail and food service, with no benefits, no job security, and no unions. Wages for men basically stopped growing in the mid-1970s and didn’t really go back up for decades.

Families had to adapt to keep the same standard of living. First, married women started working in huge numbers — by the end of the 1970s, more than half of married moms were working outside the home. Two-income families became normal, which changed everything about how households ran. Second, Americans started racking up tons of credit card debt, which spread fast in the 1970s and 1980s. Life felt more stressed and less stable. Divorce rates went up. Young people put off getting married and having kids. Working-class communities, especially white ones, felt like they were falling behind. This mix of money anxiety and cultural change is the backdrop for why the country swung conservative in the 1980s.

Chapter 30: Conservative America Ascendant (1973–1991)

APUSH Era 9 (1980–present)
Identify the Big IdeaWhat factors made the rise of the New Right possible, and what ideas about freedom and citizenship did conservatives articulate in the 1980s?
1. Which were the key groups of the new Republican coalition? Were their goals complementary or contradictory?

Reagan’s 1980 coalition brought together a bunch of different groups that didn’t usually agree. First were economic conservatives and libertarians, who wanted lower taxes, fewer regulations, smaller government, and free markets. Second were Cold War hawks, who thought the U.S. had gotten too soft in the 1970s and wanted to build up the military and confront the Soviet Union hard. Third were religious and social conservatives — especially the Religious Right, led by Jerry Falwell’s Moral Majority (1979). They opposed abortion, the ERA, gay rights, and the sexual revolution, and wanted traditional family values back. Fourth were neoconservatives, former liberals who had gotten fed up with the Great Society and the antiwar movement. Finally, there were white Southerners and working-class “Reagan Democrats” who felt abandoned by the Democratic Party.

Their goals often worked together in practice but clashed in theory. Libertarians wanted government out of the economy AND out of people’s private lives — but religious conservatives wanted government to enforce traditional morality. Economic conservatives wanted to cut spending, but defense hawks wanted to spend way more on the military. What held them all together was a shared hatred of the liberal establishment, the Soviet Union, and the chaos they saw in the 60s and 70s. Reagan was a really skilled communicator who could paper over the contradictions with a message of American renewal — but those internal tensions would eventually resurface inside the Republican Party.

2. In what ways was the New Right “reactive,” responding to liberalism, and in what ways was it “proactive,” asserting its own agenda?

The New Right was reactive because a lot of its energy came from being AGAINST stuff. Conservatives were against Great Society welfare programs, high taxes, affirmative action, school busing, and federal regulations. They were against the hippie counterculture, antiwar protesters, changing gender roles, and Supreme Court decisions on school prayer, abortion, and criminal rights. They were against détente with the Soviets, which they thought was too soft. Groups like STOP ERA, antibusing groups, and antiabortion groups were basically organized around saying NO to things.

But the New Right was also proactive — it had its own positive vision. Think tanks like the Heritage Foundation (1973) and American Enterprise Institute built up a conservative intellectual base. Activists came up with new political tools: direct mail fundraising (Richard Viguerie was a pioneer of this), talk radio, Christian TV networks, political action committees (PACs), and get-out-the-vote drives at evangelical churches. Conservatives pushed a positive agenda: cut taxes (supply-side economics), personal responsibility, traditional family values, a strong military, and freedom from big government. Barry Goldwater’s book The Conscience of a Conservative (1960) had laid out this vision years earlier. By the late 1970s, conservatism wasn’t just complaining about liberalism anymore — it was its own complete worldview.

1. Why are the 1980s seen as the beginning of a conservative age?

The 1980s are considered the start of a conservative era because Reagan’s elections in 1980 and 1984 totally shifted the direction of American politics, economics, and culture. In 1980, Reagan beat incumbent Jimmy Carter in a landslide, winning 44 states. In 1984, he beat Walter Mondale by winning 49 states — one of the biggest wins in U.S. election history. Then his VP George H. W. Bush won a third Republican term in 1988. Reagan’s core ideas — lower taxes, less regulation, free markets, distrust of big government — became the mainstream thinking in American politics for decades. Even Democrats adopted them: Bill Clinton in the 1990s said “the era of big government is over.”

It wasn’t just about winning elections. Reagan put conservative judges on the Supreme Court (Sandra Day O’Connor, Antonin Scalia, Anthony Kennedy) and appointed over 350 federal judges who would shape law for decades. He weakened labor unions by firing striking air traffic controllers in 1981. He cut tax rates on the rich dramatically and deregulated business. Culture turned more patriotic and traditional in movies and TV. And when the Soviet Union collapsed by 1991, it felt like proof that conservative ideas had won.

2. What were the key elements of Reagan’s domestic policy? How did that policy reflect conservative ideology?

Reagan’s domestic policy had four main parts: tax cuts, deregulation, cuts to social programs, and more military spending. The Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 cut income tax rates by 25% across the board and dropped the top rate from 70% to 50% (later to 28% in 1986). He cut taxes on businesses and capital gains too. He rolled back federal regulations on banking, airlines, trucking, phones, and the environment. He cut spending on things like food stamps, welfare, public housing, job training, Medicaid, and college aid — though he didn’t touch Social Security or Medicare because those were too popular. Meanwhile, defense spending exploded to about $300 billion a year by the mid-1980s.

These policies matched conservative beliefs in a few ways. Reagan’s famous line was “Government is not the solution to our problem; government IS the problem.” Conservatives believed that if you cut taxes and regulations, businesses would invest more and create jobs. They thought welfare programs made people dependent instead of fixing poverty. They trusted the free market more than government officials to make decisions. And they believed being patriotic meant having a big military and fighting communism. All together, these policies were the biggest break from New Deal/Great Society liberalism since FDR.

3. To what extent did Reaganomics achieve its goals?

Reaganomics had mixed results depending on how you look at it. Some things worked. Inflation, which had been over 10% under Carter, dropped way down. (Mostly that was because of tough Federal Reserve policy under Paul Volcker, but Reagan supported it.) After a sharp recession in 1981–82, the economy grew steadily from late 1982 through 1990, creating millions of jobs. Income tax rates got way lower, the stock market soared, and Americans felt more confident again.

But Reaganomics also failed at some big things. Reagan promised that tax cuts would grow the economy enough to pay for themselves — they didn’t. Federal deficits exploded. The national debt almost tripled, from about $900 billion in 1980 to $2.7 trillion in 1989. The U.S. went from being the world’s biggest lender to the world’s biggest borrower. Income inequality got way worse — the rich got way richer while working-class Americans saw their wages stay flat or go down. Poverty rates went up during the recession and never fully recovered. Deregulation caused the savings-and-loan crisis, which cost taxpayers hundreds of billions of dollars. Supply-side economics became a core Republican belief, but the evidence that tax cuts pay for themselves was always shaky.

AIDS Crisis (1980s)
Emergence of the HIV/AIDS epidemic, which disproportionately killed gay men, hemophiliacs, and drug users; the Reagan administration was criticized for its slow response, and activist groups like ACT UP demanded research and treatment.
“Reagan Democrats”
White, often working-class, traditionally Democratic voters (many from the Rust Belt and South) who crossed over to vote for Reagan in 1980 and 1984 because of concerns about taxes, culture, and national strength.
Supply-Side Economics (“Reaganomics”)
Economic theory that deep cuts in income and business taxes would stimulate investment, growth, and tax revenue; became the framework of Reagan’s domestic economic policy.
1. How did Reagan’s approach to the Soviet Union change between 1981 and 1989?

Reagan’s Soviet policy changed a lot over his two terms. In his first term (1981–1985), he was super aggressive. He called the USSR the “evil empire” in 1983, launched a huge military buildup, and proposed the Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI) — nicknamed “Star Wars” — a plan to shoot down Soviet missiles from space. He also backed anti-communist rebels around the world (the Contras in Nicaragua, the mujahideen in Afghanistan), which became known as the Reagan Doctrine. Tensions got as bad as they’d been since the Cuban Missile Crisis, and some Soviet officials genuinely thought the U.S. might launch a first strike.

In his second term (1985–1989), Reagan completely flipped and started negotiating. The big reason was Mikhail Gorbachev becoming Soviet leader in 1985. Gorbachev started two reforms: perestroika (restructuring the economy) and glasnost (being more open politically). He was also willing to actually talk seriously with Reagan. The two leaders met at four big summits — Geneva (1985), Reykjavík (1986), Washington (1987), and Moscow (1988). In 1987 they signed the INF Treaty, which got rid of a whole class of nuclear missiles in Europe — the first real nuclear disarmament deal of the Cold War. In 1987, Reagan stood at the Brandenburg Gate and famously said, “Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall!” By the time Reagan left office in January 1989, U.S.-Soviet relations had gone from almost-war to real partnership. The Berlin Wall fell later that year.

2. What factors led to the end of the Cold War?

The Cold War ended because of several things happening at once. The biggest reason was that the Soviet Union was falling apart from the inside. Their centrally planned economy had basically stopped growing by the 1970s, and they were way behind the West in technology, consumer goods, and living standards. Their war in Afghanistan (1979–89) drained money and lives. Corruption and cynicism in the government made people lose faith in communism. When Gorbachev tried to reform the system to save it, his reforms actually opened the door for people to demand more changes, and the whole system started crumbling.

American policy mattered too. Reagan’s big military buildup and SDI put pressure on a Soviet economy that was already stretched thin — the Soviets figured out they couldn’t keep up. The Reagan Doctrine made Soviet foreign interventions more costly. And in his second term, Reagan was willing to treat Gorbachev as a real partner, which helped Gorbachev take bold risks. Meanwhile, in Eastern Europe, people in Poland (the Solidarity movement), Hungary, Czechoslovakia, and East Germany rose up once they realized the Soviets wouldn’t send tanks this time. In 1989, the Berlin Wall fell. In 1990, Germany was reunited. In December 1991, the Soviet Union officially broke apart. It wasn’t any single cause — it was Soviet collapse, American pressure, and people power all hitting at once.

3. What was Reagan’s legacy?

Reagan’s legacy is huge and people still argue about it. His fans credit him with restoring American confidence after the 1970s, winning the Cold War without a shot fired, rebuilding the military, kicking off a long economic boom, cutting top tax rates, and turning conservatism from a fringe movement into the dominant political force for decades. He changed the Republican Party, stacked the federal courts, and set the terms of American political debate for a whole generation. His communication skills and optimism made him one of the most personally popular presidents of the 20th century.

But critics point to big failures. The national debt tripled under Reagan, and the U.S. went from lender to borrower. Income inequality got way worse. Poverty and homelessness went up. The administration barely responded to the AIDS crisis, and thousands of people died as a result. Deregulation led to the savings-and-loan financial collapse. The Iran-Contra affair — where officials secretly sold weapons to Iran and sent the money to right-wing rebels in Nicaragua, breaking a congressional ban — almost got Reagan impeached. Critics also say his talk about “welfare queens” and “states’ rights” had racial dog whistles that made politics more divided. So Reagan’s legacy is both a conservative political revolution AND a lot of economic and social problems that the country is still dealing with today.

4. Why did the US intervene in conflicts between Iraq and Iran, and between Iraq and Kuwait? What were the American goals in each case?

The U.S. got involved in both conflicts to protect its interests in the Persian Gulf — mostly access to oil and regional stability. In the Iran-Iraq War (1980–88), the U.S. mostly backed Saddam Hussein’s Iraq against Iran. Iran had taken 52 Americans hostage in 1979 and called the U.S. the “Great Satan,” so Iraq seemed like the lesser evil. The U.S. gave Iraq intelligence, loans, and technology, and by the end of the war was even protecting Kuwaiti oil tankers from Iranian attacks. The goal wasn’t really for Iraq to win — it was to keep Iran from taking over the region and threatening oil supplies. Weirdly, at the same time, the secret Iran-Contra affair showed the Reagan administration was ALSO selling weapons to Iran. So U.S. policy was secretly contradictory.

In August 1990, Saddam Hussein invaded Kuwait, a small, oil-rich country that was a U.S. ally. President George H. W. Bush put together a huge international coalition backed by the UN. The Persian Gulf War kicked off in early 1991 with a massive air campaign, followed by a 100-hour ground war (Operation Desert Storm) that pushed Iraqi forces out of Kuwait. American goals: get Kuwait back, warn Iraq not to attack Saudi Arabia next, keep oil flowing, and show that in a post-Cold War world, countries can’t just invade each other. Bush deliberately didn’t overthrow Saddam because he worried it would destabilize the region. The war was a big military win, briefly broke the “Vietnam syndrome,” and showed the U.S. was now the world’s only superpower.
