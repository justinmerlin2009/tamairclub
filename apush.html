Chapter 23: The Great Depression & the New Deal

APUSH Era 7: Early 20th Century (1898–1945)
1. Which groups were particularly hard-hit during the depression?

The working and lower class were hit the hardest by the Depression. Unemployment skyrocketed to 25%, and income dropped sharply in both cities and rural farming communities. People were so desperate that they traded labor just for food because they couldn’t afford basic necessities. Married women were actually banned from working in schools and other jobs, even though female employment overall technically increased during this period.

African Americans faced the worst unemployment rates of any group. Geographically, the North was impacted more than the South because of its heavy dependence on manufacturing. In the South, agricultural wages plummeted, and regions that relied on resource extraction industries suffered enormously as well.

2. What institutions or programs existed to help people during the early years of the depression?

In the early years of the Depression, the main sources of relief were private and local. Churches and synagogues offered charity, but by 1931 they were completely overwhelmed by the sheer scale of need. Soup kitchens, bread lines, and neighborhood mutual aid networks existed, but none of these were permanent solutions to the crisis. The government safety net was almost nonexistent — only 8 states provided any unemployment insurance, and there was no real support system for the elderly at all.

3. How did President Hoover respond to the economic emergency? Why did he choose the course of action that he did?

Hoover’s response to the Depression was shaped by his deep belief that economic outcomes were the product of individual character — he thought success came to those who deserved it, and that through voluntary action, business could regulate itself. Because of this philosophy, he was reluctant to break with the idea of limited government. He cut federal taxes to try to boost private spending and corporate investment, and called on state and local governments to create jobs by investing in public projects.

His biggest program was the Reconstruction Finance Corporation (RFC), which provided federal loans to railroads, banks, and businesses. But the RFC was not aggressive enough and was too cautious with the money it lent out, so it largely failed. Hoover kept insisting that recovery was “just around the corner,” but the economy only got worse, and people grew increasingly frustrated with his refusal to take bolder action.

1. What specific new roles did the American government take up as a result of the legislation passed during the first hundred days?

During the First Hundred Days, FDR’s presidency and court session enacted 15 bills focused on four major issues: bank failures, agricultural overproduction, business slump, and unemployment. In terms of banking reform, FDR declared a national “bank holiday” on March 6, 1933, closing all banks for seven days and calling Congress to meet to create bank reform legislation. The Emergency Banking Act permitted banks to reopen if they had sufficient cash reserves, and the Glass-Steagall Act separated commercial banking from investment banking and created the FDIC, which insured deposits up to $2,500 and prohibited banks from making risky investments. Congress also created new agencies to regulate the stock market and set rules for margin transactions.

For agriculture and manufacturing, the Agricultural Adjustment Act (AAA) began direct government regulation of the farm economy by providing cash subsidies to farmers who cut production, aiming to raise prices and address overproduction. The National Recovery Administration (NRA) set up separate self-governing associations in 600 industries where each industry regulated itself through codes of prices and quotas. For unemployment relief, the Federal Emergency Relief Administration provided funds for state programs, while the Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC) mobilized 3 million men for reforestation and conservation work, paying them $30 a month and requiring them to send $25 back to their families. The Federal Housing Administration (FHA) reformed the mortgage system and set the stage for expanding home ownership after WWII.

2. What was the philosophy behind the New Deal programs meant to reduce unemployment?

The philosophy behind the New Deal’s unemployment programs was Relief, Recovery, Reform. Roosevelt was the first president to focus so heavily on unemployment, genuinely believing it was the government’s duty to take care of people’s wellbeing and jobs. He created programs like the CCC to directly employ the jobless and build public infrastructure like bridges, roads, and trails — putting the federal government in the role of employer and economic stabilizer for the first time in American history.

3. How did critics on the right and left represent different kinds of challenges to Roosevelt and the New Deal?

Critics on the left believed that the New Deal did not go far enough. They wanted national health care and wanted the government to do more for ordinary Americans, including domestic workers and farm laborers who were excluded from many of the programs. They felt FDR was too willing to compromise with business interests and that the benefits of New Deal programs were unequally distributed toward the wealthy.

Critics on the right, mainly conservatives, believed FDR was a traitor to people of his wealthy social class and that his policies amounted to reckless spending rooted in socialist beliefs. They prioritized limited government and individual freedom, and they saw the New Deal as a dangerous intrusion into the personal and financial lives of citizens. These two groups represented fundamentally different visions — the left wanted more government action, while the right wanted less.

1. How did the Second New Deal differ from the first?

The First New Deal focused mainly on economic reform — stabilizing banks, industry, and agriculture to stop the bleeding of the Depression. The Second New Deal, by contrast, shifted toward social justice. It included the Wagner Act of 1935, which guaranteed workers the right of collective bargaining and set rules to protect unions and organizers, while also creating a board to enforce employee rights. The Social Security Act of 1935 established old-age pensions for people over 65 funded through payroll taxes, as well as an unemployment insurance system — though it excluded African Americans, domestic workers, and agricultural workers. The Works Progress Administration (WPA) employed millions building bridges, roads, public buildings, parks, and airports, representing a much more direct government role in providing jobs.

2. How did FDR redefine liberalism?

FDR’s New Deal liberalism held individual liberty as the foundation of a democratic society but fundamentally redefined what that meant. Instead of the old classical liberalism that prioritized limited government and keeping the state out of people’s lives, FDR argued that the government had a duty to ensure the welfare of its citizens. This was a major ideological shift toward social welfare liberalism — the idea that government should actively protect individuals from economic hardship through programs like Social Security, labor protections, and unemployment insurance. It created a new understanding of individual rights that included the right to economic security and a decent standard of living.

1. What was the impact of the New Deal on organized labor, women, and racial and ethnic minorities?

For labor, unions grew through “industrial unionism,” where workers in an entire industry joined a single union. Success was limited at first, but union power grew significantly thanks to the Wagner Act’s protections. For women, policymakers mostly saw the Depression as a crisis of male “breadwinners,” so women struggled for attention. Eleanor Roosevelt was a major advocate for women’s rights, but the New Deal Recovery Act set a lower minimum wage for women than for men, the CCC excluded all women, and 85% of American men believed women shouldn’t work at all.

Many Black Americans shifted their support to FDR and the Democrats during this era. About 18% had jobs through the WPA, and FDR appointed many African Americans to federal office through an informal “Black Cabinet.” However, FDR refused to support anti-lynching legislation because he needed white Southern Democratic support, and the CCC was segregated. For Native Americans, New Deal policies reversed the earlier Dawes Act by promoting Indian self-government through formal constitutions and democratically elected tribal councils, giving tribes greater religious freedom.

Mexican Americans faced harsh treatment — both Hoover and FDR promoted “repatriation,” which was really deportation to Mexico. This was unconstitutional because many of those deported were actually U.S. citizens, though some Mexican Americans did benefit from CCC jobs. Chinese Americans were classified as “aliens ineligible for citizenship” and excluded from most federal programs. Filipino Americans were also classified as aliens and immigration from the Philippines was severely restricted.

2. What aspects of the New Deal inspired ordinary Americans? What stymied their ambitions?

The New Deal inspired ordinary Americans in several ways. Roosevelt appointed some women and African Americans to his administration, offering hope and representation to groups that had been largely ignored by the federal government. New Deal policies promoted Native American self-government and gave greater religious freedom to Native peoples. The New Deal also encouraged artists to paint public murals, and the WPA Federal Art Project gave 15,000 artists jobs. FDR was also committed to preserving natural resources and using them through scientific management.

What stymied their ambitions was the political reality of the Democratic coalition. FDR wouldn’t go far enough on anti-lynching laws because he needed the support of Southern white Democrats to pass his other legislation. Additionally, farmers had stripped the Great Plains of native vegetation and destroyed the ecology, creating the Dust Bowl, though federal agencies did step in to teach farmers soil preservation practices.

3. What was the New Deal’s long-term legacy?

The New Deal’s most important legacy was that it restored hope and created a powerful welfare state that took responsibility for its citizens’ wellbeing. It fundamentally transformed the relationship between the federal government and the American people. By the 1930s, people began paying taxes to the Social Security Administration, and one-third of the population received government-assisted programs like old-age pensions and unemployment compensation. The government also regulated the stock market, reformed the Federal Reserve system, and subjected business to federal regulation for the first time.

Government involvement in social life became the norm going forward through the 20th century, and conservation programs improved the environment. Politically, the New Deal completely transformed the landscape — the Democrats became a diverse coalition of Southern and Central European immigrants, African Americans, ethnic groups, city dwellers, organized labor, and the middle class, creating a majority that dominated American politics for decades.

Chapter 24: The World at War, 1937–1945

APUSH Unit 6
1. What were the sources of American political isolationism?

American isolationism had been building in Congress throughout the 1920s and was rooted in deep disillusionment with World War I. Many Americans felt the U.S. had been drawn into a European conflict that didn’t serve American interests, and some believed that war profiteers — bankers and arms manufacturers — had maneuvered the nation into WWI for their own financial gain. Congress passed neutrality legislation reflecting this sentiment, imposing embargoes on trade with countries at war and declaring that American citizens traveled on the ships of belligerent nations at their own risk, eventually adopting a “Cash & Carry” provision.

The America First Committee (AFC), with 800,000 members, formed specifically to keep America out of the war. Geographic isolation also played a role — the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans provided a natural buffer that reinforced the belief that European and Asian conflicts were not America’s problem. Meanwhile, in the 1930s, the Japanese military was making advances in China and upsetting the balance of power in the Pacific. Even after the Rape of Nanjing in 1937, the U.S. still refused to interfere. It wasn’t until Japan occupied French Indochina that Roosevelt retaliated with trade restrictions and froze Japanese assets.

2. How did FDR sell the war to the American public?

FDR used radio addresses to frame the conflict as a threat to democracy and freedom everywhere, not just in Europe. He characterized the Axis powers as a direct threat to American values and the Western Hemisphere, and he gradually moved the country from neutrality to active support of the Allies, preparing public opinion step by step. His biggest move was pushing the Lend-Lease Act of 1941, which lent arms and other equipment to any country whose defense was considered vital to the security of the United States — eventually extending aid to the Soviet Union after it was invaded by Germany. He framed it as “lending a garden hose to a neighbor whose house is on fire,” which made military aid sound like common sense rather than a step toward war.

3. Why did the US wait until 1941 to enter WWII?

The main reason was that isolationist sentiment was incredibly strong — the majority of Americans opposed direct involvement through the late 1930s. Congress had passed neutrality legislation specifically designed to keep the U.S. out of foreign wars, and FDR could not openly push for entry into the conflict without public support, so he had to move cautiously and gradually. What finally ended the debate was the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941. The direct attack on U.S. soil instantly united the country, and Congress declared war the very next day.

1. Explain the economic boom the US experienced in the first two years of the war, including the role the federal government played.

The war produced an incredible economic boom. Between 1940 and 1945, annual GDP doubled, after-tax profits of businesses doubled, and farm output increased by a third. The federal government became the nation’s largest customer, and by 1942, two-thirds of government spending was going toward the war effort. The government raised taxes and borrowed money from the wealthy, and about 3.8 million people were employed directly by the government. The federal government awarded defense contracts and allocated scarce resources like rubber, copper, and oil to war use, and businesses switched to military production only.

Unemployment virtually disappeared as factories ran around the clock. The government financed new factories and shipyards that applied mass production techniques to building navy ships and military equipment. By 1945, the largest 100 companies produced 70% of the nation’s industrial output, creating what became known as the military-industrial complex. Wages rose, personal income increased, and the economic depression that had gripped the nation for a decade finally came to an end.

2. To what extent did FDR uphold capitalism during the war?

FDR largely upheld capitalism during the war — the government partnered with private industry rather than replacing it, and corporations received lucrative government contracts to produce war materials. However, the government exercised significant control over the economy. The government directed what factories produced, rationing limited consumer goods, and price controls prevented inflation. FDR exercised broad executive authority over all aspects of the war effort, leading to what historians call the “imperial presidency” — the far-reaching use of executive power that would characterize the 20th century. So it was really a managed form of capitalism: private ownership remained, but the government steered production priorities and set strict limits on how the market could operate.

3. What impact did war mobilization have on women, racial minorities, and organized labor?

Millions of women entered the workforce in factories and shipyards, symbolized by “Rosie the Riveter” — the poster figure urging women to take war jobs whose image became a symbol of wartime courage and patriotism. Over 140,000 women served in military duty as nurses, pilots, clerical workers, communications specialists, and healthcare providers. However, women were still paid less than men and were expected to return home after the war.

For racial minorities, Black labor union leader A. Philip Randolph called for a march on Washington because of discrimination against Black aircraft workers, and FDR responded with Executive Order 8802, which prohibited discrimination in defense industries or government based on race, color, creed, or national origin and established the Fair Employment Practices Commission — though discrimination still persisted. African Americans were segregated and assigned menial duties in the military, but Native Americans and Mexicans were not segregated. Native Americans served as code talkers, and the Bracero Program brought thousands of Mexican laborers to the U.S. for low wages to support the war effort. Union membership grew as workers were needed in war industries, though strikes were discouraged.

The GI Bill of Rights (1944) provided education, job training, medical care, pensions, and mortgage loans to military veterans — though benefits were often unequally distributed to minorities.

1. How did the war impact everyday Americans?

Rationing became a fact of daily life during the war — the government rationed sugar, meat, gasoline, rubber, and other goods to ensure the military had enough supplies. Americans planted “victory gardens” and bought war bonds to support the effort. Wages rose and unemployment dropped, bringing real prosperity after the Depression, but consumer goods were scarce since factories were focused on military production. Families were disrupted as millions of men went overseas, and women and teenagers took on new responsibilities at home and in the workplace. Propaganda campaigns promoted patriotism and sacrifice, shaping American culture completely around the war effort.

2. What effects did wartime migration have on the United States?

Over 15 million Americans moved across cities and states during the war. Millions relocated to cities with defense plants, especially on the West Coast and in the South, fueling rapid urbanization. African Americans participated in a “Second Great Migration” from the rural South to northern and western cities seeking factory jobs, transforming the demographics of cities like Detroit, Chicago, and Los Angeles. This massive wartime migration also opened up opportunities for new, vibrant gay communities in these cities.

However, the migration also led to overcrowding, housing shortages, and racial tensions. In Los Angeles, Hispanic teens formed youth gangs and wore zoot suits to underline their rejection of middle-class values. Adults identified the suits with juvenile delinquency, and in July 1943 rumors of a gang beating a white sailor led to a four-day riot where whites went into Mexican American neighborhoods and attacked zoot-suiters — these became known as the Zoot Suit Riots.

3. To what extent was Japanese incarceration constitutional?

Executive Order 9066 gave the War Department the authority to force Japanese immigrants and Americans from their West Coast homes into relocation camps during the war. About 120,000 Japanese Americans were incarcerated, and two-thirds of them were native-born U.S. citizens. Army officials only gave them a few days to dispose of their property and businesses before being forced into the camps, causing devastating financial losses.

In Korematsu v. United States (1944), the Supreme Court allowed the removal of Japanese Americans from the West Coast on the basis of military necessity but avoided ruling on the constitutionality of the incarceration program itself. The decision is widely regarded today as one of the worst Supreme Court rulings in history — there was no evidence of Japanese American disloyalty, and the incarceration was driven by racial prejudice and wartime hysteria rather than genuine security concerns. In 1988, Congress formally apologized and provided reparations to surviving internees, acknowledging the constitutional violation.

1. Why did the US not do more to help Jews trying to flee Europe during the war?

Anti-Semitism was widespread in the U.S., and immigration quotas were extremely strict — the government refused to raise them for Jewish refugees. The State Department only allowed about 21,000 Jewish refugees into the United States, though the War Refugee Board did help 200,000 find safe haven elsewhere. In 1939, the SS St. Louis, a German ocean liner carrying about 1,000 Jews fleeing persecution, tried to dock in the U.S., but FDR refused to let the ship in and forced it to return to Europe — reflecting America’s exclusionist policy. Many of those passengers later died in the Holocaust.

The State Department actively obstructed visa applications and suppressed information about the genocide. German extermination camps ultimately killed 6 million Jews. Military leaders argued that the fastest way to help Jews was to win the war and refused to bomb the rail lines leading to Auschwitz or the camp itself. Public opinion and political leadership simply lacked the will to prioritize refugee rescue during the war effort.

2. According to the textbook, why did the US drop two atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, Japan?

The Manhattan Project was a top-secret WWII government program to develop and deploy the world’s first atomic weapons before Nazi Germany could. It cost $2 billion and employed 120,000 people. When Harry Truman became president, he learned about the secret project and faced the decision of whether to use it. Truman initially opted for a demonstration of the bomb in the Pacific to threaten Japan into surrendering, but his advisors warned that if the demonstration failed, Japan would gain confidence. Military planners estimated that a land invasion of Japan could cost hundreds of thousands of American lives.

There was also a strategic desire to demonstrate American power to the Soviet Union — Truman hoped that dropping the bomb would make Stalin think twice about resisting American initiatives in the postwar world. The first bomb was dropped on Hiroshima on August 6, 1945, and when Japan did not immediately surrender, a second was dropped on Nagasaki on August 9. Japan surrendered on August 15, 1945.

3. What agreements did FDR and Churchill make with Stalin at Yalta in February, 1945?

At the Yalta Conference in 1945, the meeting between the Soviet Union, the United States, and Britain produced several major agreements. They divided Germany into occupation zones, Stalin agreed to join the war with Japan, and Germany was required to pay the Soviets for their loss of life and property. Stalin promised to let Eastern European countries hold free and fair elections, though he would later break that promise. FDR’s primary focus was on maintaining Allied unity to ensure postwar peace and stability.

They also agreed to establish the United Nations as an international organization to maintain peace. The Yalta agreements would later become extremely controversial as the Soviet Union installed communist governments across Eastern Europe rather than holding the free elections Stalin had promised.

Chapter 25: Cold War America, 1945–1963

APUSH Era 8: Cold War & Civil Rights (1945–1980)
1. How did American and Soviet viewpoints differ over the postwar fate of Europe?

At the Potsdam meeting after the war, the fundamental divide between the superpowers became clear. The United States wanted to rebuild the European and especially the German economy through free markets and democratic governments, believing that open trade and self-determination would prevent future wars. America wanted to rebuild Germany as a strong trading partner and believed capitalism and communism couldn’t coexist, which ultimately led to the policy of containment.

The Soviet Union, on the other hand, wanted to create a buffer zone of friendly communist states in Eastern Europe to protect against another invasion from the West — having suffered 27 million deaths in WWII, Stalin saw this as essential to Soviet security. The U.S. viewed Soviet expansion as aggressive communist imperialism, while the Soviets viewed American influence in Europe as capitalist encirclement. These fundamentally incompatible visions set the stage for the Cold War.

2. What did the Allies decide to do with Germany following WWII?

Germany was divided into four occupation zones controlled by the U.S., Britain, France, and the Soviet Union. Berlin, located deep within the Soviet zone, was also divided into four sectors. The Western powers eventually unified their three zones into a single state — the German Republic — without Soviet input. In response, Stalin implemented a blockade to stop the Allies from sending mail and resources into Western Berlin.

The Allies responded with the Berlin Airlift, flying supplies including food and fuel into Berlin for 11 months until Stalin finally removed the blockade. This was a major early confrontation of the Cold War and showed both sides’ willingness to push to the brink without actually going to war.

3. Why did the United States enact the Marshall Plan, and how did the program illustrate America’s new role in the world?

The Marshall Plan (1947) was a U.S. program of sending economic aid to European countries to help them rebuild after WWII, providing over $13 billion total. The U.S. feared that poverty and instability in Europe would make countries vulnerable to communist influence, so the plan was designed to create prosperous, stable democracies that would serve as allies against the Soviet Union. It illustrated America’s new role as a global superpower willing to use economic power to shape the international order, marking a permanent departure from isolationism. The Marshall Plan was a key element of the containment strategy — stopping the spread of communism by strengthening the economies and governments surrounding the Soviet bloc.

4. What contributed to the escalation of the Cold War?

Several key events escalated the Cold War rapidly. The Truman Doctrine (1947) committed the U.S. to giving economic and military aid to free nations threatened by armed internal minorities or outside pressures, establishing containment as official policy. The Berlin Blockade (1948–49) demonstrated how close the superpowers could come to direct conflict, and the formation of NATO in 1949 — a 12-nation peaceful military alliance where “an attack on one is an attack on all” — created a Western military bloc that the Soviets countered with the Warsaw Pact.

The arms race was a major escalating factor: the U.S. built the atomic bomb, then the Soviets developed their own atomic bomb (Stalin had known about the Manhattan Project through espionage), then the U.S. developed the hydrogen bomb, and the Soviets followed with their own H-bomb. The Chinese Civil War added another dimension, as Mao’s communists won and aligned with Stalin, and Americans accused the government of having “lost” China. The Korean War (1950–53) became the first “hot” proxy war, with the U.S. and South Korea fighting against North Korean and Chinese communist forces, ending in an armistice that retained the original borders at the 38th parallel.

1. What were the components of Cold War liberalism?

Cold War liberalism preserved the core programs of the New Deal welfare state while combining them with a strong containment policy abroad and fighting subversives at home. It was essentially moderate liberal policies mixed with anticommunism. It supported a strong national defense, active foreign policy, and the containment of communism, while domestically favoring moderate reform like expanding Social Security, raising the minimum wage, and supporting labor unions — but always within limits that wouldn’t appear “socialist.” Cold War liberals sought a middle ground between conservative laissez-faire economics and left-wing radicalism.

2. To what extent was Truman able to implement his “Fair Deal”? How did the Fair Deal compare to the New Deal?

Truman’s Fair Deal was ambitious — it proposed national health insurance, aid to education, a housing program, expansion of Social Security, a higher minimum wage, a new agriculture program, and efforts to help African Americans. However, Congress blocked most of it. A conservative coalition of Republicans and Southern Democrats defeated national health insurance and civil rights measures. Truman did manage to get Congress to pass the National Housing Act, which constructed 810,000 low-income housing units, and he succeeded in improving the minimum wage and expanding Social Security coverage.

Compared to the New Deal, the Fair Deal was more ambitious on civil rights but less successful overall. The political environment of Cold War anti-communism made it much harder to pass progressive domestic legislation, as opponents could simply label any reform “socialist” to kill it.

3. What were the ramifications of the Red Scare for civil liberties in the United States?

The Loyalty-Security Program (1947) permitted officials to investigate employees of the federal government for “subversive” activities. It was intended to apply to those accused of harming the U.S. but ended up being applied to everyone, and thousands were fired or forced to resign based on suspicion alone. HUAC (House Un-American Activities Committee) held public hearings on alleged communist infiltration in Hollywood, and hundreds of actors, writers, and directors were blacklisted after being mentioned in HUAC proceedings.

The Red Scare also targeted gays and lesbians, who were accused of communism and dismissed from the federal government for being “unfit.” The labor movement was deeply divided — communists were actually some of the most effective trade union organizers, and the American Communist Party promoted Black civil rights, but organizers were expelled by the NAACP and CIO when communist accusations were leveled at them. Senator Joseph McCarthy spread fear of communism by claiming he had a list of all members of the communist party (which was fake) and charged anyone who criticized him as being a communist. He launched investigations of subversive activities in the U.S. Army, but eventually his support decreased as people saw through his tactics.

4. What distinguished Dwight D. Eisenhower politically from previous Republican presidents?

Eisenhower practiced “Modern Republicanism” — unlike earlier Republicans who opposed the New Deal, Eisenhower accepted the basic framework of the welfare state. He did not try to dismantle Social Security, unemployment insurance, or labor protections. Instead, he increased federal outlays for veterans, housing, highway construction, and social security, and he raised the minimum wage from 75 cents to $1 per hour. He also signed the National Interstate and Defense Highways Act of 1956.

He was fiscally conservative with balanced budgets and limited new spending, but socially moderate, distinguishing himself from the isolationist, anti-New Deal wing of the Republican Party. In foreign policy, he continued containment and relied heavily on nuclear deterrence (“massive retaliation”) and covert CIA operations rather than large conventional military deployments.

1. In the 1950s, was the US more pro-democracy or anti-communist? Explain with examples.

The U.S. was primarily anti-communist rather than genuinely pro-democracy during this period. The CIA orchestrated the overthrow of the democratically elected government of Iran in 1953 and Guatemala in 1954, because those governments had nationalized industries or were seen as too friendly to the left — and the U.S. replaced them with authoritarian, pro-American regimes. The Eisenhower Doctrine promised military or economic aid to any Middle Eastern country needing help in resisting communist aggression, regardless of whether that country was actually democratic.

The U.S. supported dictators around the world, like Batista in Cuba and the Shah in Iran, as long as they were anti-communist. This made it clear that stopping communism consistently took priority over actually promoting democratic governance.

2. What was the main purpose of the CIA in the first years of its existence?

The CIA was created in 1947 primarily to gather intelligence and conduct covert operations to contain communism around the world. In practice, the CIA went far beyond simple intelligence gathering — it orchestrated coups, funded anti-communist political parties, conducted propaganda campaigns, and carried out secret military operations. Key early operations included overthrowing governments in Iran (1953) and Guatemala (1954). The CIA also conducted operations in Cuba and throughout Latin America, becoming a central tool of Cold War foreign policy.

3. In one sentence: Why did the United States get involved in Vietnam?

The United States got involved in Vietnam because of the domino theory — the policy that if one country fell to communism, its neighbors would soon follow, which the U.S. used as justification to intervene and prevent the spread of communism throughout Southeast Asia.

Chapter 26: Triumph of the Middle Class, 1945–1963

APUSH Unit 6
1. What primary factors led to the growth of the American economy after WWII?

Several factors came together to produce the postwar economic boom. Pent-up consumer demand after years of wartime rationing and Depression-era scarcity drove massive spending on goods that had been unavailable for years. The GI Bill (1944) provided returning veterans with education, housing loans, and business capital, stimulating economic growth across every sector. The military-industrial complex meant that continued government defense spending during the Cold War kept factories running and created high-paying jobs in aerospace, electronics, and weapons manufacturing.

The U.S. also had a huge structural advantage: it was the only major industrial power whose infrastructure was not destroyed by WWII, giving American manufacturers a dominant position in global markets. Technological innovations developed during the war — plastics, electronics, aviation — were adapted for consumer markets, creating entire new industries.

2. What were the major factors in the expansion of the middle class in the 1950s?

The GI Bill was the single biggest factor in expanding the middle class, enabling millions of veterans to attend college and buy homes. Rising wages and strong labor unions ensured that workers actually shared in the economic prosperity rather than seeing all the gains go to the top. The growth of white-collar jobs in corporations, government, and the service sector expanded the professional middle class, and suburban homeownership — made possible by FHA and VA loans — became the defining marker of middle-class life.

The launch of Sputnik by the Soviet Union in 1957 shocked America and triggered the space race, leading to the National Defense Education Act of 1958, which brought major federal investment in education — particularly math, science, and foreign language programs — further expanding opportunity for the next generation.

3. How did the economic boom of the 1950s affect American families?

Families moved to the suburbs and adopted a consumer lifestyle centered on homeownership, automobiles, and household appliances. The “baby boom” occurred as economic prosperity and optimism encouraged larger families. Television became central to family life and promoted consumer culture, while the “breadwinner/homemaker” model was idealized, with men working and women managing the household — though in reality many women were working outside the home throughout this period.

4. What were some of the consequences of the growth of labor unions in the 1950s?

Union membership peaked in the mid-1950s, giving workers significant bargaining power for higher wages, health insurance, pensions, and job security. Union contracts helped create a stable, prosperous working class that could actually afford homes, cars, and consumer goods, contributing to the broader middle-class expansion. However, many unions excluded women and minorities from leadership positions and the best jobs, limiting how far that prosperity spread.

Corruption in some unions, notably the Teamsters, led to public backlash and government investigations. The AFL and CIO merged in 1955, consolidating labor’s political power but also making the movement more bureaucratic and less militant than it had been in the 1930s.

1. In what ways did the federal government shape postwar suburbanization?

The federal government was the driving force behind suburbanization. The FHA and VA loan programs provided low-interest mortgages that made homeownership affordable for millions of families, especially veterans. The National Interstate and Defense Highways Act of 1956 built a massive highway system connecting suburbs to cities, making commuting possible for the first time on a large scale. Federal tax policies also allowed homeowners to deduct mortgage interest, effectively subsidizing suburban living. Levittowns became the model for mass-produced suburban housing — affordable and standardized, but originally whites-only.

However, this government-driven suburbanization was deeply discriminatory. FHA policies included redlining — refusing to insure mortgages in or near Black neighborhoods — and housing discrimination kept minorities out. In Shelley v. Kraemer (1948), the Supreme Court ruled against certain discriminatory housing practices, but informal discrimination continued to keep suburbs overwhelmingly white.

2. What was the connection between suburbanization and consumption?

Suburban living practically required new purchases — cars, appliances, furniture, lawn equipment, and televisions — which fueled an enormous increase in consumer spending. Shopping centers and malls were built in the suburbs, replacing downtown stores as the center of retail. Advertising and television promoted a culture of consumption that equated material goods with the “good life” and American success. The suburban lifestyle itself became a form of consumption, as keeping up with neighbors drove continuous purchasing in an endless cycle.

3. What factors explain the rise of the “Sunbelt”?

Defense spending and military bases were concentrated in Southern and Western states, bringing jobs and investment to those regions. Air conditioning made the hot climates of the South and Southwest livable year-round, which was a game-changer for population growth. Lower taxes, cheaper land, and fewer union restrictions attracted businesses relocating from the Northeast and Midwest. The interstate highway system and air travel made the Sunbelt more accessible than ever, and retirees moved south for the climate, further boosting population and economic growth in the region.

4. In what sense was the United States “two nations”?

While suburbs boomed with prosperity, inner cities deteriorated as middle-class whites left in what became known as “white flight,” taking tax revenue and investment with them. African Americans, Latinos, and other minorities were largely excluded from suburban prosperity by redlining, housing discrimination, and outright exclusion. The gap between suburban affluence and urban poverty created two starkly different Americas — one prosperous and mostly white, the other struggling and disproportionately nonwhite. Rural poverty, especially in Appalachia and the Deep South, was another dimension of this divide that suburban prosperity conveniently masked.

1. How did the Cold War impact gender roles in the US during the 1950s?

The Cold War promoted a vision of the “ideal American family” as a counterpoint to Soviet communism — the nuclear family with a male breadwinner and female homemaker was held up as a symbol of American freedom and stability. Domesticity was framed as patriotic, with women maintaining the home and raising children seen as doing their part for national security. Gender nonconformity was viewed with deep suspicion; homosexuality was treated as a security threat, and the “Lavender Scare” led to the firing of gay and lesbian federal employees. Despite this repressive climate, early gay rights efforts quietly began to emerge, though they remained small and cautious.

2. What were the cultural expectations of men and women in the 1950s?

Women were expected to be devoted wives and mothers, managing the household and supporting their husbands’ careers. Popular culture through TV shows and magazines reinforced this ideal constantly. Men were expected to be providers and breadwinners, working in corporations or professions, and they were supposed to be emotionally stoic and focused on career success. Some cultural rebels rejected these conformist expectations, embracing spontaneity, artistic expression, and nontraditional lifestyles. Despite the conformist ideal that dominated the surface of American culture, many Americans, especially women and minorities, experienced real frustration and dissatisfaction with these rigid expectations.

3. What transformations in women’s economic role took place in the 1950s and 1960s?

Despite the homemaker ideal, the number of working women actually increased throughout the 1950s — by 1960, nearly 40% of women with school-age children worked outside the home. Most working women were stuck in “pink-collar” jobs like teaching, nursing, clerical work, and retail, which were lower-paid and offered fewer advancement opportunities than men’s jobs. Women earned significantly less than men for comparable work, and professional careers were largely closed to them.

This growing gap between the cultural ideal of the stay-at-home mom and the economic reality of working women would fuel the feminist movement of the 1960s. Betty Friedan’s The Feminine Mystique (1963) gave voice to women’s frustrations and helped ignite a broader movement for gender equality.

Chapter 27: The Civil Rights Movement, 1941–1973

APUSH Unit 6
1. In what ways were African Americans unable to access their full political rights and equal economic opportunities in the 1940s and early 1950s, and how did they respond?

Jim Crow laws enforced racial segregation throughout the South — separate schools, restaurants, buses, water fountains, and public facilities, all backed by custom, law, and violence. African Americans faced voter suppression through poll taxes, literacy tests, white primaries, and outright intimidation. In the North, de facto segregation confined Black Americans to impoverished urban neighborhoods with inferior housing, schools, and job opportunities. Redlining prevented homeownership in white neighborhoods, and Black workers were concentrated in low-wage jobs and excluded from many unions.

African Americans responded in several ways. The NAACP pursued legal challenges through the courts, while the Double V campaign during WWII demanded victory over tyranny abroad and racism at home. Black voters increasingly supported civil rights candidates, and grassroots organizing in both the North and South laid the groundwork for the mass movement that would emerge in the mid-1950s.

2. Why did World War II play such a critical role in the civil rights movement?

The war exposed the hypocrisy of fighting tyranny and racism abroad while tolerating racial segregation at home, which energized the Double V campaign — the African American demand for victory over tyranny abroad and racism at home. Over one million African Americans served in the military, gaining new skills, confidence, and expectations of equality. When they returned, they were unwilling to accept second-class citizenship after having fought for freedom overseas.

Executive Order 8802 (1941) banned racial discrimination in defense industries, establishing the principle that the federal government could act against discrimination. Wartime migration brought African Americans to northern and western cities where they could vote and organize more freely, and the war’s rhetoric of freedom and democracy gave civil rights activists powerful moral arguments they could use in the decades ahead.

3. How did the Cold War help the civil rights movement? In what ways did it work against it?

The Cold War helped the civil rights movement because the U.S. wanted to present itself as the leader of the “free world,” and racial segregation was an embarrassment on the world stage that gave the Soviet Union powerful propaganda material. This international pressure motivated some federal action on civil rights — the Truman administration desegregated the military in 1948 and filed amicus briefs in civil rights cases partly to improve America’s international image.

However, the Cold War also worked against the movement in significant ways. The Red Scare equated civil rights activism with communism, and segregationists accused the NAACP and other groups of being communist fronts. Activists had to distance themselves from any association with the left, limiting their tactics and alliances. The Southern Manifesto of 1956, a statement signed by Southern congressmen opposing the Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka (1954) decision, used anti-communist rhetoric to oppose desegregation. The crisis at Central High School in Little Rock in 1957, where nine Black students attempted to integrate an all-white high school and Eisenhower had to send federal troops to enforce desegregation, showed both the power of federal intervention and the intensity of white resistance.

1. What factors explain the rise of the civil rights protest movement? Why were seasoned activists such as Ella Baker, Rev. C. T. Vivian, and John Lewis so important?

Several key events catalyzed the protest movement. The murder of Emmett Till in 1955 — a 14-year-old Black boy murdered in Mississippi whose killers were acquitted — galvanized Black communities and drew national attention to the brutality of Southern racism. The Montgomery Bus Boycott (1955–56) demonstrated that mass nonviolent protest could succeed and launched Martin Luther King Jr. to national prominence. The Greensboro sit-ins in 1960, where Black college students sat at a whites-only lunch counter, showed that young people could take the initiative, sparking a wave of sit-in protests across the South.

Seasoned activists were crucial because they provided organizational infrastructure, strategic wisdom, and training. Ella Baker helped found the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC), a youth-led civil rights organization focused on grassroots organizing and direct action. Rev. C. T. Vivian and John Lewis brought courage and moral authority from years of frontline activism. Organizations like the Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC), led by Martin Luther King Jr., coordinated nonviolent protests and provided resources for local movements across the South.

2. In what ways did white resistance hinder the civil rights movement? In what ways did it help?

White resistance hindered the movement through sheer violence and terror. White supremacist violence — bombings, beatings, and murders like the killing of Emmett Till and the 16th Street Baptist Church bombing in Birmingham in 1963 — terrorized Black communities and killed activists. State and local governments used police dogs, fire hoses, mass arrests, and legal harassment against peaceful protesters. The filibuster blocked civil rights legislation in Congress for years.

Paradoxically, white resistance also helped the movement. The brutality, when broadcast on national television, shocked the conscience of the nation and built public support for civil rights legislation. Events like the Freedom Rides in 1961, the Birmingham campaign in 1963, and the March on Washington in 1963 (where King delivered his “I Have a Dream” speech) were strategically designed to provoke violent reactions that would expose the injustice of segregation to a national audience. This ultimately built the political pressure that led to the Civil Rights Act of 1964 — banning discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin — and the Voting Rights Act of 1965, which banned discriminatory voting practices like literacy tests and poll taxes.

3. What aspects of the civil rights movement, particularly the sit-ins, were nonviolent? What was the protesters’ theory of nonviolence?

Sit-in participants were trained to remain calm, not retaliate, and absorb violence without fighting back — even when they were beaten, had food poured on them, or were burned with cigarettes. The theory of nonviolence, influenced by Gandhi and theologians like Howard Thurman, held that unearned suffering was redemptive. By accepting violence without retaliation, protesters exposed the moral bankruptcy of their oppressors and created a moral crisis that demanded resolution.

Nonviolence was also deeply strategic. It aimed to appeal to the conscience of the oppressor and the broader public, generating sympathy from white moderates, the media, and the federal government. Violent resistance, on the other hand, would have been used to justify further repression and would have allowed segregationists to paint the movement as dangerous rather than righteous.

4. What disagreements emerged between civil rights activists about tactics? Did Birmingham (1963) help resolve those disagreements?

There were significant disagreements within the movement about strategy. Some activists, like the NAACP, favored legal challenges and lobbying, while others in the SCLC and SNCC pushed for direct action — marches, sit-ins, boycotts, and civil disobedience. Within the direct-action wing itself, there were debates about whether to accept help from white allies, whether to work within the political system, and how confrontational to be. Younger activists in SNCC grew increasingly frustrated with the caution of older leaders and the slow pace of change.

The Birmingham campaign of 1963, led by King and the SCLC, used mass demonstrations including children to provoke Bull Connor’s violent response — police dogs and fire hoses turned on peaceful protesters, all broadcast on national television. Birmingham largely vindicated the direct-action approach: the shocking images built national support and directly pressured President Kennedy to propose what became the Civil Rights Act of 1964. However, disagreements about tactics continued and would only deepen in the late 1960s as some activists grew more radical.

The Big Picture — From Depression to Civil Rights

The story of America from the 1930s to the 1960s is really a story about how the country was completely transformed — economically, socially, and politically. It starts in the darkest moment of the Great Depression, when the economy had collapsed and roughly one in four Americans was out of work. President Hoover believed the government should stay out of it and that recovery would come naturally, but it never did. People were starving, churches were overwhelmed, and there was basically no safety net. When Franklin Roosevelt won the 1932 election, he promised a “New Deal” for the American people, and he delivered — fast.

In his First Hundred Days, FDR signed 15 major bills that reshaped the role of the federal government. He closed the banks, reformed them, created new agencies like the CCC to put people to work, and passed the Agricultural Adjustment Act to stabilize farmers. The government was now doing something it had never done before: directly stepping in to manage the economy and take care of its citizens. The Second New Deal went even further with the Social Security Act, the Wagner Act protecting unions, and the WPA, which employed millions building roads, bridges, and public buildings. FDR essentially redefined what “liberalism” meant in America — it was no longer about keeping government small, but about using government power to protect ordinary people from economic hardship.

The New Deal helped a lot of people, but it did not help everyone equally. Women were paid less, excluded from the CCC, and still expected to stay home. African Americans benefited from WPA jobs and FDR’s “Black Cabinet,” but FDR refused to support anti-lynching legislation because he needed Southern white Democrats. Mexican Americans were deported through “repatriation” programs, many of them unconstitutionally since they were U.S. citizens. Asian Americans were classified as “aliens ineligible for citizenship” and excluded from most federal programs. The New Deal created a powerful welfare state, but its benefits were shaped by the racism and inequality of the time.

Then came World War II, and everything changed again. Throughout the 1930s, America had been deeply isolationist — people remembered how they felt tricked into fighting World War I, and groups like the America First Committee pushed hard to keep the country out of another European conflict. FDR slowly moved the country toward supporting the Allies through programs like Lend-Lease, but it took the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941 to finally bring America into the war.

The war was an absolute economic explosion. Between 1940 and 1945, GDP doubled, unemployment basically disappeared, and the federal government became the nation’s biggest customer. The federal government directed factories to produce military equipment around the clock, and the military-industrial complex — the tight connection between government defense spending and private industry — was born. Women flooded into factories as “Rosie the Riveters,” and over 140,000 women served in the military. African Americans pushed for equality through the Double V campaign and pressured FDR into signing Executive Order 8802, banning racial discrimination in defense industries. Native Americans served as code talkers. The Bracero Program brought Mexican laborers to fill agricultural shortages.

But the war also had a dark side at home. Over 120,000 Japanese Americans — two-thirds of them U.S. citizens — were forced into internment camps under Executive Order 9066. The Supreme Court upheld this in Korematsu v. United States, a decision now widely regarded as one of the worst in American history. Racial tensions erupted in the Zoot Suit Riots in Los Angeles, where white servicemen attacked Mexican American youth. Meanwhile, the U.S. largely turned away Jewish refugees fleeing the Holocaust — the SS St. Louis carrying over 900 Jewish refugees was refused entry and forced to return to Europe.

The war ended dramatically when the U.S. dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945, weapons developed in secret through the Manhattan Project. Truman justified the decision by arguing that a land invasion of Japan would cost even more lives, but he also wanted to send a message to the Soviet Union. At the Yalta Conference earlier that year, FDR, Churchill, and Stalin had divided Germany into four occupation zones and created the United Nations, but cracks were already forming between the Western Allies and the Soviets.

Those cracks became the Cold War. The United States and Soviet Union had completely different visions for postwar Europe. America wanted to rebuild democratic, capitalist nations through programs like the Marshall Plan, while Stalin wanted a buffer zone of communist states in Eastern Europe. The Truman Doctrine committed the U.S. to containing communism everywhere, and when the Soviets blockaded West Berlin in 1948, the Allies responded with the Berlin Airlift. NATO was formed, the Soviets built their own atomic bomb, and the arms race was on. The Korean War became the first major “hot” conflict of the Cold War, ending in a stalemate at the 38th parallel.

At home, the Cold War created a climate of fear. The Loyalty-Security Program investigated federal employees for “subversive” activities, HUAC held public hearings that blacklisted hundreds of people in Hollywood, and Senator Joseph McCarthy spread panic by claiming he had lists of communists in the government. Gays and lesbians were fired from federal jobs during the “Lavender Scare.” The Red Scare made it dangerous to be seen as different or to question the status quo.

Despite this atmosphere of conformity, the postwar era also brought enormous prosperity. The GI Bill gave veterans education and housing loans, fueling the growth of the middle class. Suburbs like Levittown exploded across the landscape, connected to cities by the new interstate highway system. Americans bought cars, televisions, and appliances, and consumer culture became central to the American identity. But this prosperity was not shared equally — redlining and housing discrimination kept Black families out of the suburbs, and inner cities deteriorated as white families left. America was becoming “two nations” — one prosperous and mostly white, the other struggling and disproportionately nonwhite.

The 1950s also enforced rigid gender roles. Women were expected to be homemakers, and men were expected to be corporate breadwinners. The nuclear family was held up as a symbol of American strength against communism. But underneath the surface, many Americans were frustrated with this conformity. Some cultural rebels rejected mainstream culture entirely, embracing artistic expression and nontraditional lifestyles. And despite the cultural pressure to stay home, the number of working women actually increased throughout the decade.

The civil rights movement had been building for years, but it burst into the national spotlight in the mid-1950s. The brutal murder of Emmett Till in 1955 and the acquittal of his killers showed the nation just how violent Southern racism was. The Montgomery Bus Boycott launched Martin Luther King Jr. to national prominence and proved that mass nonviolent protest could work. The Supreme Court’s Brown v. Board of Education decision in 1954 declared school segregation unconstitutional, but white resistance was fierce — Southern congressmen signed the Southern Manifesto opposing it, and when nine Black students tried to integrate Central High School in Little Rock, Eisenhower had to send federal troops.

Through the early 1960s, the movement grew bolder. College students launched the Greensboro sit-ins in 1960, Freedom Riders tested desegregation on interstate buses in 1961, and the March on Washington in 1963 brought 250,000 people to hear King’s “I Have a Dream” speech. The movement’s strategy was brilliantly calculated — activists used nonviolent protest to provoke violent reactions from segregationists, and when Bull Connor turned police dogs and fire hoses on peaceful protesters in Birmingham in 1963, the images on national television shocked the country and pressured President Kennedy to propose civil rights legislation. Voter registration efforts in the Deep South continued to face violence and even murder.

The result of all this sacrifice was transformative legislation. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 banned discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin. The Voting Rights Act of 1965 outlawed discriminatory voting practices like literacy tests and poll taxes. These laws did not end racism, but they dismantled the legal framework of Jim Crow and represented the most significant expansion of civil rights since Reconstruction. The arc from the Depression to the civil rights movement shows how Americans — through struggle, sacrifice, and protest — reshaped their government and society in ways that are still felt today.
