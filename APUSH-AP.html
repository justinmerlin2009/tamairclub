About the AP US History Exam

The official course structure, themes, and exam breakdown — only what College Board requires.

Exam Format

  • Section I, Part A — Multiple Choice: 55 questions · 55 min · 40% of score
  • Section I, Part B — Short Answer (SAQ): 3 questions · 40 min · 20% of score
  • Section II, Part A — Document-Based Question (DBQ): 1 essay · 60 min · 25% of score
  • Section II, Part B — Long Essay (LEQ): 1 essay (choose 1 of 3) · 40 min · 15% of score
  • Total exam time: 3 hours, 15 minutes

The 8 Official Themes

Every question on the AP exam ties back to one of these 8 themes. When studying, ask: "Which theme does this connect to?"

NAT — American & National IdentityHow U.S. identity has been shaped by debates over citizenship, constitutionalism, foreign policy, and the meaning of "American."
WXT — Work, Exchange, & TechnologyHow systems of economic exchange, labor, capitalism, and technology have developed.
GEO — Geography & the EnvironmentHow geography and the environment have shaped the development of the U.S.
MIG — Migration & SettlementWhy people have come to and moved within North America, and the effects of those migrations.
PCE — Politics & PowerHow different groups have struggled to shape government, society, and the political system.
WOR — America in the WorldHow the U.S. has interacted with other countries through diplomacy, war, trade, and culture.

SOC — Social StructuresHow social categories — race, class, gender, ethnicity — have shaped lives and institutions.

Historical Reasoning Skills

The exam tests three reasoning processes you must be able to apply (especially on essays):

  • Causation: Identifying causes and effects of events. ("What caused X?" / "What were the effects of Y?")
  • Comparison: Identifying similarities and differences between things. ("Compare X and Y.")
  • Continuity & Change Over Time (CCOT): Identifying what changed and what stayed the same. ("How did X change from year A to year B?")

Historical Thinking Skills

  • Developments & Processes: Identify and explain a historical concept, development, or process.
  • Sourcing & Situation: Analyze sources for HIPP (Historical situation, Intended audience, Purpose, Point of view).
  • Claims & Evidence in Sources: Use sources to support an argument.
  • Contextualization: Place events in their broader historical context.
  • Making Connections: Use reasoning (causation, comparison, CCOT) to connect events.
  • Argumentation: Build a coherent thesis-driven argument.
Period 1 · 1491–1607

Unit 1: Pre-Columbian to European Contact

Native American societies, European exploration, and the Columbian Exchange.

Official CED Topics — Info & Background

Exam weight: 4–6% · ~8 class periods
1.2Native American Societies Before European ContactPre-1492

Before Europeans arrived, the Americas were home to millions of people in hundreds of distinct societies. The Pueblo (Southwest) built irrigated farming villages; the Iroquois (Northeast) formed a powerful confederacy of matrilineal tribes; Plains peoples were nomadic hunters; the Mississippian/Cahokian peoples built large mound cities. In Mesoamerica, the Aztec and Maya created complex empires; the Inca dominated the Andes. Geography shaped each society's economy, religion, and government.

1.3European Exploration in the Americas~1450–1600

After the Ottomans cut off land trade routes to Asia (1453), European nations sought sea routes for spices, gold, and silk. Motivated by "Gold, God, and Glory" — wealth, spreading Christianity, and national prestige — Portugal led early voyages along Africa, while Spain (under Columbus, 1492) reached the Americas. New tech (caravel ship, astrolabe, magnetic compass) made long voyages possible.

1.4Columbian Exchange, Spanish Exploration, and Conquest1492–1600s

The Columbian Exchange transferred plants (corn, potatoes, tobacco), animals (horses, cattle), people, and diseases between hemispheres. Smallpox killed up to 90% of Native populations, enabling Spanish conquest. Cortés overthrew the Aztec Empire (1521) and Pizarro the Inca (1533). Massive silver flows from the Americas reshaped Europe's economy (the Price Revolution).

1.5Labor, Slavery, and Caste in the Spanish Colonial System1500s–1600s

Spain enforced the encomienda — forced Native labor in exchange for "protection" and Christianization (effectively slavery). As Native populations died from disease and overwork, Spain began importing enslaved Africans. The casta system created a rigid racial hierarchy from peninsulares (Spain-born) at the top down to Natives and Africans.

1.6Cultural Interactions Between Europeans, Native Americans, and Africans1500s–early 1600s

Contact produced cultural blending, conflict, and resistance. Europeans imposed Christianity, language, and law, but Natives often blended new beliefs with their own. Africans, brought as slaves, preserved cultural traditions through music, religion, and language. Native resistance (e.g., Pueblo Revolt, 1680) showed colonization wasn't one-way — but disease and military force gave Europeans the upper hand.

Big Themes

  • Native societies were highly diverse and adapted to their environments — there was no single uniform "Indian" culture.
  • The Columbian Exchange transformed both hemispheres biologically, demographically, and economically.
  • Spain led early colonization with the encomienda and casta systems, locking in racial and labor hierarchies.

Native American Societies Before Contact

Before 1492, the Americas were home to tens of millions of people in hundreds of distinct societies. Their lifestyles depended heavily on geography — the kind of food they could grow or hunt shaped how their culture and government developed.

North America

Pueblo / Anasazi (Southwest)

Lived in dry desert regions of present-day NM and AZ. Built multi-story adobe villages and used irrigation to grow corn, beans, and squash (the "Three Sisters"). Their permanent farming communities show that not all Native peoples were nomadic.

Great Plains tribes

Mostly nomadic hunter-gatherers who followed buffalo herds. After the Spanish reintroduced horses in the 1500s, tribes like the Sioux, Comanche, and Cheyenne became mobile hunting societies — a huge example of how the Columbian Exchange reshaped Native life.

Iroquois Confederacy (Northeast)

An alliance of five (later six) tribes in modern-day New York. They mixed farming and hunting, lived in longhouses, and had a matrilineal society — property and ancestry passed through women. Their union of separate tribes under one council later influenced ideas of confederated government.

Pacific Northwest (Chinook, Kwakiutl)

Built rich societies based on fishing (salmon) and trade. Famous for totem poles and large wooden houses. They didn't farm because their environment was already so productive.

Mississippian / Cahokia

A massive city near modern St. Louis (peaked around 1100s) with up to 30,000 people and large ceremonial mounds. Proves that complex urban societies existed in North America long before Europeans.

Mesoamerica & South America

Aztec Empire (Mexico)

Centered on Tenochtitlán, a city built on a lake with about 200,000 people — bigger than most European cities. Ran on tribute from conquered peoples and practiced human sacrifice. Cortés exploited resentment from those tribes to defeat them.

Inca Empire (Andes)

Stretched along the Andes Mountains. Built incredible stone roads, terraced farms, and used quipu (knotted strings) to record information. Pizarro conquered them in 1533 by capturing the emperor Atahualpa.

Maya

An older classical civilization (peaked ~250–900 CE) in the Yucatán. Developed advanced astronomy, math (concept of zero), and a writing system. Mostly declined before Spanish arrival, but their descendants still lived in the region.

European Motivations for Exploration

European exploration didn't happen in a vacuum — it was driven by a mix of economics, religion, and politics, often summed up as "Gold, God, and Glory."

  • Gold (economic): After the Ottomans captured Constantinople in 1453, the old land routes to Asia for spices, silk, and gold were cut off. Europeans needed new sea routes — and quickly got greedy for the silver of the Americas.
  • God (religious): The Catholic Church (especially Spain and Portugal) wanted to spread Christianity. After the Protestant Reformation (1517), this rivalry intensified — Catholics tried to convert natives, while later Protestants colonized to escape persecution.
  • Glory (political): Strong nation-states like Spain, Portugal, France, England, and the Netherlands competed for prestige and territory. Whoever controlled the most colonies got the most wealth.
  • Technology: The caravel (a fast, maneuverable ship), the astrolabe (for navigation by stars), and the magnetic compass (borrowed from China via the Arabs) made long voyages possible.

The Columbian Exchange

The Columbian Exchange is the term for the massive transfer of plants, animals, people, diseases, and ideas between the Old World (Europe, Africa, Asia) and the New World (Americas) starting in 1492. It is arguably the most important event in modern world history because it fundamentally changed both hemispheres.

From Old World → New World

  • Animals: Horses (transformed Plains Native life), cattle, pigs, sheep, chickens.
  • Plants: Wheat, sugar, rice, coffee, bananas — sugar especially drove the slave trade.
  • Diseases: Smallpox, measles, influenza, typhus — natives had no immunity. These diseases killed an estimated up to 90% of the indigenous population within a century, an event so devastating it was the main reason Spanish conquest succeeded.
  • Culture: Christianity, European languages, gunpowder weapons, iron tools.

From New World → Old World

  • Crops: Corn (maize), potatoes, tomatoes, cacao, tobacco, peppers. Potatoes alone caused a population boom in Europe; tomatoes became central to Italian cooking; tobacco became a giant cash crop.
  • Diseases: Possibly syphilis (debated).
  • Wealth: Massive amounts of silver (from Potosí, Bolivia) flowed back to Europe, causing inflation called the Price Revolution and helping fund European wars.

Spanish Colonization

Spain led the charge in colonizing the Americas. They built the largest, wealthiest empire in the Western Hemisphere — but at enormous human cost.

Conquistadors

Spanish soldier-explorers who conquered native empires for "Gold, God, and Glory." Hernán Cortés conquered the Aztecs by 1521, partly with help from the rival Tlaxcala tribe and from disease. Francisco Pizarro conquered the Incas by 1533. They brought home enormous wealth.

Encomienda System

The Spanish crown granted colonists the right to force native labor in exchange for "protecting" and Christianizing them. In practice it was slavery — natives died in mines and on plantations from overwork and disease.

Casta System

A rigid racial hierarchy in Spanish colonies. The order: peninsulares (born in Spain) → criollos (Spanish parents born in colonies) → mestizos (Spanish + Native) → mulattos (Spanish + African) → Natives → Africans. Race shaped legal status, jobs, and marriages.

Bartolomé de las Casas

A Spanish friar who horrified by the abuse of natives wrote A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies. His criticism led to the New Laws (1542) reforming (but not ending) encomienda. Ironically, his alternative — using African slaves instead — helped justify the early Atlantic slave trade.

Pueblo Revolt (1680)

Led by Popé in present-day New Mexico, the Pueblo people drove the Spanish out for 12 years. The largest successful Native revolt in colonial America — it forced Spain to soften its religious demands when it returned.

Period 2 · 1607–1754

Unit 2: Colonial America

Colonial development, regional differences, and transatlantic trade.

Official CED Topics — Info & Background

Exam weight: 6–8% · ~14 class periods
2.2European Colonization1607–1700s

Spain, France, the Netherlands, and England colonized the Americas with very different approaches. Spain focused on conversion and resource extraction; France built fur-trade networks with cooperative Native alliances; the Dutch focused on trade through New Amsterdam; England, arriving last, built large permanent settlements (Jamestown 1607, Plymouth 1620, Mass. Bay 1630).

2.3The Regions of the British Colonies1607–1750s

Geography produced four distinct regions: New England (Puritan, small farms, fishing, town meetings), Middle Colonies (diverse, "breadbasket" wheat farming, religious tolerance), Chesapeake (tobacco plantations, indentured servants then slaves), and Southern (rice/indigo plantations, large slave populations). These differences shaped the country's later sectional conflicts.

2.4Transatlantic Trade1600s–1700s

Triangular trade linked the colonies, Africa, and Europe — rum and goods to Africa, enslaved Africans to the Americas, sugar/molasses/tobacco back to Europe. Mercantilism meant colonies existed to enrich England. The Navigation Acts (1651+) required colonial trade through English ships, but loose enforcement (salutary neglect) let smuggling flourish.

2.5Interactions Between American Indians and Europeans1607–1750s

Relations swung between trade, alliance, and warfare. Powhatan helped Jamestown survive but later wars displaced him. King Philip's War (1675) in New England devastated tribes and colonists alike. The French maintained mostly cooperative Native alliances; the English crowded Natives off land. Disease continued to weaken Native societies.

2.6Slavery in the British Colonies1619–1750s

First Africans arrived in Virginia in 1619. Initially indentured-servant-style, slavery hardened into hereditary chattel slavery after Bacon's Rebellion (1676) scared elites — they shifted from poor whites to enslaved Africans for labor. Slave codes (1705) made slavery racial and lifelong. The Stono Rebellion (1739) was the largest colonial-era slave uprising.

2.7Colonial Society and Culture1700s–1750s

Colonial society had more economic mobility than Europe but also rigid racial hierarchy. The Great Awakening (1730s–40s), led by Jonathan Edwards and George Whitefield, was a religious revival emphasizing personal faith over clergy authority — encouraging colonists to question traditional power. Enlightenment ideas (Locke, Montesquieu) about reason and natural rights were spreading. Self-governing colonial assemblies took deeper root.

The Three Colonial Regions

The 13 colonies weren't a single uniform place — they split into three regions with very different economies, religions, and cultures. Knowing those differences is one of the most-tested ideas in APUSH.

New England (MA, CT, RI, NH)

Settlement and Religion

Founded mostly by Puritans seeking freedom to practice their strict version of Protestantism. The Pilgrims (Separatists) founded Plymouth (1620) with the Mayflower Compact, an early example of self-government. The bigger group of Puritans founded Massachusetts Bay (1630) under John Winthrop, who said the colony should be a "City Upon a Hill" — a model Christian society for the world.

Economy & Society

Rocky soil and short summers meant small family farms, fishing, shipbuilding, and trade. Towns ran on town meetings, an early form of direct democracy.

Dissenters

Puritans were strict, and disagreement got you exiled. Roger Williams argued for separation of church and state and was banished — he founded Rhode Island with full religious tolerance. Anne Hutchinson was banished for preaching that direct revelation from God outranked the clergy. Their stories show early debates over religious liberty.

Middle Colonies (NY, NJ, PA, DE)

Diversity

The most diverse region by ethnicity and religion: Quakers, Dutch, Germans, Scots-Irish, Catholics. William Penn founded Pennsylvania (1681) as a Quaker "Holy Experiment" with religious freedom and fair treatment of Native Americans.

Economy

Often called the "breadbasket colonies" for their wheat and grain. Mixed economy of farming, trade, and small manufacturing.

Southern Colonies (VA, MD, NC, SC, GA)

Jamestown 1607

The first permanent English colony, in Virginia. Nearly failed multiple times due to disease, starvation ("Starving Time"), and conflict with the Powhatan Confederacy. Saved by John Rolfe's introduction of tobacco as a cash crop — which then drove the demand for cheap labor and shaped the Southern economy for centuries.

Plantation Economy

Soil and climate favored cash crops: tobacco (Chesapeake), rice and indigo (lower South). Required massive amounts of labor, first met by indentured servants, then by enslaved Africans. Few towns; Anglican Church was the dominant religion.

Slavery and Labor in the Colonies

Colonial America's labor system shifted dramatically over the 1600s and 1700s — from indentured servitude (mostly white, temporary) to chattel slavery (Black, hereditary, permanent).

Indentured Servitude

Poor English workers signed contracts to work for 4–7 years in exchange for passage to America. Most who survived eventually got their freedom and small plots of land. The system declined after 1670 because England's economy improved (fewer migrants) and freed servants demanded land — causing tensions with elites.

Bacon's Rebellion 1676

Nathaniel Bacon, a frontier planter, led poor white former servants and some enslaved Black people in attacks on Native Americans and burned Jamestown. They were angry at Governor Berkeley for not protecting frontier settlers. The rebellion terrified Virginia elites: poor Black and white workers had united against the rich. To prevent this in the future, planters shifted to African slave labor — easier to control because race was visible, and Africans had no rights at all.

The Middle Passage

The brutal voyage that took enslaved Africans across the Atlantic. Conditions were horrific — chained in tight ship holds, with high mortality from disease, suicide, and abuse.

Slave Codes 1705

Virginia and other colonies passed laws making slavery hereditary, racial, and lifelong. Children of enslaved mothers were automatically slaves. This locked in racial slavery as the foundation of the Southern economy.

Stono Rebellion 1739

The largest slave uprising in colonial America, in South Carolina. About 20 enslaved people killed white masters and tried to march to Spanish Florida (which offered freedom). It was crushed, and the response was even harsher slave codes.

British Colonial Policy

  • Mercantilism: An economic theory that a country's wealth depended on having a favorable trade balance. Colonies existed to send raw materials to England (tobacco, lumber, etc.) and buy back finished British goods.
  • Navigation Acts (1651 onward): Required all colonial trade to use English ships and pass through English ports. They were poorly enforced for decades — leading to widespread smuggling.
  • Salutary Neglect: Under Prime Minister Robert Walpole, Britain looked the other way on colonial trade violations. This gave colonists a long taste of self-government and economic freedom. When Britain tried to crack down after 1763, colonists felt their rights were being taken away.

The Great Awakening & Enlightenment

Two intellectual movements in the early 1700s reshaped how colonists thought about religion, authority, and government — both helped lay the groundwork for revolution.

Great Awakening (1730s–1740s)

A massive religious revival across the colonies. Preachers like Jonathan Edwards ("Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God") and George Whitefield emphasized personal faith and emotional conversion over the authority of established clergy. By teaching colonists to question religious authority, the Awakening made them more willing to question political authority too.

Enlightenment

An intellectual movement from Europe emphasizing reason, science, and natural rights. John Locke argued people had rights to "life, liberty, and property" and could overthrow governments that violated them. Montesquieu argued for separation of powers. These ideas directly inspired Franklin, Jefferson, and the Declaration of Independence.

French & Indian War (1754–1763)

The North American part of a global war (the Seven Years' War) between Britain and France. Both sides fought over the Ohio River Valley, with Native tribes mostly siding with the French, who treated them as trade partners rather than displacing them.

  • Britain won decisively. The Treaty of Paris (1763) gave Britain all French territory east of the Mississippi River + Canada.
  • Result: Britain was now broke from war debt — and decided the colonies, who had benefited from the protection, should help pay through new taxes. This is the spark that lit the road to revolution.
  • Proclamation of 1763: Britain banned colonial settlement west of the Appalachians to keep peace with Native tribes (and to limit defense costs). Colonists, who had fought to gain that land, were furious.

Period 3 · 1754–1800

Unit 3: Revolution and the New Republic

The American Revolution, the Constitution, and the new republic.

Official CED Topics — Info & Background

Exam weight: 8–10% · ~17 class periods
3.2The Seven Years' War (French and Indian War)1754–1763

Britain and France fought for control of the Ohio River Valley. Britain won decisively. The Treaty of Paris (1763) gave Britain all French territory east of the Mississippi. But Britain emerged with massive war debt and decided to tax the colonies — sparking conflict. The Proclamation of 1763 banned colonial settlement west of the Appalachians, angering colonists.

3.3Taxation Without Representation1763–1775

A series of British taxes — Sugar Act (1764), Stamp Act (1765), Townshend Acts (1767), Tea Act (1773) — angered colonists, who argued they couldn't be taxed without representation in Parliament. Resistance grew through the Stamp Act Congress, Sons of Liberty, the Boston Massacre (1770), and the Boston Tea Party (1773). Britain's harsh response — the Intolerable Acts (1774) — pushed colonies toward unity.

3.4Philosophical Foundations of the Revolution1760s–1770s

Enlightenment ideas drove revolutionary thinking. John Locke argued for natural rights to life, liberty, and property and the right to overthrow tyranny. Thomas Paine's Common Sense (1776) argued for full independence in plain language and sold over 100,000 copies. The Declaration of Independence (July 4, 1776) by Jefferson stated all men are created equal with inalienable rights.

3.5The American Revolution1775–1783

Started with Lexington and Concord (April 1775). The Battle of Saratoga (1777) was the turning point — convinced France to ally with the U.S. Valley Forge (1777–78) tested Washington's army. The Battle of Yorktown (1781) and the Treaty of Paris (1783) ended the war and recognized independence.

3.6Influence of Revolutionary Ideals1770s–1790s

Revolutionary ideals of liberty and equality inspired bigger conversations. Northern states began gradual abolition. Women like Abigail Adams asked their husbands to "Remember the Ladies." The American Revolution influenced the French Revolution (1789) and the Haitian Revolution (1791). But ideals were limited — slavery and gender inequality continued.

3.7The Articles of Confederation1781–1789

The first U.S. government — intentionally weak. No power to tax, no executive, no national courts. Big success: the Northwest Ordinance (1787) created a path for new states to join (and banned slavery in the NW Territory). Big failure: Shays' Rebellion (1786–87) — Massachusetts farmers revolted over debt, the federal government couldn't help, and elites realized the Articles needed to be replaced.

3.8Constitutional Convention & Debates over Ratification1787–1788

Delegates met in Philadelphia and wrote a brand-new Constitution. Key compromises: the Great Compromise (House by population, Senate equal), the Three-Fifths Compromise (counted enslaved people 3/5 for representation), and the Slave Trade Compromise (no ban until 1808). Federalists (Hamilton, Madison, Jay — wrote the Federalist Papers) supported it; Anti-Federalists demanded a Bill of Rights.

3.9The Constitution1788–1791

Created three branches with checks and balances. Federalism split power between federal and state governments. The Bill of Rights (1791) — first 10 amendments — protected individual liberties (speech, religion, due process) and was the price of ratification.

3.10Shaping a New Republic1789–1800

Washington set lasting precedents. Hamilton's Financial Plan (national bank, assumption of state debts, tariffs) built U.S. credit but split the country. The Whiskey Rebellion (1794) proved federal authority. Washington's Farewell Address warned against political parties and foreign alliances. Adams' presidency saw the XYZ Affair and the controversial Alien & Sedition Acts (1798).

3.11Developing an American Identity1780s–1800

Americans built a distinct national identity around republicanism (government by elected reps), virtue, and freedom. Religious freedom expanded; public education spread; "Republican Motherhood" gave women a civic role raising virtuous citizens. National symbols (the flag, eagle, "Yankee Doodle") emerged.

3.12Movement in the Early Republic1780s–1800

Americans pushed westward into Native lands. The Northwest Ordinance systematized settlement of the Ohio Valley. Tribes like the Shawnee under Tecumseh resisted but lost ground. Westward migration sparked conflicts that would shape the next century.

Big Themes

  • Conflict over taxation, representation, and identity led to revolution.
  • The Articles of Confederation failed → replaced by Constitution with stronger federal government.
  • The first party system emerged (Federalists vs. Democratic-Republicans) over how to interpret the Constitution and the role of government.

Causes of the Revolution

After winning the French & Indian War, Britain tried to tighten control over the colonies and pay off war debt. A series of taxes and acts pushed colonists toward open rebellion.

British Taxes & Acts

Sugar Act 1764

Tax on sugar and molasses imports — first tax aimed specifically at raising revenue, not just regulating trade. Hit New England merchants hardest.

Stamp Act 1765

A direct tax on all paper goods (newspapers, contracts, playing cards). Colonists exploded with the cry "No taxation without representation!" The Stamp Act Congress formed; Sons of Liberty harassed tax collectors. Britain repealed it but passed the Declaratory Act, asserting it could tax colonies whenever it wanted.

Townshend Acts 1767

Indirect taxes (duties on glass, paper, lead, tea) intended to feel less offensive than direct taxes. Colonists boycotted British goods anyway. The acts also created customs offices and writs of assistance (general search warrants).

Boston Massacre 1770

British soldiers fired into a Boston crowd, killing 5 colonists. Sam Adams and Paul Revere turned it into propaganda — Revere's engraving made it look like a deliberate slaughter, even though it was more of a chaotic street brawl.

Boston Tea Party 1773

The Tea Act gave the British East India Company a monopoly on tea, undercutting colonial merchants. The Sons of Liberty, disguised as Mohawks, dumped 342 chests of tea into Boston Harbor.

Intolerable Acts 1774

Britain's harsh punishment for the Tea Party: closed Boston Harbor, suspended the Massachusetts charter, allowed quartering of troops. This sparked the First Continental Congress, where colonies coordinated resistance for the first time.

Ideological Foundations

Common Sense 1776

Pamphlet by Thomas Paine arguing for full independence in plain, fiery language anyone could read. It sold over 100,000 copies in months and shifted public opinion from "we want our rights as Englishmen" to "we want to leave."

Declaration of Independence July 4, 1776

Drafted mostly by Thomas Jefferson. Drew heavily on John Locke's ideas — that all men have natural rights to "life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness," and that governments exist to protect those rights and can be overthrown if they fail. Listed grievances against King George III.

The Revolutionary War (1775–1783)

Lexington & Concord April 1775

British troops marched to seize colonial weapons. Minutemen confronted them — the "shot heard 'round the world." First armed conflict of the war.

Battle of Saratoga 1777

The most important turning point. American victory over British General Burgoyne convinced France to formally ally with the U.S. — bringing money, troops, and a navy that would eventually decide the war.

Valley Forge 1777–78

Brutal winter encampment. Washington's army nearly froze and starved, but Baron von Steuben trained them into a real fighting force. A symbol of perseverance.

Battle of Yorktown 1781

Washington's army, with French troops and a French naval blockade, trapped British General Cornwallis. His surrender effectively ended the war.

Treaty of Paris 1783

Britain officially recognized U.S. independence and gave up land all the way to the Mississippi River.

Articles of Confederation (1781–1789)

The first U.S. government. Designed to be weak on purpose — Americans had just rebelled against a strong central authority and didn't want a new tyrant. But it turned out to be too weak.

  • What it lacked: No power to tax, no executive, no national court system, no power to regulate interstate or international trade.
  • What it accomplished: The Northwest Ordinance (1787) created a process for admitting new states (had to apply when reaching 60,000 people) and banned slavery in the Northwest Territory.
Shays' Rebellion 1786–87

Massachusetts farmers led by Daniel Shays (a Revolutionary War veteran) revolted over crushing debts and farm foreclosures. The federal government had no power to help — states had to put it down themselves. This scared elites into believing the Articles needed to be replaced. Directly led to the Constitutional Convention.

The Constitution (1787)

Delegates met in Philadelphia to "revise" the Articles but instead wrote a brand-new framework of government, with three branches and shared power between federal and state levels.

Major Compromises

  • Great Compromise: Settled the dispute between big and small states by creating a bicameral Congress — the House (by population, favors big states) and the Senate (equal, favors small states).
  • Three-Fifths Compromise: Counted 3/5 of enslaved people for representation and taxation. Boosted Southern political power without giving any rights to the enslaved.
  • Slave Trade Compromise: Congress couldn't ban the international slave trade until 1808.

Federalists vs. Anti-Federalists

  • Federalists (Hamilton, Madison, Jay) supported ratification and wrote the Federalist Papers defending it. They believed a strong central government was needed.
  • Anti-Federalists (Patrick Henry, Sam Adams, George Mason) feared a strong central government would crush liberty like the British had. They demanded a Bill of Rights — added in 1791 as the first 10 Amendments.

Washington's Presidency (1789–1797)

The first president had to figure out everything from how to address the office to how to handle foreign wars and domestic uprisings. He set precedents still followed today.

Hamilton's Financial Plan

Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton proposed: federal assumption of state debts, a national bank, tariffs to protect American industry, and an excise tax on whiskey. Built U.S. credit and ties between business and government — but Jefferson hated it.

Whiskey Rebellion 1794

Pennsylvania farmers refused to pay the whiskey tax. Washington personally led 13,000 troops to crush them. Showed that, unlike under the Articles, the new federal government would enforce its laws.

Proclamation of Neutrality 1793

When France and Britain went to war, Washington kept the U.S. out of European conflicts, even though France had been our ally. Set a long-term U.S. policy of staying out of European wars.

Farewell Address

Warned Americans against two things: political parties (which he thought caused factionalism) and permanent foreign alliances. Both warnings were soon ignored.

First Party System

Despite Washington's warning, two political parties immediately formed around different visions of America:

  • Federalists (Hamilton, Adams): Strong federal government, loose interpretation of the Constitution, pro-British, urban/industrial economy. Strong in New England.
  • Democratic-Republicans (Jefferson, Madison): States' rights, strict interpretation, pro-French, agrarian (farming) economy. Strong in the South and West.
Adams' Presidency & Alien and Sedition Acts 1798

The XYZ Affair (French agents demanded bribes from U.S. diplomats) caused war fever. Federalists passed the Alien and Sedition Acts, which made it harder to become a citizen and a crime to publicly criticize the government. Jefferson and Madison wrote the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions arguing states could nullify federal laws — an early hint of states' rights doctrine that would resurface before the Civil War.

Period 4 · 1800–1848

Unit 4: The Early Republic & Antebellum Era

Jeffersonian/Jacksonian democracy, Manifest Destiny, and the Market Revolution.

Official CED Topics — Info & Background

Exam weight: 10–17% ★ · ~17 class periods
4.2Rise of Political Parties & Era of Jefferson1800–1808

Jefferson's election was the "Revolution of 1800" — the first peaceful transfer of power between rival parties. Major events: the Louisiana Purchase (1803) doubled the country's size; Marbury v. Madison (1803) established judicial review; the Embargo Act (1807) backfired economically.

4.3Politics & Regional Interests1812–1828

The War of 1812 against Britain ended in stalemate but boosted U.S. nationalism — beginning the "Era of Good Feelings." Henry Clay's American System proposed a national bank, tariffs, and internal improvements. The Missouri Compromise (1820) kept the slave/free state balance using the 36°30′ line.

4.4America on the World Stage1820s

The Monroe Doctrine (1823) declared the Western Hemisphere off-limits to further European colonization — a foundation of U.S. foreign policy for over a century. The Court ruled in McCulloch v. Maryland (1819) and Gibbons v. Ogden (1824) to expand federal power.

4.5Market Revolution: Industrialization1815–1850

A massive economic transformation. New transportation — the Erie Canal (1825), steamboats, and railroads — connected the country. The factory system arrived (Lowell Mills hired farm women). Inventions like the cotton gin (Whitney), telegraph (Morse), and interchangeable parts revolutionized production. The cotton gin massively expanded slavery in the South.

4.6Market Revolution: Society and Culture1815–1850

Industrialization reshaped daily life. Regional specialization grew: industrial North, cotton South, farming West. The Cult of Domesticity idealized middle-class women as moral homemakers. Massive immigration (Irish, German) sparked nativism and the Know-Nothing Party. Urbanization brought poverty alongside wealth.

4.7Expanding Democracy1820s–1830s

Most states dropped property requirements for voting, expanding to universal white male suffrage. Voter turnout exploded. Politics became participatory and rowdy — torchlight parades, mass rallies. Women, Black Americans, and Native Americans were still excluded.

4.8Jackson and Federal Power1829–1837

Jackson's presidency redefined federal power. The Spoils System rewarded loyalists with jobs. The Indian Removal Act (1830) led to the Trail of Tears (1838). The Nullification Crisis (1832) with South Carolina previewed the Civil War. Jackson's Bank War destroyed the Second Bank of the U.S., causing the Panic of 1837. Opposition formed the Whig Party.

4.9Development of an American Culture1820s–1840s

A distinctly American culture emerged. Transcendentalism — Emerson and Thoreau — celebrated individualism, intuition, and nature. The Hudson River School painted majestic American landscapes. American literature (Hawthorne, Melville, Poe) developed unique voices.

4.10The Second Great Awakening1820s–1840s

A massive religious revival led by Charles Finney in the "burned-over district" of upstate New York. Taught that ordinary people could choose salvation and improve society. Inspired most major reform movements of the era.

4.11An Age of Reform1820s–1840s

Reforms exploded out of the Second Great Awakening: abolition (Garrison's Liberator, Douglass), women's rights (Seneca Falls Convention 1848 — Stanton, Mott), temperance (against alcohol), education (Horace Mann's public schools), asylum reform (Dorothea Dix), and utopian communities (Brook Farm, Oneida).

4.12African Americans in the Early Republic1800–1848

Free Black communities grew in Northern cities and built churches, schools, and newspapers (Frederick Douglass's North Star). Slave resistance took many forms — Nat Turner's Rebellion (1831) in Virginia killed about 60 whites, leading to harsher slave codes. The Underground Railroad (Harriet Tubman) helped enslaved people escape.

4.13Society of the South in the Early Republic1800–1848

The cotton gin made cotton extremely profitable, deepening the South's economic dependence on slavery. Wealth concentrated in plantation elites; most Southern whites were small farmers. Pro-slavery arguments shifted from "necessary evil" to "positive good" (John C. Calhoun). Sectional differences with the North hardened.

  • Democracy expanded for white men under Jackson — but not for women, Black Americans, or Native Americans.
  • The Market Revolution transformed the economy: factories, canals, railroads, wage labor.
  • The Second Great Awakening fueled big reform movements (abolition, women's rights, temperance).

Jefferson's Presidency (1801–1809)

Jefferson called his election the "Revolution of 1800" — the first peaceful transfer of power between rival parties in U.S. history.

Louisiana Purchase 1803

Jefferson bought 828,000 sq miles from Napoleon for $15 million — doubling the size of the U.S. The catch: Jefferson believed in strict interpretation of the Constitution, which didn't say anything about buying land. He bent his own principles to make the deal.

Lewis and Clark Expedition 1804–06

Sent to map the new territory, find a water route to the Pacific (didn't exist), and contact Native tribes. Their guide Sacagawea was crucial. Returned with detailed scientific data and a stronger U.S. claim to the West.

Marbury v. Madison 1803

Chief Justice John Marshall established judicial review — the Supreme Court's power to strike down laws that violate the Constitution. This made the judiciary truly equal to Congress and the President.

Embargo Act 1807

To pressure Britain and France (who were seizing U.S. ships during their war), Jefferson banned ALL American foreign trade. It backfired — hurt American merchants more than Europeans. New England (which depended on shipping) was furious.

War of 1812

Sometimes called the "Second War for Independence."

  • Causes: British impressment (kidnapping U.S. sailors into the British Navy), British arming of Native Americans (especially the great chief Tecumseh), and "War Hawks" in Congress (Henry Clay, John C. Calhoun) who wanted to annex Canada.
  • Lowlights: The British burned Washington, D.C., including the White House, in 1814.
  • Battle of New Orleans (Jan 1815): Andrew Jackson won a smashing victory — but the war had already officially ended weeks earlier (news traveled slow). It made Jackson a national hero.
  • Hartford Convention (1814): New England Federalists, angry at the war, met and even discussed secession. After the U.S. won, they looked traitorous — this destroyed the Federalist Party.
  • Result: The war ended in a draw, but Americans saw it as a victory. National pride surged, kicking off the "Era of Good Feelings."

Era of Good Feelings & Nationalism (1817–1825)

One-party rule under Monroe. Federalists were dead, so almost everyone was a Democratic-Republican. Looked unified — but sectional tensions were brewing underneath.

American System

Henry Clay's plan to bind the country together economically: a national bank, protective tariffs for American industry, and federal funding for internal improvements (roads, canals).

Monroe Doctrine 1823

President Monroe declared the Western Hemisphere off-limits to further European colonization. The U.S. couldn't actually enforce it (the British Navy did), but it became a cornerstone of U.S. foreign policy for over a century.

Missouri Compromise 1820

Missouri wanted to enter as a slave state, threatening the balance. Clay's solution: Missouri = slave, Maine = free, and slavery banned north of the 36°30′ line. Held the country together for 30 years but didn't solve the underlying issue.

Marshall Court

McCulloch v. Maryland upheld federal supremacy over states. Gibbons v. Ogden gave Congress power over interstate commerce. Both expanded federal power.

Jacksonian Democracy (1829–1837)

Andrew Jackson ran as a champion of the "common man." His era saw an explosion of democracy — for white men.

  • Universal white male suffrage (most states dropped property requirements). Voter turnout exploded.
  • Spoils System: Jackson rewarded loyal supporters with government jobs, on the theory that ordinary people could do the work.
Indian Removal Act 1830

Authorized forced removal of Native tribes east of the Mississippi to "Indian Territory" (Oklahoma). The Supreme Court sided with the Cherokee in Worcester v. Georgia (1832), but Jackson reportedly said "John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it." The result: the Trail of Tears (1838), where roughly 4,000 Cherokee died on the forced march.

Nullification Crisis 1832–33

South Carolina, led by John C. Calhoun, declared the high "Tariff of Abominations" null and void within the state. Jackson threatened to send troops. Henry Clay's compromise tariff defused the crisis. Foreshadowed the Civil War — South Carolina was already willing to defy federal law over economics tied to slavery.

Bank War

Jackson hated the Second Bank of the United States as a tool of elites. He vetoed its re-charter, withdrew federal funds, and put the money in state "pet banks." This destabilized the economy → Panic of 1837, a major depression.

Whig Party Forms

Jackson's opponents — Henry Clay, Daniel Webster, Calhoun — formed the Whigs. They supported the American System and opposed "King Andrew." This created the Second Party System (Democrats vs. Whigs).

The Market Revolution

A massive economic transformation between roughly 1815 and 1850 that turned much of the U.S. from local farming into a national, money-based economy.

  • Transportation: The Erie Canal (1825) connected the Great Lakes to the Atlantic, making NYC the country's biggest port. Steamboats and then railroads spread commerce.
  • Factory system: The Lowell Mills in Massachusetts hired young farm women as textile workers. Industrial labor was new and harsh.
  • Inventions: Eli Whitney's cotton gin (1793) made cotton cleaning fast — which massively expanded slavery (more cotton, more demand for slaves). Other inventions: telegraph (Morse), interchangeable parts (Whitney again), McCormick reaper.
  • Regional specialization: The North became industrial; the South doubled down on cotton and slavery; the West became the breadbasket.
  • Cult of Domesticity: Middle-class ideal that women belonged in the home as "moral guardians." A "separate spheres" ideology that limited women's economic roles.
  • Immigration boom: Mostly Irish (Catholic, fleeing the Potato Famine, settled in NE cities) and Germans (often Protestant, settled in Midwest farms). Sparked nativism (anti-immigrant prejudice) and the Know-Nothing Party.

Reform Movements (Second Great Awakening)

A second wave of religious revival starting in the 1820s. Preachers like Charles Finney taught that ordinary people could choose salvation and improve society. This sparked a flood of reform movements.

  • Abolition: William Lloyd Garrison founded The Liberator demanding immediate emancipation. Frederick Douglass, an escaped enslaved man, became the most famous Black abolitionist. Harriet Tubman led the Underground Railroad.
  • Women's Rights: The Seneca Falls Convention (1848), organized by Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott, produced the Declaration of Sentiments ("all men and women are created equal") — kickoff of the women's suffrage movement.
  • Temperance: Movement against alcohol; eventually led to Prohibition.
  • Education: Horace Mann championed free public schools, normal schools to train teachers, and a longer school year.
  • Utopian Communities: Brook Farm, Oneida, Shakers — experimental societies trying to live out perfect ideals. Most failed.
  • Transcendentalism: Ralph Waldo Emerson and Henry David Thoreau emphasized individualism, intuition, and the spiritual power of nature.

Manifest Destiny

The belief, popularized in the 1840s, that the U.S. was destined by God to expand across the entire continent. Used to justify taking land from Mexicans, Native Americans, and others.

  • Texas Annexation (1845): Texas had won independence from Mexico in 1836. The U.S. annexed it under Polk, infuriating Mexico.
  • Mexican-American War (1846–48): Polk provoked war over the Texas border. The U.S. won decisively. The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo gave the U.S. the Mexican Cession — modern California, Nevada, Utah, Arizona, New Mexico, and parts of Colorado/Wyoming.
  • Wilmot Proviso: Proposed banning slavery in any land won from Mexico. Failed in the Senate, but it forced the slavery-expansion question into the open and accelerated sectional crisis.

Period 5 · 1844–1877

Unit 5: Civil War & Reconstruction

Civil War, westward expansion, and Reconstruction.

Official CED Topics — Info & Background

Exam weight: 10–17% ★ · ~17 class periods
5.2Manifest Destiny1840s

The belief that America was destined by God to expand across the entire continent. Used to justify taking land from Mexicans, Native Americans, and others. Drove Texas annexation (1845), Oregon Treaty (1846), and the Mexican-American War.

5.3The Mexican–American War1846–1848

President Polk provoked war over the Texas border. The U.S. won decisively. The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848) gave the U.S. the Mexican Cession — modern California, Nevada, Utah, Arizona, New Mexico. Now the question of slavery's expansion became impossible to ignore. The Wilmot Proviso (failed) tried to ban slavery in the new territories.

5.4The Compromise of 18501850

Henry Clay's last great compromise. California entered as a free state; the slave trade ended in DC; the territories of Utah and New Mexico used popular sovereignty; and the harsh new Fugitive Slave Act required Northerners to help return escaped slaves. The Fugitive Slave Act especially outraged the North.

5.5Sectional Conflict: Regional Differences1840s–1850s

The North was industrializing, free-labor, urbanizing; the South was plantation-based, slave-labor, rural. Different economies meant different views on tariffs, internal improvements, and especially slavery. Pro-slavery thinkers like John C. Calhoun argued slavery was a "positive good" — Northerners increasingly saw it as a moral evil.

5.6Failure of Compromise1854–1860

Compromise broke down. The Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854) used popular sovereignty, repealing the Missouri Compromise — and led to "Bleeding Kansas." It also created the Republican Party. The Dred Scott decision (1857) denied Black citizenship and the power of Congress to ban slavery in territories. John Brown's Raid (1859) on Harpers Ferry terrified Southerners.

5.7Election of 1860 and Secession1860–1861

Lincoln won with no Southern support — pledging to stop slavery's expansion. South Carolina seceded that December. By February 1861, seven states formed the Confederate States of America under Jefferson Davis. Fort Sumter (April 1861) began the war.

5.8Military Conflict in the Civil War1861–1865

Major events: the Union's Anaconda Plan blockaded Southern ports. Antietam (1862), the bloodiest single day, allowed the Emancipation Proclamation. Gettysburg and Vicksburg (July 1863) were the dual turning points. Sherman's March to the Sea (1864) introduced total war. Lee surrendered at Appomattox (April 1865).

5.9Government Policies During the Civil War1861–1865

The federal government grew enormously. The Emancipation Proclamation (1863) freed slaves in rebel states. Lincoln suspended habeas corpus, jailing dissenters. The Homestead Act (1862), Pacific Railway Act (1862), and Morrill Act (1862) all expanded federal power. About 180,000 Black soldiers fought for the Union (54th Massachusetts).

5.10Reconstruction1865–1877

After the war, the U.S. tried to rebuild and integrate freed slaves. Different plans (Lincoln, Johnson, Radical Republicans) clashed. The Reconstruction Amendments transformed the Constitution: 13th abolished slavery, 14th guaranteed citizenship + equal protection, 15th banned race-based voting discrimination. The Freedmen's Bureau aided former slaves; about 2,000 African Americans held office in the South.

5.11Failure of Reconstruction1865–1877

White Southerners used violence (KKK), legal restrictions (Black Codes), and economic control (sharecropping) to recreate racial subordination. The Compromise of 1877 ended Reconstruction by removing federal troops from the South. Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) later legalized "separate but equal" — kicking off Jim Crow segregation that lasted until the 1960s.

Big Themes

  • Slavery's expansion, not just its existence, was the central political issue.
  • One compromise after another failed; war became inevitable.
  • Reconstruction transformed the Constitution (13/14/15) but failed in practice — set up Jim Crow for almost a century.

Sectional Crisis (1850s)

Every event in the decade before the Civil War revolved around the same question: could slavery expand into new territories?

Compromise of 1850

Henry Clay's last great compromise. California entered as a free state. The slave trade (but not slavery) ended in DC. The most controversial part: a stronger Fugitive Slave Act requiring Northern citizens to help return escaped slaves. This horrified Northerners and turned many of them against slavery.

Uncle Tom's Cabin 1852

Harriet Beecher Stowe's novel showing the cruelty of slavery sold 300,000 copies in its first year. It transformed Northern public opinion. Lincoln supposedly called Stowe "the little woman who started this great war."

Kansas-Nebraska Act 1854

Stephen Douglas's plan to open these territories using popular sovereignty (let voters decide on slavery). Effectively repealed the Missouri Compromise. Pro- and anti-slavery settlers flooded in → "Bleeding Kansas" mini civil war. The fallout created the Republican Party, dedicated to stopping slavery's spread.

Dred Scott v. Sandford 1857

The Supreme Court ruled that Black Americans were not citizens and that Congress could not ban slavery in territories. It made the Missouri Compromise officially unconstitutional. Anti-slavery Northerners were outraged — peaceful resolution was now nearly impossible.

John Brown's Raid on Harpers Ferry 1859

Radical abolitionist John Brown seized a federal arsenal in Virginia hoping to start a slave uprising. He was caught and hanged. Northerners called him a martyr; the South was terrified that Northerners wanted a violent end to slavery.

Lincoln-Douglas Debates 1858

Senate race in Illinois. Douglas defended popular sovereignty; Lincoln argued slavery was morally wrong and shouldn't expand. Lincoln lost the Senate seat but became famous nationally — paving the way for his 1860 presidential victory.

The Civil War (1861–1865)

  • South Carolina seceded in December 1860 after Lincoln's election. By February 1861, seven states formed the Confederate States of America under Jefferson Davis.
Fort Sumter April 1861

South Carolina forces fired on the federal Fort Sumter — opening shots of the war. Four more states (including Virginia) seceded after Lincoln called for troops.

Anaconda Plan

Union strategy: blockade Southern ports, take the Mississippi River, and slowly strangle the South. It worked over 4 years.

Antietam Sept 1862

Bloodiest single day in U.S. history (~23,000 casualties). Tactical draw, but a Union strategic win. It gave Lincoln the political opening to issue the Emancipation Proclamation (Jan 1863), which freed enslaved people in Confederate territory and reframed the war as a fight against slavery — preventing Britain and France from supporting the South.

Gettysburg July 1863

Three-day battle in Pennsylvania. Lee's invasion of the North was crushed. Union turning point in the East. Lincoln's Gettysburg Address redefined the war as a fight for the principle that "all men are created equal."

Vicksburg July 1863

Grant captured this Mississippi River town, splitting the Confederacy in two. With Gettysburg, this was the decisive double turning point.

Sherman's March to the Sea 1864

Union General William Tecumseh Sherman burned a 60-mile-wide path of destruction from Atlanta to Savannah. Pioneered total war — destroying not just armies but the South's ability to keep fighting.

Appomattox Court House April 1865

Lee surrendered to Grant. Effective end of the war. Days later, Lincoln was assassinated by John Wilkes Booth — a tragedy that would shape Reconstruction.

Wartime Politics & Society

  • Habeas corpus suspended: Lincoln jailed dissenters without trial — controversial expansion of executive power.
  • Copperheads: Northern Democrats who opposed the war.
  • Homestead Act (1862): 160 acres of free land to settlers who farmed it for 5 years. Encouraged Western settlement.
  • Pacific Railway Act (1862): Funded the transcontinental railroad.
  • Morrill Act (1862): Created land-grant colleges (the origin of many state universities).
  • NYC Draft Riots (1863): Mostly Irish immigrants attacked Black New Yorkers, blaming them for the war and the draft (which let rich men pay $300 to skip).
  • 54th Massachusetts Regiment: Famous Black regiment showing African American troops served bravely; ~180,000 Black soldiers fought for the Union.

Reconstruction (1865–1877)

How to rebuild the South — and what rights to give freed Black Americans — was the dominant question after the war.

Plans

  • Lincoln's 10% Plan: Lenient — once 10% of a state's voters swore loyalty, it could rejoin. Aimed at quick reconciliation.
  • Andrew Johnson's plan: Even more lenient. Pardoned Confederates and let Southern states pass Black Codes restricting freedmen.
  • Radical Republicans (Thaddeus Stevens, Charles Sumner): Wanted harsh punishment, full civil rights for Black Americans, and a complete remaking of Southern society. They gradually took control of Reconstruction.

Reconstruction Amendments

  • 13th Amendment (1865): Abolished slavery (except as punishment for a crime).
  • 14th Amendment (1868): Granted citizenship to all born in the U.S., guaranteed equal protection and due process. Hugely important — basis for many later civil rights cases.
  • 15th Amendment (1870): Banned voting discrimination based on race (Black men only — women still couldn't vote).

Key Acts & Events

  • Freedmen's Bureau: Federal agency providing food, schools, and legal help to former slaves.
  • Civil Rights Act of 1866: First federal civil rights law.
  • Reconstruction Acts (1867): Divided the South into 5 military districts; required new state constitutions and Black male suffrage to rejoin.
  • Johnson Impeached: First president impeached, for violating the Tenure of Office Act. Acquitted by one vote.

End of Reconstruction

  • Ku Klux Klan used violence and terror to suppress Black voters and white Republicans.
  • Black Codes restricted freedmen's rights to work, travel, and own property.
  • Sharecropping trapped freed Black families in cycles of debt — slavery in all but name.
  • Compromise of 1877: The disputed 1876 election was resolved by giving Republican Hayes the presidency in exchange for removing federal troops from the South. This effectively ended Reconstruction. The South soon erected the Jim Crow system that lasted into the 1960s.

Period 6 · 1865–1898

Unit 6: Industrialization & The Gilded Age

Industrialization, urbanization, and the Gilded Age.

Official CED Topics — Info & Background

Exam weight: 10–17% ★ · ~18 class periods
6.2Westward Expansion: Economic Development1865–1898

The Transcontinental Railroad (1869), completed at Promontory Point, tied the country together. The Homestead Act (1862) drew settlers with 160-acre land grants. Mining booms (gold, silver, copper) and cattle drives created new economies. The closing of the frontier in 1890 (Turner's Frontier Thesis, 1893) was a major cultural milestone.

6.3Westward Expansion: Social and Cultural Development1865–1898

Native Americans lost most of their land. Major events: Battle of Little Bighorn (1876) — Sioux victory; Wounded Knee Massacre (1890) — effective end of Native armed resistance. The Dawes Act (1887) broke tribal land into individual allotments to force assimilation, costing tribes ~2/3 of remaining land.

6.4The "New South"1865–1898

Boosters like Henry Grady promoted Southern industrialization, but the "New South" largely failed. Cotton remained dominant, sharecropping continued, and the South stayed poor and agricultural. Jim Crow segregation laws hardened after Plessy v. Ferguson (1896). Lynching rose; Ida B. Wells became a key activist against it.

6.5Technological Innovation1870s–1890s

A wave of inventions reshaped daily life: Edison's electric light (1879), the telephone (Bell, 1876), the typewriter, the safety bicycle, and the Bessemer process for steel. These tools fueled urbanization and the rise of corporate America.

6.6Rise of Industrial Capitalism1870s–1890s

"Robber Barons" or "Captains of Industry" built massive corporations: Carnegie (steel — vertical integration), Rockefeller (oil — horizontal integration via the trust), Vanderbilt (railroads), J.P. Morgan (finance). Social Darwinism justified inequality as "survival of the fittest." Carnegie's "Gospel of Wealth" argued the rich must give back through philanthropy.

6.7Labor in the Gilded Age1870s–1890s

Workers faced low wages, dangerous conditions, and 12-hour days. Unions formed: the Knights of Labor (open to all) and the AFL (Samuel Gompers — skilled workers only). Major strikes were almost always crushed by federal/state troops: Great Railroad Strike (1877), Haymarket (1886), Homestead (1892), Pullman (1894).

6.8Immigration and Migration in the Gilded Age1880s–1898

"New immigrants" came from Southern and Eastern Europe (Italians, Poles, Russian Jews) — Catholic, Jewish, poorer, and not English speakers. Processed through Ellis Island (NYC) for Europeans and Angel Island (SF) for Asians under harsher rules. Internal migration pushed African Americans northward and rural Americans into cities.

6.9Responses to Immigration in the Gilded Age1880s–1898

Nativism rose against the new immigrants. The Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) was the first U.S. law banning a specific nationality. Conversely, settlement houses like Hull House (Jane Addams, 1889) provided immigrants with services. Political machines like Tammany Hall (Boss Tweed) traded jobs for votes.

6.10Development of the Middle Class1870s–1890s

A new middle class of managers, clerks, and professionals emerged. Department stores (Macy's), mail-order catalogs (Sears), and consumer goods became central. New leisure activities (baseball, vaudeville, amusement parks) and suburbs took shape.

6.11Reform in the Gilded Age1870s–1890s

Reformers tried to address Gilded Age problems. The Social Gospel (Walter Rauschenbusch) urged Christians to fight poverty. The Women's Christian Temperance Union (WCTU) pushed alcohol bans and women's rights. Civil service reform passed via the Pendleton Act (1883).

6.12Controversies over Role of Government1870s–1898

Laissez-faire capitalism dominated, but calls grew for regulation. The Interstate Commerce Act (1887) tried to regulate railroads; the Sherman Antitrust Act (1890) attempted to break monopolies (but was weakly enforced and often used against unions instead).

6.13Politics in the Gilded Age1870s–1898

Era of weak presidents, close elections, and high voter turnout. Big issues were tariffs and currency (gold vs. silver). The Populist Party (1892) demanded free silver, income tax, and direct election of senators. William Jennings Bryan's "Cross of Gold" speech (1896) rallied populists to the Democrats — but he lost to McKinley, ending the populist movement.

Big Themes

  • Immigration and urbanization transformed American society.

Industrialization

By 1890 the U.S. had become the world's largest industrial economy. Steel, oil, and railroads drove the boom.

Captains of Industry / Robber Barons

The same people were called both, depending on who you asked.

  • Andrew Carnegie — Steel. Used vertical integration (controlled every stage from mines to mills). Wrote the "Gospel of Wealth" arguing the rich had a duty to give back through philanthropy. Donated to libraries and schools.
  • John D. Rockefeller — Oil. Built Standard Oil using horizontal integration (buying out or destroying all competitors). At its peak, Standard Oil controlled 90% of U.S. refining. Pioneered the trust as a corporate structure.
  • Cornelius Vanderbilt — Railroads.
  • J.P. Morgan — Finance and banking. Created U.S. Steel (the country's first billion-dollar corporation) by buying out Carnegie.
  • Sherman Antitrust Act (1890): First federal law trying to break up monopolies — but it was vague and weakly enforced for years (often ironically used against unions instead).
  • Social Darwinism: Spencer's idea that "survival of the fittest" applied to society. Used to justify why the rich deserved their wealth and the poor deserved their poverty — and to oppose any government help.
  • Laissez-faire economics dominated the era — government should leave business alone.

Labor

Industrial workers faced low wages, dangerous conditions, 12-hour days, and child labor. Unions organized — and were often crushed.

  • Knights of Labor: Open to all workers regardless of race or skill. Big in the early 1880s, declined sharply after Haymarket.
  • American Federation of Labor (AFL) under Samuel Gompers: Skilled white male workers only; focused on bread-and-butter issues — wages, hours, working conditions. More successful long-term.
Great Railroad Strike 1877

First major nationwide strike. President Hayes sent federal troops to break it — set the precedent of government siding with business.

Haymarket Affair 1886

Labor rally in Chicago. Someone threw a bomb killing police; police fired into the crowd. Anarchists were blamed. Killed public sympathy for labor and ended the Knights of Labor.

Homestead Strike 1892

Workers at Carnegie's steel plant struck. Henry Frick hired Pinkerton guards; battle killed multiple people. State militia broke the strike — devastating for steelworkers.

Pullman Strike 1894

Workers at Pullman's railcar factory struck after wage cuts. Eugene Debs's American Railway Union supported them. President Cleveland sent federal troops, citing the need to keep mail moving. Debs went to jail; later founded the Socialist Party.

Immigration & Urbanization

  • "New Immigrants" (1880s onward) came mostly from Southern and Eastern Europe (Italians, Poles, Russian Jews) — Catholic and Jewish, poorer, less English. Replaced earlier waves from Northern/Western Europe.
  • Ellis Island (NYC) processed European immigrants; Angel Island (San Francisco) processed Asian immigrants under much tougher rules.
  • Chinese Exclusion Act (1882): First U.S. law banning a specific nationality. Showed nativist anti-Asian racism.
  • Cities exploded with tenement housing, slums, and ethnic neighborhoods.
  • Political machines like Tammany Hall (run by Boss Tweed in NYC) traded jobs and services to immigrants for votes — corrupt, but the only safety net immigrants had.
  • Settlement Houses like Hull House (founded by Jane Addams in Chicago) provided immigrants with English classes, daycare, and social services — model for later social work.

The West

  • Transcontinental Railroad (1869): Joined at Promontory Point, Utah. Built largely by Chinese labor (from the West) and Irish labor (from the East). Tied the country together economically and culturally.
  • Homestead Act drew settlers; cattle drives and mining booms created new economies.
Battle of Little Bighorn 1876

Sioux and Cheyenne warriors led by Sitting Bull and Crazy Horse wiped out General Custer's force. A Native victory — but the U.S. responded with even harsher campaigns.

Wounded Knee Massacre 1890

U.S. cavalry killed about 300 Lakota — many women and children — in South Dakota. Effectively ended Native armed resistance.

Dawes Act 1887

Broke up tribal land into individual 160-acre allotments. The goal was to force Native assimilation by making them small farmers. The result: tribes lost about 2/3 of their land within decades. Devastating for Native culture.

Frederick Jackson Turner's Frontier Thesis 1893

Argued the frontier shaped American character (rugged individualism, democracy) and that with the frontier now "closed," the U.S. would need new outlets — foreshadowing imperialism.

Gilded Age Politics

  • Era of weak presidents and very close elections. Voter turnout was high (often 80%+) because parties were like cultural identities.
  • Patronage dominated — politicians rewarded loyalists with jobs.
  • Pendleton Civil Service Act (1883): After President Garfield was assassinated by a disappointed office-seeker, Congress created a merit-based federal job exam — a reform breakthrough.
  • Tariffs and currency (gold vs. silver) were the era's biggest issues.
  • Plessy v. Ferguson (1896): Supreme Court upheld racial segregation under "separate but equal" — became the legal foundation of Jim Crow for nearly 60 years until Brown v. Board.

Populism

Farmers were getting crushed by deflation, debt, and unfair railroad freight rates. They organized to fight back.

  • Granger Movement → Farmers' Alliances → Populist (People's) Party (1892).
  • Demands: free silver (silver-backed money to cause inflation, easing debt), graduated income tax, direct election of senators, 8-hour workday, government ownership of railroads.
  • "Cross of Gold" speech (1896): Democrat William Jennings Bryan delivered the most famous speech of the era, attacking the gold standard. Democrats absorbed the populists; Bryan lost to William McKinley. The Populist Party died — but many of their ideas (income tax, direct senator elections, 8-hour day) eventually became law.

Unit 7: Progressive Era through WWII

The Progressive Era, World War I, the Great Depression, and World War II.

Official CED Topics — Info & Background

Exam weight: 10–17% ★ · ~21 class periods
7.2Imperialism: Debates1890s–early 1900s

Americans debated whether the U.S. should expand overseas. Expansionists argued for new markets, naval bases (Mahan's Influence of Sea Power), and the "white man's burden." Anti-Imperialists (including Mark Twain and Andrew Carnegie) argued imperialism violated American principles of self-government.

7.3The Spanish–American War1898

After the USS Maine exploded in Havana harbor and yellow journalism (Hearst, Pulitzer) blamed Spain, the U.S. crushed Spain in months. America gained Puerto Rico, Guam, the Philippines, and made Cuba a protectorate (Platt Amendment). The U.S. was now a global empire. The Open Door Policy (1899) demanded equal trade access to China; the Roosevelt Corollary (1904) made the U.S. the police of the Western Hemisphere.

7.4The Progressives1890s–1920

A reform movement using government to fix Gilded Age problems. Muckrakers exposed corruption (Sinclair's The Jungle, Tarbell on Standard Oil, Riis on slums). TR's Square Deal = trust-busting, conservation, consumer protection. Wilson's New Freedom = Federal Reserve Act, Clayton Antitrust, Underwood Tariff. Amendments: 16th (income tax), 17th (direct senators), 18th (Prohibition), 19th (women's suffrage, 1920).

7.5World War I: Military & Diplomacy1914–1918

U.S. entered in 1917 after German unrestricted submarine warfare (the Lusitania) and the Zimmermann Telegram. Wilson's 14 Points proposed an idealistic peace plan including the League of Nations. The Senate (led by Henry Cabot Lodge) rejected the Treaty of Versailles, fearing the League would commit U.S. troops to foreign wars.

7.6World War I: Home Front1917–1918

The Espionage Act (1917) and Sedition Act (1918) outlawed criticism of the war. Schenck v. U.S. (1919) upheld limits on speech that posed a "clear and present danger." The Great Migration moved hundreds of thousands of African Americans from the rural South to Northern cities. Women joined the workforce, helping push 19th Amendment passage.

7.71920s: Innovations in Communication & Technology1920s

A consumer culture exploded. Henry Ford's assembly line made the Model T affordable. Radio reached most households; movies became dominant entertainment. Mass advertising and consumer credit reshaped the economy.

7.81920s: Cultural & Political Controversies1920s

A clash between modern and traditional. The Red Scare (Palmer Raids), Sacco and Vanzetti, and the Immigration Act of 1924 (national-origin quotas) showed nativist fears. Prohibition (1920) fueled organized crime; the Scopes Trial (1925) pitted religion against evolution; the KKK revived. The Harlem Renaissance (Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston) and the Lost Generation (Hemingway, Fitzgerald) flourished.

7.9The Great Depression1929–1939

Black Tuesday (Oct 29, 1929) crashed the stock market. By 1933, unemployment hit 25%. Causes: speculation, overproduction, bank weakness, and the high Smoot-Hawley Tariff (1930). Hoover's faith in "rugged individualism" failed; "Hoovervilles" sprang up; the Bonus Army was driven out by federal troops in 1932.

7.10The New Deal1933–1938

FDR's "Three R's: Relief, Recovery, Reform." First New Deal (1933–34): CCC, AAA, NRA, TVA, FDIC, SEC. Second New Deal (1935–38): WPA, Social Security Act (1935), Wagner Act. The court-packing plan (1937) failed. Critics included Huey Long (left) and the Liberty League (right). The New Deal didn't end the Depression but permanently expanded the federal government's role.

7.11Interwar Foreign Policy1919–1941

After WWI rejection of the League, Americans turned isolationist. The Neutrality Acts (mid-1930s) banned arms sales to warring nations. As WWII began, FDR found ways to help — Lend-Lease (1941) sent weapons to Britain and the USSR.

7.12World War II: Mobilization1941–1945

Pearl Harbor (Dec 7, 1941) brought the U.S. in. Mobilization ended the Depression. The War Production Board redirected industry; rationing began. Rosie the Riveter symbolized women in factories. Japanese internment (~120,000 Japanese Americans) — upheld in Korematsu v. U.S. (1944) — was a major civil liberties failure.

7.13World War II: Military1941–1945

In Europe: D-Day (June 6, 1944) — Allied invasion of Normandy. In the Pacific: island-hopping campaign. The war ended with the atomic bombs on Hiroshima (Aug 6) and Nagasaki (Aug 9, 1945), developed by the secret Manhattan Project. The Tuskegee Airmen, Navajo Code Talkers, and the Double V Campaign showed minorities' contributions.

7.14Postwar Diplomacy1945

The Yalta Conference (Feb 1945) — FDR, Churchill, Stalin — planned the postwar world. The Potsdam Conference (July 1945) — now Truman, Churchill/Attlee, Stalin — finalized terms. The United Nations was founded in 1945. Soviet broken promises about free elections in Eastern Europe set the stage for the Cold War.

Big Themes

  • The federal government's role grew massively (Progressive reforms, New Deal, war mobilization).
  • The U.S. became a global power through imperialism and two world wars.
  • Conflicts over civil rights, immigration, and economic inequality persisted.

Imperialism (1890s–early 1900s)

  • Drivers: Hunger for new markets after the frontier "closed," racial ideas about the "white man's burden," and Alfred T. Mahan's Influence of Sea Power, which argued empires need strong navies.
Spanish-American War 1898

The U.S. battleship Maine blew up in Havana harbor. Yellow journalism (sensationalist papers run by Hearst and Pulitzer) blamed Spain — "Remember the Maine!" The U.S. crushed Spain in months and gained Puerto Rico, Guam, the Philippines, and made Cuba a protectorate via the Platt Amendment. The U.S. was now an empire.

  • Open Door Policy (1899): Sec. of State John Hay demanded equal U.S. trade access to China.
  • Roosevelt Corollary (1904): Extension of the Monroe Doctrine — the U.S. would act as the "police" of the Western Hemisphere.
  • Panama Canal (1914): TR backed a Panamanian revolt against Colombia to acquire canal rights. Massive engineering project that linked the Atlantic and Pacific.

Progressive Era (1890s–1920)

A reform movement responding to Gilded Age corruption, inequality, and urban poverty. Progressives wanted to use government to fix capitalism's worst problems.

  • Muckrakers: Investigative journalists exposing wrongdoing.
    • Upton Sinclair's The Jungle exposed disgusting Chicago meatpacking conditions → Meat Inspection Act and Pure Food and Drug Act.
    • Ida Tarbell exposed Standard Oil.
    • Jacob Riis's How the Other Half Lives showed tenement poverty.
  • Theodore Roosevelt's Square Deal: three Cs — control of corporations (trust-busting), conservation (created national parks/forests), consumer protection.
  • Taft: Continued trust-busting (more cases than TR), but more conservative; split the GOP.
  • Wilson's New Freedom:
    • Federal Reserve Act (1913): created the central banking system (still used today).
    • Clayton Antitrust Act (1914): strengthened antitrust law and protected unions.
    • Underwood Tariff (1913): lowered tariffs, paired with the new income tax.
  • Progressive Amendments:
    • 16th — federal income tax.
    • 17th — direct election of senators.
    • 18th — Prohibition of alcohol.
    • 19th — women's suffrage (1920) after decades of activism (Susan B. Anthony, Alice Paul, Carrie Chapman Catt).
  • African American responses:
    • Booker T. Washington: Accommodation — Black Americans should focus on economic self-improvement before pushing for full rights ("Atlanta Compromise").
    • W.E.B. Du Bois: Demanded immediate equality and college education for the "Talented Tenth." Co-founded the NAACP (1909).

World War I (1914–1918)

  • U.S. neutral at first; Wilson reelected on slogan "He kept us out of war."
  • Why we entered (1917): Germany's unrestricted submarine warfare (sinking the Lusitania killed 128 Americans), and the Zimmermann Telegram (Germany asked Mexico to attack the U.S.).
  • Wilson's Fourteen Points: idealistic peace plan, including a League of Nations. The Senate (led by Henry Cabot Lodge) rejected the Treaty of Versailles, fearing the League would drag the U.S. into more wars.
  • Espionage Act (1917) and Sedition Act (1918): banned criticism of the government and the war. Schenck v. U.S. (1919) — the Court upheld limits on speech that posed a "clear and present danger."
  • Great Migration: hundreds of thousands of African Americans moved from the rural South to Northern cities for factory jobs — reshaping the demographics of cities like Chicago, Detroit, and NYC.
  • Women joined the workforce, helping push the 19th Amendment over the line.

The 1920s

  • Red Scare: Fear of communism after Russian Revolution; Palmer Raids deported suspected radicals.
  • Sacco and Vanzetti: Italian immigrant anarchists executed despite weak evidence — symbol of nativism.
  • Immigration Act of 1924: set strict national-origin quotas favoring N/W Europeans, almost banning Asians.
  • "Return to Normalcy": Republican presidents Harding, Coolidge, Hoover — pro-business, low taxes, high tariffs.
  • Roaring 20s: consumer culture, the Model T Ford, radio, jazz, flappers (independent young women), Babe Ruth.
  • Harlem Renaissance: explosion of Black art, music, and literature — Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston, Duke Ellington.
  • Lost Generation: writers disillusioned by WWI — Hemingway, Fitzgerald.
  • Scopes "Monkey" Trial (1925): Tennessee teacher tried for teaching evolution — symbolized clash between rural fundamentalist religion and urban modernism.
  • Prohibition (1920–33): backfired massively — fueled organized crime (Al Capone). Repealed by the 21st Amendment.

Great Depression (1929–1939)

  • Causes: stock market speculation, overproduction, bank weakness, weak agriculture, the high Smoot-Hawley Tariff (1930) which strangled global trade.
  • Black Tuesday (Oct 29, 1929): Stock market crashed, wiping out fortunes overnight. By 1933, 25% unemployment.
  • Hoover: believed in rugged individualism and limited federal action. "Hoovervilles" (shantytowns) sprang up. The Bonus Army (1932) — WWI vets demanding early payment — was driven out by federal troops, hurting Hoover's image.
FDR's New Deal

Franklin Delano Roosevelt's response to the Depression. Three goals — the Three R's: Relief, Recovery, Reform.

First New Deal (1933–34):

  • CCC (Civilian Conservation Corps) — jobs for young men in conservation.
  • AAA (Agricultural Adjustment Act) — paid farmers to grow less, raising prices.
  • NRA (National Recovery Administration) — set wage and price codes; struck down by Supreme Court in 1935.
  • TVA (Tennessee Valley Authority) — built dams, brought electricity to the rural South.
  • FDIC — insured bank deposits (still exists).
  • SEC — regulated stock market (still exists).

Second New Deal (1935–38):

  • WPA (Works Progress Administration) — huge public works program.
  • Social Security Act (1935): pensions for elderly, unemployment insurance — biggest single legacy of the New Deal.
  • Wagner Act (1935): protected workers' right to unionize. Membership exploded.
  • Court-packing plan (1937): FDR tried to add justices to the Supreme Court after it struck down New Deal laws. Failed politically and damaged him.
  • Critics from the left: Huey Long ("Share Our Wealth"), Father Coughlin, Dr. Townsend — said FDR didn't go far enough.
  • Legacy: didn't fully end the Depression (WWII did), but permanently expanded the federal government and birthed the modern welfare state.

World War II (1939–1945)

  • Neutrality Acts (1930s): kept the U.S. out — Americans didn't want another European war.
  • Lend-Lease (1941): FDR found a workaround to send weapons to Britain (and later USSR).
Pearl Harbor December 7, 1941

Japanese surprise attack killed ~2,400 Americans. Brought the U.S. into the war the next day. FDR called it "a date which will live in infamy."

  • Mobilization: the War Production Board redirected industry; rationing; war bonds. Mobilization, not the New Deal, finally ended the Depression.
  • Rosie the Riveter: millions of women filled factory jobs while men were off fighting.
Japanese Internment

About 120,000 Japanese Americans (most of them U.S. citizens) were forced into camps. Korematsu v. U.S. (1944) upheld it. A major civil liberties stain — formally apologized for by Reagan in 1988.

  • Black Americans in the war: the Tuskegee Airmen served as elite pilots; the Double V Campaign demanded victory abroad and at home over racism.
  • Navajo Code Talkers used their language to send unbreakable battlefield messages in the Pacific.
  • D-Day (June 6, 1944): Allied invasion of Normandy. Beginning of the end for Nazi Germany.
  • Atomic bombs: Truman ordered the use of two atomic bombs — Hiroshima (Aug 6) and Nagasaki (Aug 9). Japan surrendered. Built by the secret Manhattan Project. Still controversial — saved U.S. soldiers' lives, but at the cost of ~200,000 civilian deaths.
  • Yalta Conference (Feb 1945): FDR, Churchill, Stalin met to plan post-war world. Stalin's broken promises about free elections in Eastern Europe set the stage for the Cold War.

Big Themes

  • The Cold War shaped both foreign and domestic policy for over four decades.
  • The Civil Rights movement transformed law and society.
  • The 1960s liberal consensus broke down by the 1970s amid Vietnam, stagflation, and Watergate.

Early Cold War (1945–1960)

After WWII, the U.S. and USSR — uneasy allies during the war — became enemies. The "Cold War" was a global ideological standoff between democratic capitalism and Soviet communism.

  • Containment: the U.S. strategy proposed by diplomat George Kennan (in his "Long Telegram") — don't try to defeat communism militarily, just stop it from spreading.
  • Truman Doctrine (1947): U.S. would support free peoples resisting communism; aid to Greece and Turkey.
  • Marshall Plan (1948): $13 billion to rebuild Western Europe, partly to make capitalism more attractive than communism.
  • NATO (1949): military alliance of Western democracies. USSR responded with the Warsaw Pact (1955).
Berlin Airlift 1948–49

Stalin blockaded West Berlin. The U.S. responded by flying in food and supplies for nearly a year. A huge propaganda victory.

  • NSC-68 (1950): top-secret memo calling for a massive U.S. military buildup. Tripled defense spending.
Korean War 1950–53

Communist North Korea (backed by China and USSR) invaded South Korea. UN forces, led by the U.S. under General MacArthur, pushed them back. MacArthur wanted to invade China; Truman fired him for insubordination — defending civilian control of the military. Ended in a stalemate at the 38th parallel, where Korea is still divided today.

Second Red Scare & McCarthyism

Senator Joseph McCarthy claimed the State Department was full of communists, ruining careers without proof. The House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC) investigated Hollywood and government workers. The Hiss case and Rosenberg executions for atomic spying fueled the panic. McCarthy fell after the televised Army-McCarthy hearings (1954) exposed his bullying.

  • Eisenhower (1953–61): "New Look" defense — emphasized nukes (massive retaliation) over conventional troops; ended Korean War; Interstate Highway Act (1956) built 41,000 miles of highways for both commerce and Cold War defense.

1950s Society

  • Suburbs: Levittown-style mass-produced suburbs exploded. The GI Bill gave returning veterans cheap mortgages and college tuition, fueling the middle class.
  • Baby Boom: birth rates skyrocketed; ~76 million babies born between 1946 and 1964.
  • Consumer Culture: TV (now in most homes), cars, fast food, conformity. Critics like William Whyte (The Organization Man) worried about loss of individualism.
  • Counterculture seeds: the Beat Generation (Kerouac's On the Road, Allen Ginsberg) and rock 'n' roll (Elvis) challenged conformity.

Civil Rights Movement

Brown v. Board of Education 1954

NAACP lawyer Thurgood Marshall argued the case. The Court unanimously ruled that "separate is inherently unequal," overturning Plessy v. Ferguson. Schools across the South had to desegregate (though many resisted).

Montgomery Bus Boycott 1955–56

Rosa Parks refused to give up her bus seat → year-long boycott led by a young preacher, Martin Luther King Jr. The boycott succeeded; segregated buses were ruled unconstitutional. MLK's nonviolent leadership made him the movement's face.

Little Rock Nine 1957

Arkansas Governor blocked nine Black students from entering Central High School. Eisenhower sent federal troops to escort them in — a massive moment of federal action against segregation.

  • Civil Rights organizations: SCLC (MLK), SNCC (student-led), CORE.
  • Greensboro Sit-ins (1960): Black college students sat at "whites-only" lunch counters → spread across the South.
  • Freedom Rides (1961): integrated bus trips through the South tested federal desegregation rulings — riders met with horrific violence.
March on Washington August 1963

250,000 people gathered at the Lincoln Memorial. MLK delivered his "I Have a Dream" speech. Pressure helped pass landmark legislation.

  • Civil Rights Act (1964): banned discrimination in public accommodations and employment based on race, religion, sex, national origin.
  • Voting Rights Act (1965): banned literacy tests; sent federal officials to monitor Southern voter registration. Black voter registration soared.
  • Selma to Montgomery March (1965): "Bloody Sunday" violence on national TV pushed the Voting Rights Act over the line.
  • Black Power: by mid-1960s, frustration with the slow pace of change birthed a more radical movement. Malcolm X (Nation of Islam, then later more inclusive Pan-African views), the Black Panthers (community programs + armed self-defense), and Stokely Carmichael's rallying cry.
  • MLK assassinated in Memphis on April 4, 1968 — sparked riots in 100+ cities.

The 1960s — Liberal Reform

  • JFK (1961–63): his "New Frontier" pushed civil rights and the space race. Foreign crises: Bay of Pigs (failed CIA-backed Cuba invasion) and Cuban Missile Crisis (1962) — 13 days when nuclear war seemed imminent. Resolved through quiet U.S. concessions on Turkey missiles. Kennedy assassinated in Dallas, Nov 22, 1963.
  • LBJ's Great Society: domestic agenda the size of the New Deal. War on Poverty, Medicare (health care for elderly) and Medicaid (poor) — both 1965. Head Start, Voting Rights Act, the Immigration Act of 1965 (ended national-origin quotas).
  • Warren Court: liberal Supreme Court under Earl Warren. Major rulings:
    • Miranda v. Arizona (1966) — police must read suspects their rights.
    • Gideon v. Wainwright (1963) — right to a lawyer in criminal cases.
    • Engel v. Vitale (1962) — banned mandatory school prayer.

Vietnam War

  • Why we got involved: the domino theory — fear that if Vietnam fell to communism, all of Southeast Asia would follow.
  • Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (1964): after a disputed naval incident, Congress gave LBJ near-unlimited war powers. Set off massive escalation.
  • Tet Offensive (Jan 1968): coordinated North Vietnamese attacks across the South. Militarily a U.S. victory, but it shattered the official line that we were "winning." Public opinion turned against the war.
  • Anti-war movement: Students for a Democratic Society (SDS), massive campus protests, draft-card burning. Kent State (1970): Ohio National Guard killed 4 student protestors — radicalized opposition further.
  • Pentagon Papers (1971): leaked classified docs revealed the government had repeatedly lied about Vietnam. Eroded public trust.
  • Nixon's Vietnamization: gradually withdraw U.S. troops while training South Vietnam. Paris Peace Accords (1973) ended U.S. involvement; Saigon fell to the North in 1975.
  • War Powers Act (1973): Congress required the president to notify it within 48 hours of military deployment and pull out within 60 days without approval.

Other Movements

  • Women's Liberation: Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique (1963) critiqued the "happy housewife" myth. NOW founded 1966. Title IX (1972) banned sex discrimination in federally funded education (huge for women's sports). Roe v. Wade (1973) legalized abortion. The Equal Rights Amendment failed ratification — Phyllis Schlafly's "Stop ERA" movement led the conservative backlash.
  • Latino Movement: Cesar Chavez and Dolores Huerta led the United Farm Workers and the grape boycott. The Chicano Movement asserted Mexican-American pride.
  • Native Americans: the American Indian Movement (AIM); the Wounded Knee occupation (1973).
  • Environmental: Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962) exposed pesticide dangers. Earth Day (1970), the EPA (1970), and the Clean Air/Water Acts followed.
  • LGBTQ: the Stonewall Riots (1969) in NYC kicked off the modern gay rights movement.
  • Counterculture: hippies, psychedelic drugs, Woodstock (1969), communes — rebelled against mainstream materialism and the Vietnam War.

Nixon Era (1969–1974)

  • "Silent Majority": Nixon appealed to working-class and middle-class whites who were tired of protests.
  • Détente with USSR (relaxed tensions; SALT I treaty); historic opening of China (1972) playing the two communist powers off each other.
  • Stagflation: a strange new economic problem — high inflation and high unemployment together — triggered partly by the 1973 OPEC oil embargo.
Watergate 1972–74

Nixon operatives broke into Democratic headquarters at the Watergate complex. The cover-up — not the break-in — was what brought Nixon down. Washington Post reporters Woodward and Bernstein, with help from "Deep Throat," exposed the scandal. The Supreme Court (U.S. v. Nixon) forced Nixon to release tapes. Facing certain impeachment, Nixon resigned Aug 9, 1974 — the only president to ever do so. Massive blow to public trust in government.

  • Ford pardoned Nixon — controversial, hurt him in the 1976 election.

The Reagan Revolution (1981–1989)

Reagan's election marked a major shift right after decades of liberal dominance. He promised to shrink government, cut taxes, and confront the USSR.

  • "New Right" coalition: three groups linked together — evangelical Christians (the Moral Majority, led by Jerry Falwell) opposing abortion and gay rights, economic conservatives wanting low taxes and deregulation, and Cold War hawks wanting to confront the USSR.
  • Reaganomics / Supply-side / "Trickle-down": the theory that cutting taxes on the wealthy and big business would create investment that would "trickle down" to everyone else. Result: huge tax cuts (1981, 1986), but also massive deficits tripled from defense spending.
  • PATCO Strike (1981): Reagan fired 11,000 striking air traffic controllers. Sent a strong anti-union signal — labor never fully recovered.
  • Cold War buildup: Reagan called the USSR an "Evil Empire" and proposed the Strategic Defense Initiative ("Star Wars") — a missile defense system. Combined with Soviet economic stagnation, this pressured the USSR.
  • Iran-Contra Scandal (1986): Reagan officials secretly sold arms to Iran (to free hostages) and used the money to fund anti-communist Contras in Nicaragua — both illegal. Damaged Reagan's image but he wasn't impeached.
  • Gorbachev's reforms: Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev introduced glasnost (openness) and perestroika (restructuring). Combined with U.S. pressure, this led directly to the end of the Cold War.

End of the Cold War & H.W. Bush (1989–1993)

  • Berlin Wall fell (1989); USSR dissolved (1991). The Cold War ended without a shot.
  • Gulf War (1991): Iraq under Saddam Hussein invaded Kuwait. Bush led a UN coalition that pushed Iraq out in just 100 hours of ground combat. Showed the power of post-Cold-War U.S. military.
  • Americans with Disabilities Act (1990): civil rights protections for disabled Americans.
  • Recession + broken "no new taxes" pledge hurt Bush. He lost the 1992 election to Bill Clinton.

Clinton Era (1993–2001)

  • "New Democrat": Clinton moved his party to the center — pro-business, fiscally moderate.
  • NAFTA (1994): the North American Free Trade Agreement with Canada and Mexico. Boosted trade but also accelerated U.S. manufacturing job losses.
  • Welfare Reform (1996): ended the welfare entitlement, replaced it with time-limited benefits and work requirements.
  • "Don't Ask, Don't Tell": compromise letting gay people serve in the military if they stayed closeted.
  • Tech / dot-com boom drove huge economic growth and budget surpluses by the late 1990s.
  • Impeached (1998): over the Monica Lewinsky scandal. Acquitted by the Senate.
  • "Contract with America" (1994): Newt Gingrich led a Republican wave, taking the House for the first time in 40 years. Government shutdowns followed.

Globalization & Technology

  • Personal computers and the internet reshaped daily life — Microsoft, Apple, Amazon, Google. Smartphones (iPhone, 2007) followed.
  • Outsourcing: manufacturing jobs moved to Mexico, China, and elsewhere. The Rust Belt (Midwest factory towns) declined; service and tech sectors grew.
  • NAFTA, WTO: liberalized trade; controversial among workers losing jobs.
  • Immigration shift: away from Europe, toward Latin America and Asia. Major debates over reform.

9/11 & War on Terror

September 11, 2001

The terrorist group al-Qaeda, led by Osama bin Laden, hijacked four planes. Two struck the World Trade Center in NYC; one hit the Pentagon; one crashed in Pennsylvania. Nearly 3,000 Americans died. Reshaped U.S. foreign and domestic policy for two decades.

  • Patriot Act (2001): dramatically expanded government surveillance powers.
  • Department of Homeland Security: new cabinet department; airport security (TSA) created.
  • War in Afghanistan (2001–21): targeted the Taliban government, which had sheltered al-Qaeda. America's longest war.
  • Iraq War (2003–11): Bush claimed Saddam Hussein had weapons of mass destruction (turned out to be false). Years of insurgency followed. Hugely controversial.
  • Hurricane Katrina (2005): devastated New Orleans. Government response was widely criticized — particularly racial inequities in the aftermath.
  • Bush tax cuts (2001, 2003); No Child Left Behind education reform.

2008 Financial Crisis & Obama (2009–2017)

  • Subprime Mortgage Crisis (2008): banks had given out shaky housing loans, then bundled them into complex securities. When the housing bubble burst, major banks and insurance companies collapsed (Lehman Brothers). Worst economic shock since the Great Depression.
  • TARP and stimulus: bipartisan bailouts ($700B+) saved banks and the auto industry. Saved the economy but were politically toxic.
  • Obama: first Black president. Major accomplishment: the Affordable Care Act ("Obamacare," 2010) — biggest health reform since Medicare; expanded coverage but very polarizing. Bin Laden killed by Navy SEALs in 2011.
  • Tea Party (2009): conservative grassroots backlash to bailouts, ACA, and rising debt. Pushed the GOP further right.
  • Same-sex marriage: Obergefell v. Hodges (2015) legalized it nationwide.
  • Black Lives Matter: emerged after the killing of Trayvon Martin (2013) and police killings in Ferguson (2014) and elsewhere. Renewed focus on racial justice and policing.

Section I, Part B · Free Response

Short Answer Questions (SAQ)

3 questions · 40 minutes total · 20% of exam score

The Format

  • 3 SAQs total on the exam.
  • SAQ 1 & 2 are required: SAQ 1 covers periods 3–8 with a secondary source; SAQ 2 covers periods 3–8 with a primary source.
  • SAQ 3 is a choice between one covering periods 1–5 OR one covering periods 6–9.
  • Each SAQ has 3 parts: A, B, C — each worth 1 point, for 3 points each (9 total).
  • Aim for ~10 minutes per SAQ.

SAQ Scoring (per part)

Part A — typically asks you to describe or identify1 pt
Part B — typically asks you to explain or give a specific example supporting the prompt1 pt
Part C — typically asks you to explain a different example or counter-perspective1 pt

How to Approach Every SAQ — the "ACE" Method

  • A — Answer. Directly answer the question in your first sentence. Don't restate the question; just answer it.
  • C — Cite. Give a specific historical example (a person, event, law, or document with a date).
  • E — Explain. Briefly explain how that example proves your answer.

Each part should be 2–4 sentences. You do NOT need a thesis on SAQs — that's only for DBQ/LEQ.

✓ DO
  • Answer in complete sentences
  • Use specific dates, names, and events
  • Make sure your example actually fits the time period
  • Label your answers A, B, C clearly
  • Stay focused — be concise
✗ DON'T
  • Use bullet points or lists
  • Be vague ("things were bad," "people were upset")
  • Include outside-of-period info
  • Write a 5-paragraph essay (it's just 3 short answers)
  • Forget to actually answer the question — describe ≠ explain

Sample Prompt & Student Response

Prompt: Using your knowledge of U.S. history, answer (a), (b), and (c).

  • Briefly describe ONE specific economic cause of the American Civil War.
  • Briefly explain ONE specific way that the Compromise of 1850 contributed to sectional tensions.
  • Briefly explain ONE specific reason the Republican Party emerged in the 1850s.
Student Response — Earned 3/3

(a) One specific economic cause of the Civil War was the conflict over tariffs. Northern industries supported high protective tariffs to shield U.S. manufacturers from British competition, while the South opposed them because tariffs raised the price of imported goods that Southerners depended on. This economic divide deepened sectional tension long before 1861.

(b) The Compromise of 1850 contributed to sectional tensions because it included the Fugitive Slave Act, which forced Northern citizens to help return escaped enslaved people to the South. This angered abolitionists and many ordinary Northerners who had no desire to be involved in slavery, and it directly inspired Harriet Beecher Stowe's Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852), turning Northern opinion against slavery.

(c) The Republican Party emerged in 1854 in direct response to the Kansas-Nebraska Act, which used popular sovereignty to potentially open new territories to slavery. Anti-slavery Whigs, Free-Soilers, and some Northern Democrats joined together because they wanted a party committed to stopping the expansion of slavery into Western territories.

Why this earned full credit: Each answer (1) directly answers the question, (2) uses specific evidence (Fugitive Slave Act, Uncle Tom's Cabin, Kansas-Nebraska Act, Republican Party formation in 1854), and (3) explains the connection to the larger issue. Notice how short and focused each part is — this is roughly 80 words each, which is plenty.

Common SAQ Question Stems

  • "Briefly describe ONE..." → Define or identify with a specific example.
  • "Briefly explain ONE..." → Identify + give a specific example + explain how/why it works.
  • "Briefly explain how the historian's interpretation..." → Identify the historian's claim, then give specific evidence supporting it.
  • "Briefly explain a different historical interpretation..." → Provide an alternative view AND specific evidence for it.
Section II, Part A · Free Response

Document-Based Question (DBQ)

1 essay · 7 documents · 60 minutes (15 reading + 45 writing) · 25% of exam score

The Format

  • You'll get 7 historical documents (primary sources) about a single topic.
  • The DBQ will focus on a topic from periods 3 through 8.
  • You write one essay using the documents AND outside knowledge to answer the prompt.
  • Worth 7 points total — the most important essay on the exam.

DBQ Scoring Rubric (out of 7)

Thesis / Claim — A defensible thesis that responds to the prompt with a line of reasoning1 pt
Contextualization — Describe a broader historical context (before, during, or after) relevant to the prompt1 pt
Evidence — Documents: Use content from at least 3 docs to address the prompt (1 pt) OR use 6 docs to support the argument (2 pts)2 pts
Evidence Beyond Docs — Use at least 1 specific historical example NOT in the documents to support your argument1 pt
Sourcing — For at least 3 docs, explain HIPP (Historical situation, Intended audience, Purpose, or Point of view) and how it's relevant to the argument1 pt
Complexity — Demonstrate a complex understanding (multiple variables, qualification, connections across periods)1 pt

How to Use Your 60 Minutes

  • 0–15 min — Reading Period: Read the prompt twice. Read every document, jotting notes in the margin: source, main idea, HIPP. Group docs into 2–3 categories. Plan your thesis and outline.
  • 15–25 min — Intro: Write your contextualization paragraph + thesis.
  • 25–55 min — Body Paragraphs: 2–3 body paragraphs, each focused on a category. Use docs + outside evidence + sourcing in each.
  • 55–60 min — Conclusion + Review: Restate thesis with a complexity twist. Quickly check that you used 6+ docs and sourced 3.

The HIPP Method (for Sourcing Points)

For at least 3 docs, you must explain something beyond just what the doc says. Pick one of:

  • H — Historical Situation: What was happening when this was written? ("This was published in 1854, during the height of sectional tension over Kansas-Nebraska...")
  • I — Intended Audience: Who was it for? ("Aimed at Southern slaveholders to convince them...")
  • P — Purpose: Why was it written? ("Designed to rally abolitionist support...")
  • P — Point of View: Whose perspective is this? ("As an enslaved person, Douglass had personal experience that gave his account...")

The trick: You must explicitly connect the HIPP to the argument — saying "the author was a Northern abolitionist" alone earns nothing. You need to add "...which is why he focused on the brutal aspects of slavery rather than its economics."

Sample DBQ Prompt

Prompt: Evaluate the extent to which the Civil War was a turning point in the lives of African Americans in the United States from 1861 to 1880.

(Imagine 7 documents: Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation; testimony of a freed slave; Black Codes from Mississippi (1865); a photograph of the Freedmen's Bureau; the 14th Amendment; an 1875 KKK threat letter; a sharecropping contract.)

Sample Student-Written DBQ — Earned 7/7

Introduction (Contextualization + Thesis)

By the start of the Civil War in 1861, slavery had been the foundation of the Southern economy for over two centuries, and four million enslaved African Americans had no legal rights, no citizenship, and no protection from the federal government. Free Black communities in the North faced widespread discrimination, and the Supreme Court's 1857 Dred Scott decision had even ruled that Black Americans were not citizens. While the Civil War and Reconstruction did transform the legal status of African Americans through the 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments, by 1880 the gap between legal freedom and real freedom was massive. Therefore, the Civil War was a turning point in the legal and political status of African Americans, but it was a far more limited turning point in their economic and social lives, since white Southerners used violence, sharecropping, and Black Codes to recreate a system of racial subordination that closely resembled slavery.

Why this earns the thesis + contextualization points: The first 4 sentences set up the historical context (slavery, Dred Scott, the war's start). The thesis is defensible — it gives a clear position with reasoning ("turning point legally and politically, but not economically and socially") rather than just restating the prompt.
Body Paragraph 1 — Legal/Political Turning Point

The Civil War unquestionably transformed the legal status of African Americans. Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation (Doc 1) declared all enslaved people in Confederate states free as of January 1, 1863. While this didn't free anyone in border states, it reframed the war as a fight against slavery and prevented Britain or France from supporting the Confederacy. After the war, the Reconstruction Amendments (Doc 5) made this transformation permanent: the 13th Amendment abolished slavery, the 14th guaranteed citizenship and equal protection, and the 15th protected Black men's right to vote. Lincoln's Proclamation must also be read in the context of military necessity — it was issued only after the Union victory at Antietam (Sept 1862) gave Lincoln the political opportunity, suggesting that emancipation was both a moral and strategic decision. Outside the documents, the Freedmen's Bureau, established in 1865, helped over 4 million freedpeople by setting up schools, providing food and shelter, and negotiating labor contracts. By 1870, more than 2,000 African Americans held political office across the South, including senators Hiram Revels and Blanche Bruce of Mississippi.

What this paragraph earns: Cites 2 documents (Doc 1, Doc 5). Uses outside evidence (Freedmen's Bureau, Revels, Bruce). Sources Doc 1 by explaining the historical situation (Antietam victory) and how that connects to Lincoln's strategic motives. ✓ 2 docs used + 1 outside + 1 sourcing.
Body Paragraph 2 — Limited Economic and Social Change

However, the legal transformation hid a much more limited economic shift. Mississippi's Black Codes (Doc 3), passed almost immediately after the war, required freedpeople to sign yearly labor contracts and prevented them from owning land in many areas — recreating the conditions of slavery in everything but name. The sharecropping contract (Doc 7) shows how this played out economically: a freedman would work the land in exchange for a portion of the crop, but landowners controlled the seeds, tools, and store credit, locking many Black families into cycles of debt that lasted generations. Because this contract was written by a white landowner, its lopsided terms reflect the fact that freedpeople had little bargaining power and few alternatives — even legal freedom didn't translate into economic freedom. Outside the documents, the famous "40 acres and a mule" promise — Sherman's Field Order 15, which redistributed Confederate land to former slaves — was reversed by President Andrew Johnson in 1865, returning the land to its former owners and ensuring that the South's plantation economy would rebuild itself on Black labor.

What this paragraph earns: Uses 2 more documents (Doc 3, Doc 7) — running total now 4. Sources Doc 7 (point of view of the white landowner) and connects it to the argument. Uses outside evidence (Field Order 15, Johnson's reversal).
Body Paragraph 3 — Violent Backlash

Worse, white Southerners actively used violence to undermine the war's legal gains. The 1875 KKK threat letter (Doc 6) shows how white supremacist terrorism targeted Black voters, teachers, and political leaders. The intended audience of letters like this — Black families and Republican officials — reveals that the goal was not just intimidation but to dismantle the political gains of Reconstruction by making participation deadly. The freed slave's testimony (Doc 2) describes the sense of joy and possibility immediately after emancipation, which makes the contrast with the post-1877 reality even sharper. After the Compromise of 1877, federal troops left the South, and within two decades Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) made segregation the law of the land — clear evidence that the war's social transformation was rolled back.

What this paragraph earns: Uses Docs 2 and 6 (running total = 6 — full evidence point). Sources Doc 6 (intended audience). Uses outside evidence (Compromise of 1877, Plessy v. Ferguson).
Conclusion (Complexity)

The Civil War was therefore a contradictory turning point: it ended one legal system of racial control while allowing another, less formal one to take its place. Looking forward, the 14th Amendment's equal protection clause would become the legal foundation for the Civil Rights Movement nearly a century later, in cases like Brown v. Board of Education (1954). In that sense, even though Reconstruction failed in the short term, its constitutional reforms planted the seeds of a more lasting transformation — one that ordinary Black Americans had to fight to claim for another hundred years. The war's true legacy was not what it accomplished in 1865, but what it made possible after 1954.

Why this earns the complexity point: The conclusion makes a sophisticated connection across periods (linking Reconstruction's amendments to the 20th-century Civil Rights Movement and Brown v. Board). It also qualifies the thesis ("contradictory turning point"). Complexity is one of the hardest points to earn — connecting your topic to a different time period or making a nuanced "to a certain extent" argument is the most reliable way.

Top DBQ Tips

✓ DO
  • Use 6+ documents (you only need 3 for 1 pt, but 6 = 2 pts)
  • Cite documents in parentheses: (Doc 3) — but DON'T just quote; analyze
  • Group docs into 2–3 categories to organize body paragraphs
  • HIPP at least 4 docs to be safe (only need 3, but more = insurance)
  • Make sure your thesis takes a CLEAR position
  • Add "even though" or "to a certain extent" language for complexity
✗ DON'T
  • Just summarize each document one by one
  • Forget outside evidence — it's a separate point
  • Write "the author is a Northerner" without explaining HOW that affects the doc
  • Use a thesis like "There were many causes" — too vague
  • Spend too much time on one doc — distribute usage
  • Run out of time on the conclusion (skip it before skipping body paragraphs)
Section II, Part B · Free Response

Long Essay Question (LEQ)

1 essay (your choice of 3 prompts) · 40 minutes · 15% of exam score

The Format

  • You'll get 3 LEQ prompts — pick ONE.
  • Option 1: Periods 1–3. Option 2: Periods 4–6. Option 3: Periods 7–9.
  • Pick the one you know the most specific evidence for.
  • You write one essay (no documents — pure outside knowledge).
  • Worth 6 points total.

LEQ Scoring Rubric (out of 6)

Thesis / Claim — A defensible thesis that responds to the prompt with a line of reasoning1 pt
Contextualization — Describe a broader historical context relevant to the prompt1 pt
Evidence — Use specific historical examples to support the argument (2 pts) — 1 pt for providing 2 specific examples, 2 pts for using them effectively to support the thesis2 pts
Analysis & Reasoning — Use a historical reasoning process (causation, comparison, continuity/change) to frame the argument1 pt
Complexity — Demonstrate a complex understanding1 pt

The 3 Reasoning Skills LEQs Ask About

  • Causation: "Evaluate the extent to which X caused Y." — Discuss multiple causes/effects, weigh which were most important.
  • Comparison: "Compare and contrast X and Y." — Discuss similarities AND differences, with specific examples.
  • Continuity & Change Over Time (CCOT): "Evaluate the extent to which X changed/stayed the same from year A to year B." — Identify what changed AND what stayed the same.

How to Use Your 40 Minutes

  • 0–5 min — Plan: Pick the prompt you have the most evidence for. Outline 2–3 specific examples per body paragraph.
  • 5–10 min — Intro: Contextualization (3–4 sentences) + thesis (1–2 sentences).
  • 10–32 min — Body: 2–3 body paragraphs with specific evidence and explanation.
  • 32–38 min — Conclusion: Restate thesis with complexity (qualification, comparison to a different period, etc.).
  • 38–40 min — Review.

Sample LEQ Prompt

Prompt: Evaluate the extent to which the New Deal changed the relationship between the federal government and the American people from 1933 to 1945.

Sample Student-Written LEQ — Earned 6/6

Introduction (Contextualization + Thesis)

Before 1929, the federal government played a small role in most Americans' daily lives. The Republican administrations of Harding, Coolidge, and Hoover believed in laissez-faire economics and "rugged individualism," meaning the government should stay out of the economy and let the free market handle problems. When the stock market crashed in October 1929 and the Great Depression began, Hoover's faith in volunteer charity and limited government failed to address the crisis, and unemployment reached 25% by 1933. Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal, beginning with his inauguration in March 1933, fundamentally transformed this relationship. The New Deal greatly expanded the role of the federal government in American economic and social life, providing direct assistance, employment, and long-term security to ordinary Americans for the first time. While critics from both the left and the right opposed it, and while many programs were temporary, the New Deal permanently established the principle that the federal government was responsible for managing the economy and protecting the welfare of citizens — a transformation extended further by World War II.

What this earns: Contextualization point (sets up Hoover, the crash, pre-New Deal philosophy). Thesis point (clear, defensible position with reasoning: "transformed permanently, even though temporary programs and critics existed").
Body Paragraph 1 — Direct Federal Help to Citizens

The First New Deal (1933–34) created a series of agencies designed to provide direct relief to struggling Americans. The Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC) employed over 3 million young men in environmental projects like building trails, planting trees, and fighting forest fires — sending most of their wages directly home to support their families. The Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA) brought electricity to rural Southern communities for the first time, while the Public Works Administration (PWA) built schools, hospitals, and bridges nationwide. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC), still in existence today, insured bank deposits up to a set amount, ending the bank runs that had wiped out countless savings accounts. Each of these programs represented a fundamental change: the federal government was now directly employing, paying, insuring, and electrifying the lives of ordinary citizens — something it had not done before.

What this earns: Multiple specific examples (CCC, TVA, PWA, FDIC). Each is connected to the thesis (showing direct federal help). Strong evidence work — way more than the minimum 2 specific pieces of evidence.
Body Paragraph 2 — The Permanent Welfare State

The Second New Deal (1935–38) created programs designed to be permanent — and they were. The Social Security Act of 1935 established old-age pensions, unemployment insurance, and aid to dependent children, fundamentally changing what Americans could expect from their government. For the first time, the federal government took responsibility for the basic economic security of the elderly, the disabled, and the unemployed — a system that still exists today, nearly 90 years later, and that millions of Americans still depend on. The Wagner Act (1935) gave workers the legal right to organize unions and bargain collectively, leading union membership to nearly triple by 1945. The Works Progress Administration (WPA) eventually employed over 8.5 million Americans on roads, bridges, post offices, and even arts projects. These programs reframed the federal government's job: it was no longer just to defend the country and collect taxes, but to actively protect citizens from economic harm.

What this earns: More specific examples (Social Security Act, Wagner Act, WPA). Strong analytical reasoning — explicit comparison of "before" vs. "after" the New Deal showing change over time.
Body Paragraph 3 — Limits of Change

However, the New Deal's transformation was not unlimited. African Americans were largely excluded from many programs, especially Social Security, which initially did not cover domestic workers and farm laborers — jobs disproportionately held by Black workers. Critics like Senator Huey Long argued the New Deal was too cautious and proposed his more radical "Share Our Wealth" plan, while conservatives like the American Liberty League denounced it as socialism. The Supreme Court struck down the National Recovery Administration (NRA) in 1935 (Schechter v. U.S.) and the Agricultural Adjustment Act (AAA) in 1936, slowing reform. FDR's 1937 "court-packing" plan to add justices failed politically and damaged his standing. World War II, however, finished what the New Deal began: massive defense spending and military mobilization permanently expanded the federal government's size, taxing power, and economic role, finally ending the Depression and cementing the larger federal role the New Deal had begun.

What this earns the analysis & reasoning point: The essay uses causation reasoning consistently — showing how the Depression caused the New Deal, which caused a permanent shift in federal-citizen relations. This paragraph also qualifies the argument by acknowledging limits, which is essential for the complexity point.
Conclusion (Complexity)

By 1945, the relationship between the federal government and the American people had been permanently transformed. Although the New Deal did not end the Depression on its own — World War II ultimately did — it changed the very expectations Americans had of their government. Programs like Social Security and the FDIC continue today; bigger ideas like federal responsibility for the unemployed and elderly continue to shape American politics, including 21st-century debates over the Affordable Care Act and government stimulus during the 2008 and 2020 crises. The New Deal therefore was not just a Depression-era response but the foundation of the modern American state — a transformation as fundamental as Reconstruction in the 1860s or the Progressive Era of the early 1900s.

Why this earns the complexity point: The conclusion connects the New Deal to (a) WWII, (b) the 2008 and 2020 stimulus packages, and (c) Reconstruction and the Progressive Era. Connecting your topic to other historical periods is the most reliable way to earn complexity. Other ways: making a "to a certain extent" argument, addressing counter-arguments, using multiple variables.

Top LEQ Tips

✓ DO
  • Pick the prompt you know the MOST specific examples for
  • Use 4–6+ specific historical examples (names, dates, laws)
  • Write a clear thesis with a "because" or "even though" structure
  • Make connections to OTHER time periods in your conclusion
  • Use the right reasoning skill (causation, comparison, CCOT)
✗ DON'T
  • Be vague — "many people were upset" earns nothing
  • Skip contextualization — it's a free point
  • Write only one body paragraph — aim for 2–3
  • Just list events; you need analysis showing WHY they matter
  • Forget the prompt halfway through — refer back to it

Universal Thesis Templates That Work

Use one of these as a starting point — then plug in your specifics:

  • Causation: "While [other cause] played a role, [main cause] was the most significant factor in [event] because [reason 1] and [reason 2]."
  • Comparison: "Although [X] and [Y] both [similarity], they differed significantly in [difference 1] and [difference 2], because [reasoning]."
  • CCOT: "From [year] to [year], [topic] changed significantly in [way 1] and [way 2], although [continuity] remained largely consistent."
